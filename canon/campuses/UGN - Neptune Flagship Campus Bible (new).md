# University of Greater Neptune — Neptune Flagship Campus Bible (new)

*The Ways, the Loft, the working waterfront, and a university built on the bones of a shipyard*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

**Status:** Shared public-register worldbuilding. New site-level details in this Bible are adopted for the Neptune Continuum; existing regional and UGN facts are preserved. No new deep-register rules.

**Authority:** *The Greater Neptune Region — Master Reference v3*; *The University of Greater Neptune*; *Neptune and Cadence*; *The Twentieth Century*; *Neptune English*. The Road Atlas controls mileage and travel time. For Mirth-specific character details, *The Tithe*'s Master Archive controls.

## 1. The Campus in One Glance

**Formal identity.** The University of Greater Neptune's flagship is its **city campus in Neptune**, called **UGN** or **the U**. It occupies the former principal shipways of the Neptune Bay Shipbuilding Corporation on the north bank, in the **Ways** neighborhood of **Ward 2 · North Bank**. A person says "I go to the U" and, when giving directions, "Meet me at the Ways." The university's name belongs to the whole system; people in Neptune often forget to specify that this is one campus among four and a field station.

**The numbers.** Approximately **17,800 students**, mostly from Greater Neptune, including first-generation undergraduates, transfer students, veterans, commuters, working adults and graduate researchers. The **Ospreys** play Division I basketball. Football has a loyal following and a reliably frustrating record. The campus serves as the seat of the system formed in **1979**; the site's college history begins in **1946**.

**The two accurate descriptions.** The university's brochure says students learn where industry meets ocean, river, forest and public service. An older resident of the Rivers might answer: "My father welded on that ground. They kept the building and forgot his name." Both claims shape the place. A student can have a wonderful education here while the institution owes its neighbors a fuller account.

**Visible shorthand.** Terraced green slipways descending toward the seawall; a navy-painted whirley crane; the broad shell of the mold loft; wet backpacks on hooks; a train horn from farther along the north bank. The working **Yard Reach** shops and **North Terminal** remain separate from the university. Nothing about the campus resurrects the wartime shipyard.

## 2. The Ground Before the School

**Before the university.** This coast remains **Siuslaw country**, as in the shared Master Reference. The twentieth-century industrial history starts with the Bay Yard's wartime construction. In **March 1942** its builders laid the first keel. It produced **141 Liberty ships** and **38 Victory ships** before its 1946 closure and employed around **22,000** people at its peak. Recruiters brought workers from Shreveport, Houston, Beaumont and Pine Bluff. Many Black workers and their families established the neighborhood that became **the Rivers** on the opposite side of the city.

**The inequity written into the worksite.** In **1943**, eighty-four Black yard workers were fired for refusing the Boilermakers' segregated auxiliary; most were reinstated after federal complaints. The university has shipbuilding plaques, but the Rivers has lists of workers, addresses, union grievances and missing names that its plaques did not preserve. The yard's last ship launched in fall **1945**; the facility closed in **1946**.

**College genealogy.** The **Neptune Extension Center** opened in the empty Bay Yard administration building and mold loft in summer **1946**, offering two years of college to about **300 veterans** initially, nearly **1,500** by the following fall. It became **Neptune State College (1955)**, **Neptune State University (1968)**, and the flagship of **UGN (1979)**. The 1979 system joined Neptune with the Cadence and Middle Fork colleges; Amity Gate opened in 1981. The university received its own board of trustees in **2015**.

**The day the institution changed.** Black students from the Rivers occupied the president's office for two days in spring **1968**. They won a **Black Studies department**, an admissions pathway from Rivers high schools, and a promise the university would never build on land taken from the Rivers. Black Studies opened in **1969**. The land promise still holds. Some records of the occupation are in the campus library; the memories of students and families who participated remain with the people who lived them.

**How the campus remembers.** The restored yard administration building carries institutional dates in polished lettering. The **1968 occupation** is marked within the building where it occurred, near a passage regularly used for mundane administrative errands. The notice includes the three commitments in plain words. It remains possible for a student to walk past it for four years without reading it. A complementary public archive display lists the 1943 auxiliary firings without claiming the list is complete. This memorial interpretation is new campus detail; the historical events and outcomes are already established.

## 3. The Campus Underfoot

**Orientation.** The Ways descend toward the Neptune River's seawall in a sequence of graded former launch slopes. From the upper arrival edge, the old administration building and newer teaching halls face a campus green whose shape is unmistakably industrial. The **Loft** lies at the social center of the converted yard. The **navy whirley crane** stands near the water at the foot of the Ways. A route along the outer edge of campus approaches the **fitting-out pier gym**, while working repair and cargo facilities continue farther along the bank, outside campus control.

**The main student walk.** A reliable route starts at the **North Bank bus stop**, passes a row of rented rooms and the upper teaching frontage, crosses the former administration building's steps, enters the **Loft**, descends the grass of the **Ways**, reaches the old crane and waterfront walk, and ends at the **pier gym**. From some points students can see the working repair yard beyond campus; from other points the nearest water is hidden by buildings until the ground tips down.

**Edges.** The Ways neighborhood has students, long-term renters and working households. **Yard Reach** is a working fabrication and repair district, not a university makerspace. **North Terminal** belongs to port commerce. The public walkway ends where fenced industrial operations begin. The riverwall has storm gates and posted restrictions on exposed sections during high-water or severe-wind conditions. The campus does not own the seaward bar, the Choke, or the port's right to operate.

**Access.** Most students arrive by bus, carpool, bicycle or on foot from nearby rentals; commuters come from Dune Side, the Rivers and towns elsewhere in the region. Bicycle racks near the Loft are full in dry spells. The route down the old slopes has accessible graded paths alongside the stepped landscape; those paths are longer than the direct desire lines across the grass. Night lighting follows the inhabited paths, leaving some industrial silhouettes dark. Schedules for long-distance trips are determined by the regional roads, weather and the Road Atlas, never by a convenient invented shortcut.

## 4. Buildings That Have a Life

**The former administration building.** The oldest campus office core was first built for the Bay Yard and became the Extension Center's first college home. Its wide entrance stairs remain, while an accessible side approach and interior lift bring students to registration, academic services and administrative offices. The corridors are stubbornly narrow for a building that now handles enrollment lines. On the first rainy week of term, the stair rails carry drying scarves and everyone's shoeprints show on the linoleum.

**The Loft.** A mold loft is where shipbuilders once chalked vessel parts full scale. Today its great floor holds the **student union**, with a corner of original **chalk lines preserved beneath glass**. Students eat, study, complain, join clubs, queue for coffee and try to avoid someone they dated last winter. Along one wall are day-use lockers, a campus food-pantry pickup room and an overburdened noticeboard advertising roommate openings, paid crewing shifts, childcare swaps and bar-closure alerts. The building can be loud while the wet wind is still audible at the doors.

**The Library of the Ways.** A plain, mid-century library building faces the former yard administration building, keeping its quieter reading rooms on an upper level away from the primary walk. It holds campus archives, local newspapers, union records and the records the university has legal custody to retain; it does **not** claim ownership of restricted Nation-held materials. One reading room is informally called **the Chart Room** because its wall cases contain navigation charts and shipyard plans. Nursing students occupy its window tables after evening clinical shifts. This library and room name are new campus-level developments.

**The teaching waterfront.** The **School of Marine and Coastal Science** uses labs and a small research-boat berth on the campus side of the working water, with vessel departures governed by the Choke's bar conditions. **Naval architecture** uses studios where full-scale lofting methods and modern design tools are taught together. The **School of Nursing** works in skills classrooms here and trains clinically at **Neptune Bay Medical Center** in Dune Bend. The **School of Law**, established in **1974**, uses a teaching building on the drier upper campus rather than the exposed pier.

**The museum.** UGN's **Museum of Natural and Cultural History** once held Singee belongings collected during the twentieth century. Those objects were returned to the Fold between **1994 and 2006** after the Nation's Return desk requested them. The old Singee gallery now displays **photographs of empty cases and the Nation's statement**. It contains no replicas passed off as replacements and no invented disclosure of restricted knowledge. A visitor may leave disappointed; that disappointment is part of the point.

**The pier gym.** The Ospreys' basketball gym stands on the old fitting-out pier. On nights when a Bay line train passes, the bleachers tremble, students chant over it, and the building reminds everyone which kind of infrastructure came first. The pier has its own structural inspection and flood-safety routines. It is a celebrated place that still has to close when a storm makes the access unsafe.

**The unglamorous heart.** Behind the Loft are loading bays, a campus receiving desk, linen and janitorial cupboards, and repair shops staffed before dawn. A room called **Dispatch** holds work orders and the keys to spaces the student-tour map omits. The people who keep the place habitable know the building's leaks by name.

## 5. The Academic Map

**Marine and Coastal Science.** The school's signature subjects are estuary ecology, fisheries, coastal hazards and the river mouth. A field student learns to read the bar board before asking to board a research vessel. When the Choke closes, the trip is canceled, not magically rescheduled as a scenic exercise. Students work through real changes in sampling schedules, safety plans and the quality of the data they can gather.

**Naval architecture.** The program's lineage runs through Bay Yard loftsmen who became instructors, some teaching into the 1960s. Students measure parts and learn why a tiny drafting error becomes expensive in steel. Several take paid work in **Yard Reach** shops; an old fabricator can respect their mathematics and still question whether they understand a repair contract that needs finishing by Friday.

**Nursing and the hospital.** Nursing supplies much of the region's medical workforce, and **Neptune Bay Medical Center** is its principal coastal clinical partner. The hard parts of student life include transportation to early clinical rotations, unpaid preparation time, fatigue and a term that continues while emergency rooms fill. A graduate may stay in Neptune because every shift is needed here.

**Law, Black Studies, and public history.** The Law School is known for **natural-resources** and **federal Indian law**, with graduates arguing on multiple sides of the timber wars and Nation attorneys using the same training to challenge the Authority. Black Studies holds the intellectual legacy of the **1968 occupation**. Its courses draw on the Rivers' history, local oral testimony with consent, and national histories of labor, housing and exclusion. Neither unit is treated as a prop for an all-knowing professor. Students and faculty disagree about sources, strategy, access and what a university owes the people it studies.

**Other studies and limits.** The university provides the expected arts, sciences and general undergraduate teaching of a regional public flagship, but it has **no veterinary school and no agriculture college**. Students who want those fields travel to **Oregon State in Corvallis**, outside the line. The campus's existence should not erase **Neptune Bay Community College**, whose trade programs and night students serve needs the flagship does not.

## 6. Who Can Afford to Be Here

**The typical student is a misleading fiction.** A student living with grandparents in Dune Bend, a thirty-year-old nursing transfer who works weekends, a first-generation law student from the Wilds and a graduate diver based part-year at **Varuna Station** can all identify with the U without sharing the same daily schedule.

**Housing.** Limited campus residence halls occupy newer buildings behind the upper Ways frontage; many students rent rooms in **the Ways**, **North Flats** or farther away. Older boardinghouses became duplexes long before the university had a housing policy. Campus growth creates demand for rooms and makes ordinary neighbors worry about rent. Students with money buy convenience; students without it learn the last bus schedule. The student housing description is a new campus-level development and implies no university expansion onto Rivers land.

**The long commute.** A commuting student packs a jacket, headphones, lunch and a backup phone battery before a 7:30 class. If a bridge approach backs up, she misses the opening quiz even if she woke on time. The student union's day lockers, microwave line and food pantry are important because a timetable can be more punishing than the coursework.

**The resident's day.** A first-year resident can spend whole weekends between the Loft, library and seawall without seeing much of Neptune. A club president thinks the city revolves around the academic calendar; a longshore worker leaving a shift knows the campus empties just when the waterfront gets busy.

**The returning adult.** A ship-repair apprentice taking an evening design class may know the names of the tools better than the young faculty member teaching their calculations. A hospital employee finishing a nursing credential keeps her parking receipts next to a packet of flashcards. The university is large enough to hold both, but its registration website assumes a student who can rearrange Tuesday at noon.

**Necessary frictions.** Advising queues, childcare needs, fee deadlines, inaccessible shortcuts down the graded ways, gaps between paid work and unpaid placement, and the uneven hours of student support all matter. A scene should put one of those difficulties in someone's path rather than use "college stress" as a generic label.

## 7. The People Who Keep It Running

**The governing lines.** In **1979** the Oregon Legislature created UGN from three existing colleges. From that date until **2015** it belonged to Oregon's public-university system. Since 2015 one **UGN board of trustees** has overseen the entire multi-campus university; one trustee is nominated by the **Nurven Nation** by statute. A flagship campus executive manages daily campus operations within the system's decisions. Neither the campus nor the system rules **Lhunpasi College**, which belongs to the Nation and remains independent.

**Whose work is visible.** Faculty teach, research assistants collect samples, boats are maintained, librarians preserve records, cooks open early, custodians clean the Loft after a basketball victory, technicians repair classroom air systems, grounds crews keep the Ways accessible, and student employees hold all of it together for hourly pay. Some workers have family ties to the wartime yard; others arrived last month.

**Outside partners.** The university works with the hospital, the port, federal coastal agencies, remaining repair yards, local schools and the Nation where written partnerships exist. The port can restrict a berth for safety or commerce; a research proposal is no exemption. A faculty member may need Nation approval for a collaborative project and cannot treat a trustee seat as blanket permission. Lhunpasi's credit transfers and joint teaching with the Normal continue on the Nation's terms.

**A standing argument.** Waterfront flood defenses, gym maintenance, classroom repairs and student aid compete for scarce money. A donor may favor a glass-fronted science wing while commuters need bus service and workers need predictable schedules. The university can describe every expenditure as educational; people affected by it can still reasonably contest the priorities.

## 8. The Public Memory and the Claim

**The official timeline has gaps in its emphasis.** University tours celebrate the transition from shipyard to open college, the preserved chalk floor, scientific work on the river and the long line of Ospreys. Community-led walking history remembers the 1943 firings, the workers who returned home to the Rivers, the realty restrictions that followed Black students, and the 1968 occupation.

**Three durable commitments from 1968.** Black Studies, the admissions program for Rivers high schools and the pledge against building on land taken from the Rivers are settled history. The pledge is kept. That fact matters: there is one specific victory to defend, and it cannot stand in for everything the city still owes the Rivers after the **1963–66 Harbor Center clearance**.

**The museum's emptied cases.** The gallery is a public record of restitution that declines to place someone else's restricted belongings on display. UGN can explain the return dates and leave the Nation's own words intact. Its absence of objects remains visible.

**What remains unresolved.** The **Red River Street Committee's** demand for land, a fund and an apology from the city remains unanswered; the city's **1998 study** remains on a shelf. That civic dispute is distinct from the university's 1968 land pledge. Students may organize around it, but no future campus scene should falsely claim the university paid, the city settled, or the Nation endorsed a particular solution.

## 9. A Year of Campus Life

**Autumn.** Rain begins to make the upper ramps slick. First-year students cluster beneath the Loft awnings; boat labs check weather more often; the student-housing noticeboard fills with late roommate requests. On a football Saturday, the town can hear celebrations from people who already know how the game will probably end.

**Winter.** The sea wind drives fine rain sideways. Salt gathers on exposed metal. Gloves and damp coats fill the Loft's hooks, and the library is quieter until the first missed bus brings a commuter in late. A blocked waterfront entrance reroutes students uphill through the teaching buildings; an evening nurse-student still needs to reach a clinical placement elsewhere in the city.

**Spring.** The first genuine **March sunbreak** brings students down the Ways to sit on grass above the seawall. There are too many jackets piled behind too few benches. Graduation announcements appear in the Loft, and some student finally dares someone to climb the old navy crane.

**Summer.** The city can begin under a thick marine layer. Marine students head out early if the bar allows; maintenance crews use the lighter class schedule to repair roof seams and flooded stairwell drains. Research groups rotate to **Varuna Station**, while the port and Yard Reach continue working their ordinary hours.

**The clock.** Before dawn: cleaners, hospital-bound nursing students and working waterfront vehicles. Midmorning: lecture corridors, food queues, field boats if conditions allow. Dusk: empty lecture rooms, gym noise and late library users. After midnight: occasional student windows lit above the Ways and the Bay line crossing farther along the bank.

## 10. Rituals, Teams and Student Speech

**Ospreys.** The athletics name is established. For this campus Bible the **colors are harbor navy and weathered copper**, carried on jerseys and old pennants. Basketball draws the biggest crowds at the **pier gym**; the football team is beloved regardless of its record. The colors are a new site-level choice and can anchor future campus art without changing the existing team's name.

**The first sunbreak.** Nobody officially schedules the gathering on the Ways. A lecturer watches her class empty when the clouds part; a student on a bench explains that there may not be another such afternoon all week.

**Graduation and the crane.** Every graduating class has students who climb the navy whirley crane illegally. Facilities staff spend commencement season adding warnings, calling people down and explaining that rusted industrial machinery is not a commemorative staircase. The tradition is established; the hazards are ordinary and real.

**The Galilee forum.** Every election year the **Galilee** candidate forum is held in the Loft. Politicians stand on ground remade into a university while people from the Rivers question them about the city that cleared their streets. The forum is neither a student-club showcase nor a substitute for Galilee Missionary Baptist Church's community authority.

**Three ordinary voices.**
- A commuter, at the bus stop: "If the door is shut, tell them the bridge ate my ten minutes."
- A boat-lab student: "The bar's red. We have spreadsheets today."
- A grounds worker, at the foot of the Ways: "That crane is older than your diploma. Come down."

## 11. Story Routes and Pressure Points

**Route A — A first-week mistake.** A student leaves the bus stop uphill, walks past the old administration stairs, and reaches the Loft only to learn the classroom is in a lab near the waterfront. Rain drives her to the longer accessible path around the steep old ways. Her professor has already begun. This is a route about money, time and the campus's inherited geometry.

**Route B — The evidence and the job.** A marine research team is ready to sail; the Choke's conditions close the bar. A graduate assistant loses planned sample time, a student worker loses scheduled hours, and a boat technician is relieved not to argue with weather. The same canceled departure has different consequences for each.

**Route C — A history in two records.** A student reads the yard's familiar honor plaque, then goes to a Rivers community event and sees the 1943 auxiliary firing list. She returns to the Chart Room to compare surviving records, discovering that the missing entry is an archival problem she has to investigate with the people who own their testimony. The scene's tension comes from evidence, permission and accountability rather than a supernatural revelation.

**Reusable situations.** A law student prepares an argument against a policy their own alumnus helped write; a nursing student cannot reach a shift when a storm blocks the road; a family wants a worker's name included on a yard plaque; a resident resists another rental being converted into student rooms; a basketball victory is interrupted by a safety closure that staff have been warning about for months.

**The contradiction that endures.** A public university can offer opportunity and still occupy a landscape whose economic and racial inequalities preceded it. The campus inherits the shipyard's structures, the Rivers' labor and a civic debt it cannot unilaterally settle. That tension supports many stories across decades without forcing any one novel to resolve the city's history.

## 12. The Continuity Ledger

**Established regional facts used here.** Neptune lies on the coastal river mouth with a working port and the Choke; UGN flagship is on the former Bay Yard principal shipways in **Ward 2, the Ways**; the Loft is the mold loft; the Ways lead to the seawall; the crane is navy; the Ospreys have Division I basketball in the fitting-out-pier gym; the Bay line runs nearby at night; enrollment is about **17,800**. The yard dates and ship totals, 1943 firings, Rivers housing discrimination, 1968 occupation and commitments, 1969 Black Studies department, university names, 1979 UGN merger, 2015 board, 1994–2006 repatriation, program specialties, clinical partner and the lack of agriculture/veterinary schools are all established in the parent files.

**New public-register developments in this Bible.** The small upper-campus residence halls; library informally called the **Library of the Ways** and its **Chart Room**; receiving/work-order room called **Dispatch**; day lockers and food pantry at the Loft; graded accessible alternate routes; the form of the 1968 administrative marker and auxiliary list display; student route descriptions; and harbor navy/weathered-copper athletic colors. These are ordinary local additions, without changes to regionwide geography, history or supernatural mechanics.

**No new facts about protected mysteries.** The campus has no special insight into the forest's deep register. The Nation's restricted knowledge remains restricted. Lhunpasi College is sovereign and separate. Present-day presidents, trustees, student activists and novel protagonists are intentionally unnamed so independent books can cast them without cross-novel cameos.

**Before writing a campus scene.** Check the season and coastal fog, the public bar report, whether the character is commuting or housed near the Ways, whether a route touches active port land, who owns a historical account, and whether their degree's institutional name matches the year. All travel time and distances defer to *The Greater Neptune Region — Road Atlas*.
