# UGN Cadence — Campus Bible (new)

*The college at the crest: a school shaped by snow, fire seasons, working students, and the long road home*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

**Scope:** Public-register campus Bible for **UGN Cadence (UGNC)**, the west-side campus of Cadence, Oregon, in the Greater Neptune Region. The shared regional Master Reference and University of Greater Neptune companion govern established facts. This Bible adds named buildings, routines, student-life texture, campus-scale geography, and narrative possibilities within those limits. It creates no new supernatural rules, no new roads or mileages, and no claims over the Nurven Nation's independent college.

**Template:** *UGN Campus Bible — Reusable Template (new)*, twelve sections. Keep this campus distinct from the Neptune flagship: UGNC's organizing forces are **weather, the fire calendar, and the problem of getting home**.

## 1. The Campus in One Glance

**Formal name.** University of Greater Neptune at Cadence. On course catalogs and institutional signs: **UGN Cadence**. Out loud: **UGNC**, four letters spoken quickly. Students seldom say the university's full name; they say "I'm at UGNC," "up at the college," or, when there is no other campus in the conversation, "campus."

**Founded and folded into the system.** The state founded **Central Cascades State College in 1966** on the west edge of the Suttle Lake basin. It became part of the University of Greater Neptune when the statewide legislature formed the UGN system in **1979**. The flagship in Neptune is the system's administrative seat; UGNC retains its own practical identity and priorities. Roughly **8,200 students** are enrolled at UGNC, separate from the flagship's approximately 17,800.

**Where it sits.** The campus begins at **Causeway Mile 178**, in **Lakeward**, on Cadence's western edge. Cadence's older center is at **Mile 181**; the boundary of the Greater Neptune Region is at **Mile 189**, with real Sisters immediately beyond it. These mileposts refer to the Causeway, not to campus roads or a shoreline. The college stands among pine and fir on the basin's western margin, with the high crest visible behind it when weather allows. It is **not a waterfront resort campus**, and no campus building requires a newly invented arm of Suttle Lake.

**Its reputation.** The **School of Forestry and Fire** attracts students from across the region. The campus also teaches outdoor recreation, hospitality, nursing, chemistry and the ordinary general-education work of a regional university. Its **fifty-meter natatorium** is the only pool of that size inside the region. The **Lookouts**, in **fire orange and charcoal**, play in the gym called **the Tower**. The cross-country team trains along the Rim Line Road while it is open.

**What the viewbook says.** "Learn from the country you will work in." It shows a student in a hard hat against ponderosa trunks and another studying by a window with mountains beyond.

**What an older Cadence resident says.** "Every fall they bring in more students, and every February half of them find out why the roofs are built like that." This is affectionate when said by a bus driver and less affectionate when said by a landlord discussing rent.

**The campus's working contradiction.** UGNC teaches people how to manage forests, water, hospitality and emergency response, while those same industries make their students' lives unstable. Its success is measured as much by who manages to finish after a season of work as by who arrives on the first day of term.

## 2. The Ground Before the School

**Before 1966.** Cadence was a ranch-and-timber crossroads whose later prosperity depended on the road over Santiam Pass, the **ski bowl opened in 1938**, and postwar growth in mills, tourism and services. By the early 1960s it had a hospital, schools and seasonal jobs, but families wanting college often had to send their children to Eugene or Bend. Central Cascades State College was founded to keep more of that learning and work on the high side of the mountains. The school began on previously settled edge-of-town ground in the pine-and-fir basin; its record does not confer a fictional Indigenous land claim or invent a new historical treaty.

**The first building.** **First Hall** is the campus's original 1966 teaching-and-administration building, a low, long structure of masonry and cedar-faced entries with broad snow-shedding roofs. A small brass plaque lists the founding year and original college name. Retrofitted entrances and service corridors reveal decades of compromises: an entrance sheltered from wind, a ramp added later, an elevator fitted where an office once stood, and an old external staircase permanently closed in winter. "First Hall" is a new campus-level name.

**The changes that mattered.** In **1979**, the college joined UGN while keeping its local academic strengths. In **1987**, after **Marlow Pacific's East Wild mill closed**, UGNC offered retraining for laid-off mill workers. Some drove over **Saltlick Grade** while it was seasonally open and then took the longer southern road network when snow closed it. A portion of those workers later became instructors. The campus remembers this as evidence that a public university can answer an economic crisis, but former students remember tuition forms, lost wages and the difficulty of learning beside eighteen-year-olds.

**The winter people still date by.** During the **Nine-Week Winter of 1993**, Santiam Pass closed to the wet side for nine weeks. UGNC's dormitories housed stranded travelers, and its dining hall fed people from Cadence's west end. The campus became a heating and information center; that is why the most important memorial to 1993 is an ordinary community-services binder that facilities workers still update. The eastern approach through Sisters and Bend remained possible; descriptions of the event must never claim the entire city was sealed on every side.

**The record on the wall.** The dining commons has a framed 1993 notice reproduced from the period—where to find a cot, where to sign in, when to return for hot food. Across from it hangs a class photograph from the first forestry program. Each is useful, but neither tells a complete story. The people who shoveled routes, staffed kitchens, sheltered strangers and drove deliveries kept the college operating. A new oral-history corner in the campus library records their accounts with permission instead of presenting them as a single heroic legend.

**A continuing argument about the past.** Alumni brochures tend to tell the 1987 retraining story as uplift. Families remember the mill shutdown as years of foreclosed mortgages, long commutes and embarrassment over learning a new trade. Both accounts can be true. Nobody at UGNC is entitled to tidy the second one away.

## 3. The Campus Underfoot

**The shape.** UGNC is compact enough to cross on foot, though weather changes what "across" means. Rooflines remain low and broad beneath pine and fir, with deep eaves designed to shed snow away from the busiest doors. A linked series of **covered walkways** is known as **the Walk**. The buildings are deliberately grouped around an inward-facing paved **Shelter Court**, protected by the building mass from the worst winter wind. Students call the court **the Pocket** because it holds a little warmth on days when the exposed parking lot is bitter.

**The west entrance.** The regional **fifty-meter natatorium** and its early-shift parking area stand at the campus's west edge beside **Mile 178**. The **Lakeward bus stop** and main campus arrival path are nearby. Cars bearing swim bags arrive before classes, often from towns with smaller pools or no regulation-length pool at all. The bus stop is an ordinary transit stop, not a new highway spur; it leaves the Road Atlas's mileposts unchanged.

**A walk a first-year can repeat.** Enter at the Lakeward bus stop, pass the natatorium's glass-fronted lobby, continue under the first covered segment to **First Hall**, cross **the Pocket** to **the Commons**, follow **the Walk** toward **Crest Library**, turn into **Crew Hall** for Forestry and Fire, and continue toward **the Tower**. The **East Walk residence halls** lie on the more sheltered campus side toward nearby rentals. Someone leaving for **Juniper Flats** follows the public sidewalk toward town. This is an internal campus route, with no asserted road distances.

**What each edge means.** The forest-facing side holds teaching plots, managed demonstration plantings and an equipment compound; these are instructional spaces with posted access, not an unregulated wildland burn area. Residential rentals, gear repair, shops and municipal streets lie toward Lakeward and Juniper Flats. The city, not the university, runs its own roads, bus service, water decisions and emergency response. An adjacent forest edge does not grant UGNC authority over federal forest land or the Nation's sovereign institutions.

**Who gets to move easily.** Snow sheds where the roof designers planned it, and piles where students want to walk. The covered Walk reduces exposure; it does not eliminate ice, plow banks or maintenance closures. Accessible routes are graded and cleared first under the campus's winter plan, though the long detour around a closed stair can make someone late. A permanent weather board at the Commons entrance lists building access and class changes. A campus shuttle circulates among parking and principal buildings during the academic day; the city bus connects Lakeward to Old Pass, the hospital and housing, subject to its seasonal schedule. Students plan for its last run.

**The view.** A clear morning gives the campus a startling white crest beyond dark trees. On many winter days a person sees only the near line of pines and the orange jacket of someone crossing between buildings. A sudden blue opening in the clouds can last as long as the walk from First Hall to the Tower.

## 4. Buildings That Have a Life

**First Hall — the old college inside the new university.** This is still where students register, meet advisers, find humanities classrooms and make appeals. The hall's cork noticeboards are more useful than its polished welcome screen: a fire-season bursary form, a room for rent, a lost glove, a handwritten change to office hours. From one stairwell window students can see the first roof from 1966, patched in a slightly different metal from the extensions.

**The Commons — heat, food and the public.** The dining hall, student gathering room, commuter lockers and basic-needs desk occupy a broad building with two sets of draft-catching vestibules. Its layout preserves the ability to move tables aside and serve large numbers of people during emergencies, learned from the Nine-Week Winter. A low-key supply room now holds blankets, bottled water, cot frames and the winter contact list. On a regular Tuesday the building smells of toast, damp wool and someone else's microwaved leftovers. The **Weather Board** by the door is checked by students before they check their messages.

**Crest Library — the quiet middle.** An unshowy library has tall windows facing pines, a ground-floor map and government-documents area, a quiet study room called **the Still Room**, and small booths used by students taking remote meetings with advisers or family. It holds forest-management records, oral histories, public planning files and student research. It does not possess restricted Nurven Nation archives. The first winter exam week fills the windows with fleece jackets hung over chair backs, but a campus worker seeking quiet before a night shift can usually find the rear table.

**Crew Hall — the Forestry and Fire home.** Boot brushes and a covered gear vestibule give away the building's purpose. It contains labs for forest ecology and mapping, forestry measurements, equipment-maintenance instruction, incident-command tabletop exercises, and a controlled training yard. The yard teaches safe handling and inspection on campus; real fire assignments and authorized burns happen only under the responsible agencies' rules off campus. A row of lockers bears taped notes from people who will return from crews after the term begins. A temporary notice reading **"RETURNING FROM ASSIGNMENT? START HERE"** redirects them to a standing late-start advising process.

**The chemistry building — the argument made concrete.** **Basin Science Hall** contains general chemistry and core sciences. Its instructors, laboratory safety rules and required sequences run on scheduled terms. The **School of Forestry and Fire's late-start allowance** is not honored by the chemistry department. That mismatch is institutional and personal: a student can return from a demanding wildfire assignment in October, having done the right thing by their employer, and still be behind in a chemistry laboratory that does not offer a safe, simple make-up session. The campus keeps both policies. A faculty meeting can be about fairness while also being about lab staffing, safety and money.

**The natatorium — the regional pool.** The **UGNC natatorium**, at the west end near Mile 178, holds the **region's only fifty-meter pool**. Clubs and water-polo teams reserve lanes before dawn. Swimmers enter with steaming breath, shake melting grit from shoes and move into air thick with chlorine and the odor of wet wool. The pool's regional importance makes scheduling contentious: university teams, youth clubs and public-access sessions all claim precious hours. The building has an accessible changing route, spectator seating and strict separation between wet deck and winter boots. Its notices distinguish campus athletics from independent club reservations.

**The Tower — a small gym with a big memory.** The Lookouts play basketball here. Its name comes from an original squat observation-like entry element rather than a new forest lookout tower on a mountain. Orange and charcoal banners hang above the bleachers; the floor is older than the newest heating equipment. The **Wilds Cup** against **the Normal's Engineers** brings East Wild to Cadence each February. When a game's last bus runs, student staff have to manage an enthusiastic crowd whose ride home has become complicated.

**East Walk halls — where college continues after class.** UGNC has real dorms; their residential wing occupies the sheltered side of campus along the Walk, with shared kitchens, laundry, study alcoves and a lounge that gets crowded when the ski-bowl buses return. Students who cannot afford campus rooms take rentals in **Juniper Flats** or elsewhere in town. The halls' common room opens as extra shelter during severe regional disruptions, subject to emergency operations. Housing staff are workers with shifts and limits, not perpetual caretakers.

**The room people overlook.** Behind the Commons, **the Snow Desk** is a small operations office holding shift rosters, plow-priority maps, spare radios and the keys to emergency supplies. Its name is informal and new. A student's appeal for a cleared ramp can reach the Snow Desk sooner than a formal complaint; it can also reveal that only two workers are scheduled where five were budgeted.

**The building everyone argues over.** First Hall needs extensive envelope repairs, and a proposal to expand modern science space has raised the question of whether to preserve it or replace its aging wing. Faculty see classrooms; preservation advocates see the college's founding; accessibility staff see a plan that might finally correct the difficult routes; custodians see a building whose leaks have been theirs for decades. None of those interests is foolish.

## 5. The Academic Map

**The School of Forestry and Fire.** UGNC's defining school teaches forest ecology, silviculture, recreation management, fire behavior, fuels, field safety, mapping and public-land decision-making. Students carry measuring tapes and notebooks into controlled teaching plots, return from field days with grit in their socks, and learn that a forest stand has more than one economically and ecologically significant history. Some students spend summers with **the Forest Service, state agencies or private contractors**. They take those jobs for experience, wages, credentials or the practical possibility of returning the next year.

**The late-start rule and its edge.** A standing School of Forestry and Fire policy accommodates students whose fire assignments extend into autumn. They can start approved school work late without academic penalty in participating courses. **Chemistry does not follow that accommodation.** There is no secret campus-wide exemption, no guaranteed replacement of every missed lab, and no effortless return from a dangerous job to an ordinary course schedule. The policy's limits are a reliable source of character stakes.

**Fire training is also public argument.** A silviculture classroom can contain students whose parents worked for a timber company, a student who values older stands and a student whose summer wages come from suppression contracts. A good teacher gives them evidence to contest and work to complete. Different views about cutting, controlled fire, protection and recreation do not secretly prove anything about the forest's deep register.

**Recreation and hospitality.** The campus's outdoor recreation and hospitality programs train guides, facilities managers, outdoor-program leaders and seasonal business workers who know a closed pass can erase a wedding booking or postpone staff arrivals. They use the ski bowl and the lake-country economy without assuming that tourism is the only kind of life Cadence contains. An instructor's fieldwork may depend on an accessible trail, an operating lift or a permit held by someone else.

**Nursing.** Students train with **Cadence Basin Medical Center**, a general hospital that treats winter crashes, respiratory illness, smoke exposure and the everyday needs of families living in the basin. A nursing student's clinical shift is a morning on the hospital floor, not an invitation to invent a university-owned hospital. Snow, childcare and road access influence attendance as surely as examinations do.

**Chemistry and general education.** UGNC's science faculty teach cohorts that include forestry students, nursing students, hospitality majors and people taking core requirements. The chemistry department's strict lab sequence should be treated as a coherent institutional choice with costs, not as the personal cruelty of one invented professor. The same student might find a thoughtful faculty mentor in chemistry and still be required to repeat a missed course.

**One commuter's Tuesday.** A junior staying with relatives in **Old Pass** checks the city transit notice, carries a thermos to the Commons, attends morning chemistry, eats in the Still Room between classes and leaves early for a shift at a gear shop. A snow-clearing delay can cost a lab attendance mark and two paid hours.

**One forestry student's October return.** A crew member completes an extended fire assignment, arrives in Cadence with a bag of dirty clothing, heads first to Crew Hall to arrange an approved late start, then learns that the next chemistry lab requires work from three weeks earlier. They have one evening to do laundry, buy groceries, email two instructors and find a way to stay enrolled.

**Where the limits are.** UGNC is a regional campus, not a duplicate of every professional school at the Neptune flagship. The university's law school and marine programs are based in Neptune. **Lhunpasi College remains independent of UGN**; any shared research or instruction involving the Nation requires appropriate consent and partnership. Academic research about the forest cannot answer the shared world's protected mysteries.

## 6. Who Can Afford to Be Here

**Students come from more than one economy.** UGNC's approximately 8,200 students include families of mill workers, ranchers, teachers and tribal-government employees, city and basin residents, wet-side students who cross the pass, seasonal resort workers, aspiring foresters and adults changing careers. Some have parent-funded apartments and money for skis. Others need a crew assignment to afford the year. The university's picturesque trees can be a luxury to one student and a worksite to another.

**Three lives at one bus stop.** A local **first-generation commuter** lives in a crowded Juniper Flats rental and knows which route the city clears first. A **wet-side student** shares an East Walk room and learns to say **"going wet"** when leaving for home over the pass on a weekend. A **returning adult** who once worked in timber is enrolled in a short credential while supporting children, and can name every mill that used to hire. Their reasons for being here barely overlap.

**Housing.** East Walk dorm rooms reduce commuting but have limited space and cannot replace the town's rental market. **Juniper Flats** offers duplexes and shared houses; **Lakeward** offers student apartments above or behind shops. Rising demand collides with seasonal tourism, construction wages, short-term rentals and ordinary local housing needs. No fixed rent figures are assigned here; any future period piece must price housing to its own year.

**The essential gear problem.** A student studying fire and forestry may need boots, protective clothing for approved activities, lab supplies and transport to field placements. The campus gear-loan cupboard in Crew Hall can lend measuring instruments and certain instructional items; employers issue their own protective equipment under agency rules. Financial aid seldom arrives precisely when a student's work boots fail. A student may keep a damaged pair a week longer than they should because their paycheck is delayed.

**Access requires maintenance.** Covered paths help students with mobility, sensory, concentration or fatigue-related access needs as much as everyone else. Good intentions do not clear snow. The campus has accessible building routes, quiet study options, recorded public lectures where appropriate and a disability-services office; pressure points include last-minute room relocations, ice at entrances and the belief that every field student can simply hike a faster route.

**A common administrative trap.** A working student can qualify for the Forestry and Fire late-start policy, keep a legitimate paid fire-season job, and still lose progress in a required chemistry sequence. Financial-aid satisfactory-progress rules, housing dates, staffing and course availability can compound the delay. It is a material story problem that does not need an unkind dean to be real.

**The town has its own students of life.** A college student may learn from a lift mechanic, plow driver, nurse, tribal forester, town water technician or 1987 retraining alumnus who has never held an academic title. Conversely, an outsider should not be written as if every Cadence resident knows or cares about UGNC's athletics calendar.

## 7. The People Who Keep It Running

**Where decisions sit.** The UGN system was formed in **1979** and acquired its own **board of trustees in 2015**, with one trustee nominated by the **Nurven Nation** under statute. UGNC has campus administration and academic leadership within that system. The **Cadence city council** is elected at large on nonpartisan ballots; it manages municipal matters, separate from the regional **Bioregional Authority** and separate from UGN. County lines divide Cadence between **Jefferson and Deschutes** without dividing it into two cities. The campus does not make municipal water policy.

**The work before sunrise.** The natatorium opens to swimmers while staff test pool water and clear the deck. Grounds and facilities workers inspect the Walk, clear ice from accessible approaches, remove plow piles and restart a finicky heating unit. The Commons opens early; a hospital-bound student catches a bus. Custodial crews may stay late after a Lookouts game; the work is ongoing and rarely appears in the recruitment photographs.

**The School of Forestry and Fire's partnerships.** Field training is negotiated with relevant landowners and agencies, with safety rules and permits. Seasonal placements with federal, state and private fire organizations are jobs, with supervisors, overtime, equipment standards and responsibilities beyond the university. Industry relationships sometimes fund equipment and sometimes create real arguments over research independence.

**The hospital and the city.** **Cadence Basin Medical Center** employs nurses and clinicians who also teach or supervise UGNC students. The city's public-works and fire-and-rescue departments coordinate with campus operations over road conditions, emergencies and smoke. The local bus system's route choices can determine whether a student reaches placement. A dispute about more student buses is also a dispute about service for hospital staff and elderly residents.

**The Nation.** The Nurven Nation nominates a trustee to UGN's system board, but that does not make **Lhunpasi College at the Fold** a branch, annex or program administered by UGNC. Partnerships require explicit agreement. A Nation student's knowledge or political views should not be used as automatic explanation for the forest's deep register.

**Who gets heard.** A donor may want a conspicuous new fire-science facility. Field instructors need maintained vehicles and safe equipment. Students need predictable course schedules. Workers need enough staff to keep walkways open. Neighbors want better housing and groundwater stewardship. A university budget meeting becomes useful fiction when those claims compete and the person taking minutes can see each cost.

## 8. The Public Memory and the Claim

**A shared memory worth commemorating.** The central UGNC story is **1993**. The campus sheltered stranded travelers and fed residents when the pass to the west closed for nine weeks. Each winter, an early-season orientation in the Commons reviews the emergency plan and displays the names of participating community organizations where records allow. The focus is on practice—supplies, access, staffing, neighbor contact—rather than a sentimental promise that one building could save a city alone.

**A remembered injury.** **1987** is a second founding story for some families. The mill closure brought skilled workers into classrooms for retraining but also altered the economic prospects of whole households. The school is entitled to take pride in the program; students and descendants are entitled to remember why it was needed. A retired sawyer teaching a field course may bristle at a poster saying "reinvention" while remembering people who could not afford the term.

**The college's present claim.** UGNC often presents itself as the region's practical steward of forests and fire. That public commitment can be tested by who is hired for research, whether community representatives participate in decisions, whether student jobs pay, whether data are made usable to residents, and who bears risk when field programs expand.

**The water question.** Cadence's growth raises sustained concerns about wells, water rights and connected groundwater systems. UGNC has hydrologists and other scientific expertise it can share, but it does not get to resolve municipal water-use politics by producing one authoritative chart. A student whose family owns a low-yield well may feel very differently about campus expansion than an administrator planning new housing.

**The fire question.** Forest-interface neighborhoods argue over defensible space, screening trees, evacuation access, insurance and cost. Students who have worked crews know that fire safety entails hard choices; another resident may know that a required clearance project costs more than a family can pay. No group needs to become a caricature for conflict to exist.

**Author's knowledge only.** Avoid turning a campus's expertise into a supernatural explanation. An old-growth stand may carry a story elsewhere in the Neptune Continuum, but in this Bible every public course, survey, exercise and archive remains ordinary evidence about ordinary subjects. Restricted histories stay with their custodians.

## 9. A Year of Campus Life

**Autumn — return in uneven waves.** Some forestry students return from fire crews weeks after their classmates. Packs lean against Crew Hall lockers. Notices about making up an academic term compete with flyers for field jobs next summer. The first cold morning sends most people along the Walk instead of cutting across the Pocket. The mountain wind removes the day's warmth before evening classes end. Chemistry labs continue on their schedule while the late-start advising desk stays busy.

**Winter — the campus shrinks to covered paths.** Plows pile gray-edged snow beyond the main entries, boots squeak on grit and the air in the Commons smells of soup and wet wool. The natatorium car park fills before sunrise with drivers who checked the pass report. Campus closure notices carry consequences: some students can stay in dorms, some commute locally, and some cannot cross from the wet side. **Santiam Pass is normally maintained and open with chains**; it is inaccurate to treat every snow as a nine-week closure. The **1993** shutdown remains an exceptional shared memory. The Rim Line Road, by contrast, **closes with the first real snow**.

**Spring — thaw makes its own inconveniences.** Meltwater finds the weak joints in First Hall's oldest roof. Snow fences and grit linger after students begin eating outside again. The forestry plots yield measured changes between seasons. A student who considered leaving in January signs up for next year's fire-assignment interviews; another discovers that a delayed chemistry requirement will extend graduation.

**Summer — the academic calendar splits in two.** Many forestry students leave for crews, recreation students take seasonal jobs, and the college's campus population thins. The school keeps practical courses and research running, but **smoke** can ground fieldwork, restrict outdoor sport and fill the hospital with respiratory patients. Visitors in shorts read the tourism brochures; locals look at the air-quality notice. At dusk the heat can drop abruptly under the pines.

**Before dawn.** Cars turn into the natatorium with wipers running. The pool deck smells of chlorine, and a student employee tries to find the key to a supply cupboard.

**Midday.** People stream from First Hall to the Commons; the Pocket is briefly warm when the sun is out. A forestry lab student has mud on one cuff; an adviser has a queue of exhausted first-years.

**Dusk.** The last available town bus becomes urgent. Orange Lookouts shirts emerge from the Tower, while the Still Room stays occupied.

**After midnight.** A few residence windows glow behind pines. Facilities staff hear a heating relay restart. During a severe weather response, the Snow Desk light stays on and the Commons may reopen for people with no safe route home.

**A field test of time.** A club-swimmer's parent leaves a wet-side town in darkness, drives through snow conditions as road rules permit, and finds that the natatorium itself is open while the westbound trip home is becoming less certain. A campus student has to decide whether to stay in Cadence for the day. The road report should shape the scene rather than be mentioned as decoration.

## 10. Rituals, Teams and Student Speech

**The Lookouts.** UGNC's existing athletics name is the **Lookouts**, with **fire orange and charcoal** as established colors. Basketball is played in **the Tower**. The cross-country team runs on **Rim Line Road** before snow closes it; afterward coaches use lower local routes and indoor conditioning. A Lookouts sweatshirt is a campus allegiance. A **Switchbacks** cap is allegiance to Cadence's separate summer baseball team.

**The Wilds Cup.** Every February the Lookouts meet the **Normal's Engineers**, drawing supporters from East Wild. A visitor can feel the rivalry in a crowded small gym. A home-game triumph does not make the trip back down from Cadence any easier, and student volunteers are responsible for the mundane business of clearing bleachers after the celebration.

**"Going wet."** Students from west of the crest call weekend travel home **going wet**, a nickname established in the UGN canon. For a local raised in the dry-side basin, it can sound slightly theatrical. A student who has been stuck on the wrong side of the pass for a holiday uses it differently.

**The first pass report.** Each autumn students crowd the Commons Weather Board for the first notice of chain restrictions or approaching snow. Older students announce the conditions as if they personally arranged the weather. This gathering is an unofficial campus ritual; the warnings themselves come from ordinary road and weather authorities.

**The orange line.** On the first Monday of the academic year the Lookouts' student volunteers lay a strip of orange tape along a safe section of the Walk toward the Tower for newcomers to follow to orientation. By the end of the day the wind and wet shoes have lifted most of it. Facilities insists it stay off accessible thresholds; the student union insists the ritual is harmless. This new tradition offers a campus image without inventing a reckless ascent of an actual fire tower.

**The crew check-in.** Students arriving late from fire assignments leave a dated note or check in with the Forestry and Fire adviser. The annual pinned list on Crew Hall's noticeboard reads **"Back in town"**. It makes returning visible without presenting all fire work as glory: sometimes somebody writes only a name and "tired."

**Three voices from different parts of the school.**

> **Pool desk attendant, 5:07 a.m.:** "If you've got a dry pair of shoes, change before you come through this door."

> **Returning forestry student, outside Basin Science Hall:** "Crew Hall says I'm back on time. Chemistry says I wasn't here."

> **Custodial worker, after a February game:** "Lookouts won. The bleachers didn't clean themselves."

**What not to transplant.** UGNC students do not refer to Neptune's shipyard lawn as their own campus green, and they do not treat the Ospreys as their campus team. The U's Loft, the Normal's railway identity and the Station's lake research belong to other places in the same university system.

## 11. Story Routes and Pressure Points

**Route A — the 5 a.m. swim.** A car stops at the Mile 178 edge after an early drive, the swimmer walks from the natatorium entrance across rubber mats to the deck, and the driver waits under fluorescent lights with a thermos. After practice, they check conditions for the return journey. The conflict can be a missed work shift, a frightened parent, a coach refusing a late lane change or a conversation neither traveler wants to have. The pool remains a public athletic institution; any supernatural consequences belong to an individual novel.

**Route B — the October return.** A forestry student comes back from a summer crew assignment after the term has started, crosses the Pocket with a duffel, goes to Crew Hall for the allowed late start, then to Basin Science Hall where chemistry is already three labs ahead. Finally they go to the Commons to decide whether to call home, ask for help or register for the course next term. The scene is shaped by an authentic departmental contradiction.

**Route C — the night the shelter plan matters.** A winter storm complicates travel. A city bus driver leaves students near the covered Walk; the Snow Desk tracks staffing; the Commons shifts from dinner service to a warming and information space. A visiting parent expects the institution to take charge, while the city and campus have separate legal powers. The tension lies in limited supplies and imperfect coordination, with the 1993 precedent behind the decision.

**Five additional reusable situations.** A town resident challenges the draw of new campus housing on limited groundwater; an outdoor-recreation intern is asked to work an unpaid shift during a lucrative tourist weekend; a senior in chemistry volunteers to tutor a returning crew student and finds the department cannot waive a safety requirement; a library archivist discovers that the 1987 retraining photographs credited donors but omitted workers' names; a student in a smoke-season class has to decide whether to report unsafe training conditions when their future employer may be listening.

**A contradiction with no clean ending.** The university depends on the very fire, recreation and health-care labor that disrupts students' schooling. Cadence depends on the university while contending with housing demand, water use, transportation and emergency resources. A responsible campus can keep revising its policies without ending those disagreements. They should survive any one protagonist's graduation.

## 12. The Continuity Ledger

**Established regional canon preserved.**
- Cadence is built in the **Suttle Lake basin and Black Butte flats**, west of real Sisters, on the dry side of the crest; UGNC is at **Causeway Mile 178** in **Lakeward**, Cadence center at **Mile 181**, the regional boundary at **Mile 189**.
- The campus began as **Central Cascades State College in 1966** and joined UGN in **1979**; approximately **8,200** students.
- Long, low buildings, deep eaves and **covered walkways** are established weather adaptations.
- **School of Forestry and Fire** offers late fall starts for fire-crew students; **chemistry does not**.
- The **1987** East Wild mill closure produced UGNC retraining programs; the **1993 Nine-Week Winter** closed Santiam Pass to the west for nine weeks, and the university sheltered travelers and fed the west end.

- Lookouts colors are **fire orange and charcoal**, settled in *Neptune and Cadence*. The **Cadence Switchbacks** are a separate city baseball club.

- The **Road Atlas** controls all mileage, drive times and seasonal closures. Santiam Pass generally remains open with chains; Saltlick Grade and Rim Line Road close seasonally as specified.
