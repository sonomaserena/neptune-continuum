# The Singee: A History

*From the river terraces to the immersion preschool at the Fold*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3* and the three *Singee Language* files

## How to Use This History

This is the public history of the Singee: what the Nation's archive, the federal record and the region's schools would all agree happened, and what any character could learn. It is written the way a Singee historian would want an outsider to read it, plainly, with the dates. It leaves out three things on purpose.

The first is the Singee's own account of where they come from. It's told with the old-telling ending, it belongs to the Nation, and no outsider's book retells it. The second is anything inside the restricted-disclosure parcels. The third is what the Singee call Mrs. Birdsong. All three stay closed.

Everything here sits on real Oregon history. Where a date belongs to the real world, it's the real date, and real nations appear only as they really were.

## Before Contact: The Terraces and the Confederation

The Singee lived where they still live: in the wet heart of the forest, on the river terraces of the McKenzie, the Middle Fork and the North Santiam, and in the high, rainy country between them. A village sat wherever a terrace was wide enough to hold one, above the floods and below the snow. The oldest villages stood on ground that is now Five Fords, Veilwood and Fold Creek.

Each village ran its own affairs. What held them together was the Nurven, the confederation, Nuxven in Singee. Every autumn, when the salmon ran, speakers from every village met at Vasa'isa, the five crossings, the place English now calls Five Fords. They settled boundaries and marriages and old grudges, traded what the high country had for what the coast and the valley had, and went home before the snow. The Singee still call that gathering by the plain word for the season, ruveni, autumn, the way you'd say "Thanksgiving" and mean the whole weekend.

The Singee married and traded outward in every direction: down the rivers to the coast, out onto the valley floor and over the passes to the dry side. They were never isolated. They were simply uphill.

## The Deep Night: The Fever Years, 1830 to 1834

In 1830, a fever came up the Columbia. The settlers called it fever and ague, and it was malaria. Year after year it struck in the warm months, and it killed Native people in numbers the region had never seen. On the lower Columbia and across the Willamette Valley, whole towns were emptied. By the time it passed, the peoples of the valley floor had lost most of their population.

The Singee were hit too, in their lower villages, but less, and the reason was ecological. The mosquitoes that carried the fever bred in warm, low, swampy ground, and they didn't breed in the high country. The terraces and the rainy uplands where most Singee lived were above the fever's reach.

The Singee call those years pulhi masi, the deep night. Some survivors from the valley came up into the high country, and Singee families took them in. It is a hard thing to be the people the fever mostly missed, and Singee elders speak of it carefully. When they tell it, they use the told ending, not the seen one: nobody living saw it.

The fever left another legacy no one intended. By the 1840s, when settlers began claiming the valley, they found a country that looked empty because it had just been emptied. The high country, where the Singee still were, looked empty for a different reason. Nobody from outside had gone up to look.

## Left Off the List: The 1850s

In the 1850s, federal treaty commissions worked the coast and the valley, negotiating the cessions that would carry the region's peoples onto reservations. They never went up into the interior forest. On their maps it was blank, and blank land needs no treaty. No Singee treaty was ever written.

When the removals came in 1856, some Singee families who had married into the valley or the coast were swept onto reservations under other peoples' names. Their great-grandchildren would spend the twentieth century carrying two identities, one on paper and one at home. Most Singee stayed in the high country, where nobody came looking.

## The Man with the Phonograph: 1894

In the summer of 1894, a museum ethnographer from Philadelphia named Lucius V. Thorne came up the McKenzie with a wax-cylinder phonograph. The machine was barely four years old as a field tool. Each cylinder held about two and a half minutes, and speakers had to stand close and shout into the horn. Thorne recorded forty-one cylinders at Five Fords and on the Veilwood terraces: songs, word lists, a man counting to a hundred, and one elder telling a story he didn't understand and labeled "fragment, unintelligible."

Thorne had a theory before he arrived, and the summer didn't change it. In his 1896 monograph he classified the Singee as a "mountain band" of the valley's Kalapuyan peoples, speaking what he called a "corrupted upland dialect." He published his word lists in a notation full of symbols no printer had type for. Then he moved on to the Southwest and never came back.

His classification did more harm than his visit. He didn't cause the Singee to be left out. That had happened in the 1850s. But he certified it. For the next seventy-five years, every time a Singee petition reached Washington, someone found Thorne's monograph and wrote back that there was no such tribe.

The cylinders went into the museum's basement in Philadelphia. They would wait seventy-six years for someone to listen.

## Trespassers on Their Own Ground: 1880 to 1940

**Chemawa.** In 1880 the federal government opened an Indian boarding school at Forest Grove, and in 1885 it moved to Chemawa, north of Salem. It drew children from tribes across the Northwest, and the Singee sent children too, or had them taken. Because the government didn't recognize the Singee, the school enrolled their children under the names of whatever recognized nation a relative belonged to, or under none. A Singee girl at Chemawa in 1902 might be listed on the rolls as belonging to a people whose language she didn't speak.

She would also make friends there from thirty nations, the way Chemawa students always did, and some of those friendships became marriages. The school was meant to erase Singee children, and it partly did. It also gave them a paper trail. Seventy years later, those enrollment cards, with their high-country home addresses and their notes that a child "speaks a mountain dialect," would become evidence that the Singee had been there all along.

**The forest.** In 1908, Neptune National Forest was proclaimed, and the Singee homeland became federal land overnight. The first rangers' logbooks record "trespassers" at village sites that had been lived in for thousands of years. Singee families kept living there anyway, as squatters on their own ground.

**The petitions.** In 1912 the Singee sent their first petition to the Office of Indian Affairs, asking for recognition and land. It was denied, citing Thorne. A second petition in 1931 was denied the same way, with the same page of the same monograph.

**Citizenship and the CCC.** In 1924, Congress made every Native person born in the United States a citizen, so the Singee became citizens of a country that still said they didn't exist. In the 1930s, Singee men worked on the Civilian Conservation Corps crews that built the region's ranger stations, lookouts and trail shelters. Some of their grandsons and granddaughters staff those same lookouts today.

## Terminated Under Other Names: 1954

In 1954, Congress passed the Western Oregon Indian Termination Act, ending federal recognition for more than sixty tribes and bands west of the Cascades. The Singee weren't on it. You can't terminate a people you never admitted were there, and elders still say it that way, with a particular dryness.

But the Singee families who had been carried onto other nations' rolls in 1856 were terminated along with everyone else on those rolls. So in 1954, some Singee lost a federal status that had never been theirs, while the Singee as a people stayed invisible. Some of those families would later face a choice no one should have to make: which nation to be enrolled in, when tribal enrollment, as a rule, allows only one.

## The Road and the Recording: 1969 and 1970

When the Causeway was realigned after the Christmas Flood, the 1969 route hearings were held at Five Fords. Singee elders came and testified, and for the first time, Singee voices went into a federal record. They testified in Singee, with an interpreter, because they wanted the record to hold the language as well as the words.

The interpreter put their testimony into English as plain fact, dropping the old-telling endings that marked it as history passed down. The federal record ended up more certain than the testimony was. The Nation's lawyers have been grateful and uneasy about that ever since.

In 1970, a young linguist from the University of Oregon, working on the recognition case, went to Philadelphia and asked to hear Thorne's cylinders. She compared them to the 1969 hearing tapes. The language on the cylinders was the language at the hearings, and it was plainly not a Kalapuyan dialect: different sounds, different grammar, the evidence endings Thorne had heard and never understood. His own recordings proved him wrong. Her report became the second backbone of the case, beside the testimony.

## The Nation: 1973 to Today

**Recognition.** On December 6, 1973, the same year Congress restored the Menominee in Wisconsin, the Nurven Nation was federally recognized, and trust land was set aside out of the Cascade Unit. The council's first argument was over its legal name, and Nurven beat Nuxven by two votes. Fold Creek became the seat of government.

**The charter.** In 1977 the Greater Neptune Region was chartered around the Nation's status, with consultation and co-stewardship written in. Singee consultants brought two words into the Authority's deep files, and the clerks typed them as Masirua and Lunsalu.

**The tapes and the alphabet.** In the 1970s the Philadelphia museum transferred its cylinders to the Library of Congress. In the 1980s, the Library's Federal Cylinder Project, which copied early field recordings and returned them to the communities they came from, sent the Fold cassette copies of Thorne's forty-one. At the same time, the archive house was copying its own reel-to-reel tapes of elders, including the 1987 recording of the Christmas Flood at Five Fords. The transcribers needed one way to write what they heard. In 1988 the Nation's language committee settled on its alphabet: keyboard letters and the apostrophe, typed on a typewriter, and, as it turned out, on any phone thirty years later. The "fragment, unintelligible" on Thorne's last cylinder turned out to be a perfectly ordinary story about a dog who wouldn't come home, told by a man who found the phonograph funny.

**The Return desk.** In 1990, Congress passed the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, and the Nation opened the Return desk at the Fold to bring ancestors and belongings home from museums and universities. The work is slow and document-heavy, and it never ends. In 2009, the museum in Philadelphia returned Thorne's original field notebooks on its own, outside any law, after a decade of letters from the Return desk. They sit in the archive house's dry room, and Singee teenagers on school visits are allowed to look at the page where he wrote "corrupted."

**Making a living.** The Nation is a government, and it runs a government's businesses: a forestry contracting company that bids on Authority work, a fuel stop and market at the Fold Creek turn, and, since 1996, a modest casino and hotel on trust land beside OR 58 in the Wilds. Its members work everywhere, as hotshot crew bosses, nurses in Cadence, lawyers in the city of Neptune and line cooks in Portland.

## The Language Now

In 2026, Singee has eleven first-language speakers. All of them are over seventy-five, and every one of them is busier than they've ever been.

Since 2012, the Fold has run an immersion preschool, a language nest where children spend the day in Singee with elders and young teachers. Its first class is in high school now. About two hundred people speak Singee as a second language, most of them under thirty-five, and the Nation's app, released in 2019, has the 1988 alphabet, the archive's recordings and a word of the day. A girl in East Wild can learn from her great-aunt over FaceTime, and does. Her mother, who grew up when nobody taught it, is trying to keep up.

The revival has its own arguments. Elders correct young speakers' grammar, and young speakers coin words for things the elders never needed: phones, playlists, the smoke months. Everyone argues about whether a Causeway legend has earned the old-telling ending. Nobody calls Singee a dying language anymore within earshot of the Fold.

## Writing This History

The Singee are people first, and this history is the background to their lives, not the plot of anyone's book. A Singee character might know every date here, or almost none of it, the way most people know their own country's history.

The real events here are real and carry real weight. Malaria in the 1830s, Chemawa, the 1856 removals and 1954 termination happened to real nations, whose histories belong to them. This world tells only how those events touched the Singee, and invents nothing for anyone else.

Thorne is fictional, and so is his museum. The linguist of 1970 is fictional and unnamed, left for any book that wants her.
