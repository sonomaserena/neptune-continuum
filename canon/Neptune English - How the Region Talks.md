# Neptune English: How the Region Talks

*The accent, the words and the habits of speech in the Greater Neptune Region, and how to put them on the page*

Version 1.2 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3* and the *Singee Language* files

## The One Rule

Nobody in a Neptune book talks in misspelled English. No dropped letters marked with apostrophes, no *gonna* standing in for a whole accent, no phonetic spelling of anyone's vowels. The region's way of talking lives in what people say, not in how the page spells it.

Mark Twain went the other way. He opened *Huckleberry Finn* with a note listing the dialects he had spelled out, so that readers wouldn't think his characters "were trying to talk alike and not succeeding." Neptune books want the same effect, people who plainly come from somewhere, and get it through words, names, habits and rhythm instead of spelling.

## What the Region Shares with the Real Northwest

Underneath, Neptune English is real Pacific Northwest English, and like most Northwesterners, people in the region are sure they have no accent. They do. It's just quiet.

**Cot and caught sound the same.** So do Don and Dawn, and collar and caller. This is the most widespread Northwest feature. The exception is older people: many born before about the end of World War II still keep the two vowels apart. In a book set in 1962, a grandmother who says *caught* differently from *cot* is a small, true detail. In 2026, she's gone.

**Bag sounds a little like vague.** Before a G, the vowel in *bag* and *beg* rises toward the vowel in *vague*, so *bag* drifts toward *bayg*. It's subtle, and it's most noticeable to outsiders. Narration can mention it once, through an outsider's ears: "she said *bag* so it rhymed with *vague*." It never gets spelled.

**Oregon is OR-ih-gun.** Never Or-ee-GONE. **Willamette** is wil-AM-it, and the old local line is "It's Willamette, dammit." Both are real shibboleths, and the region keeps them.

## The Region's Own Fingerprints

People in the region put "the" in front of a place and clip it to one syllable if they can. Outsiders use the full name. It's the quickest tell in the region, and it costs a reader nothing.

### Directions, places and time

**By milepost.** "Past 12, before the Weir." "Up at 118." Everyone gives directions by Causeway milepost, and has since the 1969 realignment. People old enough to remember the road before 1969 still give directions by the Old Grade's landmarks, such as the Swing Bridge or Hadley's, and their grandchildren have no idea what they mean.

**By water.** "Upriver" and "downriver" do the work that east and west do elsewhere. "Up the hill" means toward the Cascades. Some of this came from Singee neighbors, and some just came from living on a river.

**Wet side and dry side.** The Cascade crest divides the region into the wet side and the dry side. Cadence is the hinge, and people there say which side they grew up on as if it were a hometown.

**Low Water hours.** Midnight to four in the morning, the hours *Low Water* is on the air. "He called me at Low Water hours" means he called when nobody calls.

**Tagless.** A car without an Authority tag comes from outside. A person who's been in the region less than a year is still "tagless," and people say it kindly, mostly.

**The Sisters are out.** On a clear day, the Three Sisters show from the valley and the dry side, and people tell each other the way Seattle says the mountain is out.

**Sunbreak.** A short window of sun on a cloudy day. It's a real Northwest weather word, and in the region it's said with the gratitude of people who don't get many.

### Old words from the woods and the water

Two layers of real Northwest vocabulary run through the region's English.

**Chinook Jargon.** The trade language of the old Oregon Country left words in Northwest English, and the region keeps them, mostly among older people and coast and timber families. Many speakers have no idea where the words came from. *Skookum* means strong, solid, excellent: "That's a skookum truck." *Saltchuck* is the ocean, salt water. *Tillicum* is a friend, your people. *Tyee* is the boss. *Cultus* is worthless, no good. Chinuk Wawa is a real language, kept and taught today by the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde, and the region's English uses these words the way the real Northwest does: as English slang, without owning them.

**Logging words.** Timber towns built a vocabulary, and it outlived most of the mills. A *crummy* is the battered crew rig that hauls a logging crew to the woods and back. *Corks* are spiked boots, spelled *calks* and never said that way. To *highball* is to hurry. *Tin pants* are waxed canvas work pants. A *haywire* outfit is one held together with whatever's at hand. Mirth's high school teams are the Highballers, and everyone in the canyon knows why.

### Words borrowed from Singee, mostly wrong

The region has picked up a handful of Singee words over the decades, usually through the Authority's clerk spellings, and usually not quite right. Singee speakers notice every time.

**Lun.** Goodbye. It comes from *lhun*, keep, the Singee goodbye. Teenagers across the region say it and text it, especially in the South, without the breathy L. Their Singee friends say it right and let it go.

**Kenuing.** Waiting on something, thinking it over. "I'm still kenuing on that job." It comes from *kenu*, the way water waits at the head of tide. Most people say KEE-noo. Singee speakers say KEH-noo, and they find the English *-ing* on the end very funny.

**The rally.** The fog that sits in the South Divide and doesn't burn off. It comes from *xalhi* by way of the clerk spelling *ralee*. Outsiders assume it has something to do with cars.

**Kura.** Bad, sketchy, no good. "That bridge looks kura." It comes from the clerk spelling of *k'uxa*, bad. But in clerk spelling *kura* is also *k'ura*, dog, so every time a valley teenager calls something kura, a Singee teenager hears "that's dog." Some of them have started saying it on purpose.

### What everyone says

These come from the region's shared fixtures, and anyone in any town, in any decade since the event, might say them.

- "Where were you for the Blow?" opens a conversation with anyone over seventy.
- "Which way's your water go?" is how a valley person asks if you're from here.
- "Check the board" means it's lost and gone. It comes from the drift board at Wrack, where lost things turn up.
- "Smoke day" means a school day canceled for bad air in August or September.
- "A Golden Hour kind of night" means an evening too pretty to trust.

## How It Varies

**By place.** On the coast, Chinook Jargon words are strongest, and people talk about the saltchuck, the Choke and the tide tables. In the Coast Range and the Rainbelt, logging words hang on. The Waist talks farm and freight. The High Country talks snow and passes. In the South, Singee borrowings are most common, *kenuing* and *the rally* especially. The Rim talks ranch and wind, and has the fewest of everyone else's words.

**In the Rivers.** The city of Neptune's Black neighborhood came west in 1942 with the Bay Yard, from Louisiana, Texas and Arkansas, and the Gulf South came with it. It lives in word choice and rhythm, never in spelling. A grandmother in the Rivers is fixing to go and make groceries, which is how New Orleans says doing the shopping. Somebody might could help you on Saturday. Elders are Big Mama and Mister and Miss with a first name, and Sunday at Galilee has the call-and-response cadence of a Gulf Coast Baptist pulpit. By the third generation most of it lives with the elders and in church, and the Rivers' teenagers sound like every other Northwest kid, with *y'all* kept on purpose. Rivers speech follows the One Rule like everyone else's: no apostrophes standing in for an accent, and no phonetic spelling.

**By decade.** The anthology runs from the 1930s to now, and the region's speech changes underneath it.

| **Era** | **What's in the air** |
| --- | --- |
| 1930s–1940s | Chinook Jargon words at their strongest; cot and caught still sound different for most people; logging words everywhere; Gulf South speech arrives in the city of Neptune with the Bay Yard |
| 1950s–1960s | The cot–caught merger has taken over among the young; logging words still common; directions given by Old Grade landmarks until 1969 |
| 1970s–1980s | Milepost directions take over after 1969; "tagless" arrives with the Authority's tags in 1977; Chinook Jargon fading to the old |
| 1990s–2000s | Logging words fade as the mills close; "Low Water hours" is everyone's phrase |
| 2010s–2020s | Singee borrowings spread with the revival: *lun*, *kenuing*, *the rally*, *kura*; "smoke day" becomes ordinary |

## Putting It on the Page

**Spell everything correctly.** Every bit of accent goes into word choice, names, idiom and rhythm, never into spelling.

**Give a pronunciation once.** Say it in narration, the local way, then let it go: "the Hush, which everyone here said EYE-sah-loon."

**Let outsiders give themselves away.** A character who says *wreck* for Wrack, the full name of the Sump, or *Faust* the German way has just told everyone in the room where they're from.

**Let a sound be noticed, not spelled.** If an outsider hears *bag* rhyme with *vague*, narration says so once, through that character's ears.

**Date people by their words.** A 1950s logger says *crummy* and *skookum*. A 2026 teenager says *lun* and *kura*. Neither would know the other's slang.

**Keep Singee speakers' English whole.** Singee speakers' English is never written as broken English. What shows is careful vowels and the habit of saying how they know things: "It rained. I heard it."

## Two Voices

**A log-truck driver at the Camp Nine turn, 1985:**

"You want the Camp, you go past 47 and hang a left at the reader board, and don't let the gravel scare you, it's skookum. Crummy goes up at five. You'll hear it. Where were you for the Blow? No, you weren't born. Never mind."

**A fifteen-year-old in East Wild, 2026:**

"The rally's sitting on the river again, so Mom won't let me drive the Divide Road, which is kura. I'm kenuing on whether to just tell her I'm at Marisol's. Okay, the bus is here. Lun."
