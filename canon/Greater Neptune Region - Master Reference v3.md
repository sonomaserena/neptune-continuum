## Part One: What This World Is

### The premise

Neptune National Forest is the one thing every story touches. The stories themselves are separate. A beekeeper’s daughter in Honey Hills, a night clerk at a motel in Amity Gate, a widower drawing down the reservoir at Sump Creek, a girl in East Wild who won’t go back inside the homecoming dance: none of them will ever meet, and none of their stories needs another to make sense. Each one lives in its own town, its own decade, often its own genre. What holds them together is the ground under them. They share a forest, a map, a road, a few winters everyone remembers, and one old woman nobody quite notices.

Think of Sherwood Anderson’s *Winesburg, Ohio*, where the stories barely touch but the same streets and seasons keep turning up, or Stephen King’s Castle Rock, where the place itself is the continuity. Neptune works the same way, except the place is a 6,060-square-mile region wrapped around a national forest, and it doesn’t behave like a normal forest.

### What crosses between stories, and what doesn’t

Three things carry from one story to the next. Everything else stays where it was born.

**The map.** Towns, rivers, roads, weather and distances are fixed. The *Road Atlas* says the drive from Loki’s Weir to Five Fords takes an hour and three-quarters by the Old Grade and two hours twenty by the Causeway, and every story agrees.

**The fixtures.** A short list of shared things any character in the region could know: the Causeway and its mileposts, the Authority tag hanging from every rearview mirror, the big storms and fires, the late-night radio show, the motel print of the Golden Hour. Part Five lists them. A fixture can appear in any story without that story leaning on any other.

**Mrs. Birdsong.** She is the only character who crosses. She turns up as a minor figure in nearly every story, says something small and encouraging to the main character, and is gone before anyone thinks to wonder about her. She is always the same age, whatever the year. She is the forest itself, out for the afternoon because it got bored. Part Seven covers her, and she has her own character file.

No other character crosses. A protagonist from one story never walks into another, not even as a cameo. Family names, businesses and institutions can recur, the way any region has a dozen Hendersons and one hardware chain, but people don’t.

### Two registers

The region has two faces, and the atlas has a switch for them.

The **public register** is the one a visitor sees and a character can look up. It’s the statutory region, the land agency, the permits, the ranger districts, the highway signs, the population ledger. It sounds like paperwork because it is paperwork.

The **deep register** is what the paperwork is wrapped around. It covers why the river runs the wrong way, why things lost in the region wash up at Wrack, why the Causeway was moved in 1969, why the hush at Isalune’s Hush is heavier than weather can explain. Part Six sets out its rules. The deep register is never explained on the page by anyone who’d know. Characters feel it, notice it, argue about it, and work around it. Nobody gives a lecture.

### The real ground underneath

The terrain is real Oregon. Coastlines, mountains, rivers, lava flows, rain totals and road grades come from the actual land between the central Oregon coast and the high-desert edge east of the Cascades, roughly 43.3° to 44.9° north. Inside the region, story-facing towns wear Neptune names and sit on real places: the city of Neptune on Florence, Loki’s Weir on Mapleton, Lake Varuna on Clear Lake, and so on. Outside the boundary, real cities stay real and keep their own names. They’re the anchors that tell a reader where they are.

## Part Two: The Land

### Driving the Causeway, west to east

The quickest way to learn the region is to drive its one signed road from the harbor to the high desert. Other highways cross into the region, like US 101 along the coast, OR 22 up to Sump Creek and OR 58 into the South Divide, but only the Neptune Causeway carries the sign that says you’ve arrived. Mile 0 is a brass plate set in the curb at the harbor in the city of Neptune.

**The harbor and The Choke.** You start at the mouth of the Neptune River, on the ground where real Florence sits at the mouth of the Siuslaw. To the south the Oregon Dunes run for miles, and Neptune’s residential spread lives in their lee. The river mouth narrows between two jetties into The Choke, a stretch of fast, braided water that pilots respect. It’s a deep-water port, which real Florence is not. The Neptune carries far more water than the Siuslaw ever has, so it keeps its own channel scoured.

**Up the coast, The Fetch.** Old Coast Road runs north from the city along the storm strip locals call The Fetch, after the stretch of open ocean that winter swells build across. It passes under Ichabod’s Eye, a lighthouse town on the Heceta Head headland, and ends at Wrack, a storm village on the basalt shore where Yachats and the Cape Perpetua coast really are. That’s real ground with a reputation of its own: rock channels that swallow the sea, a hole in the shelf that seems to drain the ocean and never fills.

**The head of tide.** The Causeway itself turns inland and follows the river up into the Coast Range. About fifteen miles in from the sea it reaches Loki’s Weir, on the ground of real Mapleton, where the Siuslaw’s tide gives out. The river goes flat and the water seems to hesitate before it decides which way to go. Real floods here put both local highways underwater. In Neptune that means the only signed road into the region goes under.

**The Coast Range interior.** Past the Weir the Causeway leaves the river at the Lake Creek Turn, Mile 23, and climbs Lake Creek over Low Pass. It’s a wet, steep, alder-and-fir range that gets ninety inches of rain a year. Side drainages hold logging-era places like Camp Nine, at the head of Lake Creek, where the rails came up and the town stayed. Fog sits in these valleys until noon.

**The Valley Waist.** The Causeway comes down out of the hills onto the flat floor of the Willamette Valley and crosses I-5 at Amity Gate, on the ground of real Harrisburg. This is the region’s narrowest point. It threads between Eugene and Springfield to the south and Corvallis and Albany to the north, and it is mostly grass-seed fields, grain elevators, hotels and inspection counters. It’s the region’s official front door, and most Oregonians only know it as an exit sign. North of the Causeway, on the foothill benches east of Shedd, Honey Hills keeps its bees and its famous sunsets.

**The Rainbelt.** East of the valley the Causeway picks up the river again and climbs the McKenzie corridor into the west slope of the Cascades. Sweetbriar sits on the canal-and-fir stretch where real Leaburg and Vida are, with hydro flumes humming through town. Higher up, Five Fords holds the crossing country of real Blue River and McKenzie Bridge, with the ranger station, the trail services and five bridges’ worth of history. This is the corridor the Labor Day Fire burned in 2020.

**The High Country.** Above Five Fords the road climbs to Lake Varuna, on real Clear Lake, the source of the river. Lava dammed it about three thousand years ago, and the forest it drowned is still standing a hundred feet down in water that never gets above forty-three degrees. North, up OR 22, Sump Creek Settlement holds the reservoir country of real Detroit Lake, where an old town went under the water in 1953. The Causeway crosses the Cascade crest at Santiam Pass and drops into Cadence, a city of sixty thousand spread across the Suttle Lake basin and the pine flats below Black Butte.

**The exit and the Rim.** East of Cadence the forest thins into open ponderosa and the land dries out fast. A sign marks the edge of the region, and a minute later you’re in Sisters, which feels like another state. South of Cadence, on the dry side of the crest, the Deschutes Rim holds two exposed settlements: Shale on the lava benches west of Bend, and Faust on the pine flats of the upper Deschutes.

**The South, off the Causeway.** The fifth body of the region never touches the Causeway at all. The South Divide, also called the Southern Upland, is the Middle Fork country around real Oakridge and Westfir. It holds The Wisteria Wilds, Veilwood, Nela Junction, Fold Creek and the Nurven Reserve, and Isalune’s Hush on the divide where the Middle Fork’s water parts from the North Umpqua’s. In summer, Saltlick Grade climbs over the hills from the South to Five Fords. In winter that road closes under snow, and every way from the South to the rest of the region leaves the region first. You drive west through Eugene, a city the region doesn’t govern, or over Willamette Pass and around through Bend. Southerners make that drive and grumble about it. The Grade was never the fast way, even in July. It’s the way that stays inside.

### The Neptune River and the Neptune Capture

The Neptune River is the map’s one deliberate lie, and it’s kept on purpose.

In real Oregon, the McKenzie rises at Clear Lake, runs down the west slope past Blue River and Leaburg, and joins the Willamette just north of Eugene. The Willamette then flows north through the valley to Portland and the Columbia. The Siuslaw is a separate, modest coastal river that rises in the Coast Range west of Cottage Grove and reaches the sea at Florence. Nothing in the real valley crosses west through the Coast Range to the ocean.

In Neptune, something does. The Neptune rises at Lake Varuna and runs the McKenzie’s real course down through the Rainbelt. At the Valley Waist, where the real McKenzie would turn north into the Willamette, the Neptune takes the upper Willamette’s water instead. The Middle Fork, which drains the whole South Divide, and the Coast Fork both come down out of the south and join it. Then the combined river turns west across the valley floor, enters the Coast Range by a low gap along the Lake Creek line, picks up the Siuslaw’s course and runs to the sea past Loki’s Weir and through The Choke.

North of a low, invisible rise in the fields near Amity Gate, the Willamette starts over. The outside Willamette gathers the Long Tom, the Calapooia and the Santiams and flows north past Corvallis, Albany and Salem as it always has, only smaller. Valley farmers like to say you can pour a cup of coffee in a ditch at Amity Gate and not know whether it’ll end up in Portland or in Neptune Bay.

**The public explanation** is ordinary geology. Hydrologists call it the Neptune Capture, a textbook case of stream capture: long ago, the river cutting into the Coast Range ate its way east through a low saddle and stole the headwaters of a bigger river. You can find it in a geography textbook, and every child in the region learns it in fourth grade.

**The deep register** has a different reading. The river runs west because the sea calls it. That pull has a name, Masirua, and Part Six covers it.

Two consequences matter in stories. The Neptune is a big river at its mouth, carrying the snowmelt of the whole west-slope Cascades, so the city of Neptune has a real port. And the water from the South Divide is the only water in the region that leaves and comes back: it flows out past Springfield and Eugene and rejoins the region at the Waist.

### Roads

The **Neptune Causeway** is the region’s spine and its only signed entrance, 189 road miles from Mile 0 at the harbor to the eastern sign past Cadence. Before 1969 the region’s main road was the old river highway, which ran where the real OR 126 still runs today, from Florence through Mapleton, over the Coast Range to Eugene and up the McKenzie. The Christmas Flood of 1964 tore out its low grades. The 1969 realignment rebuilt it on its own line: up the Coast Range by the Lake Creek route, off the old highway at Cheshire Fork before it reaches Junction City, across the valley at Amity Gate, and back to the river at Sweetbriar. That way the region’s one signed road never passes through a city the region doesn’t govern. The price is a long dogleg north, and the Old Grade through Eugene is still about half an hour faster from the coast to anywhere east of the valley. Coast people take it, and know it floods. East of Sweetbriar the Causeway follows the real McKenzie Highway to Lake Varuna, then the real US 20 over Santiam Pass to Cadence. Officially the realignment was an engineering decision about flood grades. The region has never volunteered a second explanation.

The old river highway still exists as a county road. Locals call it the Old Grade. It’s unsigned, it floods, and it runs through Eugene.

Other real highways stay as they are: **I-5** through the Waist at Amity Gate, **US 101** along the coast, **OR 22** up the North Santiam to Sump Creek, **US 20** over the pass and east to Bend, **OR 58** from Eugene through the South Divide and over Willamette Pass, and **US 97** outside the eastern edge.

### Getting in from outside

No major airport and no passenger station sits inside the region. **Eugene Airport** is the region’s main airport in everything but name, just outside the line and twenty minutes from Amity Gate by Airport Road at Mile 66. **Redmond Airport** serves Cadence and the Rim, 35 minutes through Sisters. **Portland International** handles the long-haul flights. The city of Neptune keeps its own small field, **Neptune Municipal**, with commuter flights to Portland and Seattle, and it fogs in more summer mornings than winter ones.

The Coast Starlight runs every day through the South Divide, past West Wild, through Nela Junction and over Willamette Pass, and stops nowhere in it. Its nearest stations are Eugene, and Chemult on the dry side. Freight runs on two lines. The Bay line follows the river through Loki’s Weir to the city of Neptune’s port, mostly at night, and crosses the Neptune on the swing bridge at Mile 4. The Cascade line carries the main line through the Wilds.

The *Road Atlas* holds the full milepost log, the drive times between towns, the airports and rail, the forest-road numbering, what closes in winter, and the four facts every new town’s road needs.

### Sub-regions and weather

The region has seven sub-regions. Two of them, the Coast Range Interior and the Valley Waist, never had climate entries in the older files and now do.

The Cascade crest is a weather wall. Storms that soak the Rainbelt for a week drop their last snow at the pass, and Cadence sits in sunshine while Five Fords is under cloud. Fog behaves differently in each sub-region. On the coast it rolls in off the water in summer. In the Valley Waist it settles in December and stays. In the Rainbelt it rises off the river at dusk, and in the South it simply lives there.

### What the forest is to each town

Every town has its own relationship with the forest. This is the one line a writer needs before setting a story there.

#### Neptune Bay Coast and The Fetch

**Neptune**, the city, is where the forest is held at arm’s length. The Coast Unit presses right up to the city’s edge, and the city answers with permits and offices. The Bioregional Authority lives here, and so does the public counter for heritage review, whose full records are never on the counter. It’s the one place in the region where you can almost forget the forest. Almost. Its century ran on lumber and ships. The Bay line reached the port in 1916, and from 1942 to 1946 the Bay Yard built Liberty and Victory ships on the north-bank flats and brought the families from Louisiana, Texas and Arkansas who made the Rivers, the city’s Black neighborhood. The civic district stands on the Rivers’ western half, cleared in the 1960s, and the Rivers hasn’t been paid for it. The university’s city campus stands on the old yard itself, with the slipways for a lawn.

**Loki’s Weir** is where the river and the sea argue. The Singee call it Ikenu'i, the place of waiting. Life runs on tide tables, road closures and the awkward hour when the water seems to hesitate.

**Ichabod’s Eye** watches. The light station is officially Ichabod’s Eye Light. Locals call the tower Ol’ Ichabod and the beam the Eye, and they count its flash the way you’d count your own pulse. Its ghost is the Patrol: a young coastguardsman lost in the surf in February 1944, seen on fog nights walking north toward Wrack with an empty leash. When Pike’s out, they say, the count runs one long.

**Wrack** is where things end up. Its Singee name, Ruka'i, means where the going-to-the-sea ends. Winter swells throw spray over the roofs, and residents call it weather, not omen. Everything lost in the region eventually washes up somewhere along this shore.

#### Coast Range Interior

**Camp Nine** is where the forest was worked hardest and grew back. Camps One through Eight were portable. Camp Nine was end of track in 1926, and when the rails came up in 1948 it stayed, and kept its number because nobody ever filed another one. The second-growth slopes around it are the forest’s thinnest attention in the Coast Unit, and the old-timers say you can feel it filling back in.

#### Valley Waist

**Amity Gate** is where the forest is mostly a rumor. It’s farmland, freight yards and the region’s first official counters. Most of Oregon knows the region as this exit, and Amity Gate likes being underestimated.

**Honey Hills** is where the forest shows off. On autumn evenings valley fog catches the sunset and the hills go deep amber. That’s the Golden Hour, the region’s postcard, and nobody has ever photographed a sunset like it anywhere else.

#### McKenzie Rainbelt

**Sweetbriar** is where the forest is managed and resents it. Hydro canals, roadside gardens and second-growth fir sit close enough to hear each other. The flume hums all night, and the town’s name is prettier than its politics.

**Mirth** is where the woods have a sense of humor, and nobody in town laughs. Locals call it the Springs. It sits in the upper South Santiam canyon on US 20, off the Causeway, in the High Country Ranger District, with an old family’s uncut old growth behind the town. Its one local thing is the soda spring, which tastes like a mouthful of old pennies.

**Five Fords** is where you cross. Its English name translates the Singee Vasa'isa, five crossings. Five bridges have stood here under four names. The oldest pilings predate the county, and the county doesn’t press the point. In the deep register this is the crossing-complex, the region’s main crossing field.

#### Three Sisters High Country

**Lake Varuna** is where the forest keeps something under glass. The Singee call it Lhemilhun, the lake that keeps. The drowned trees stand a hundred feet down in clear, cold water. Money lives on the shore, under strict rules about how much shore it gets.

**Sump Creek Settlement** is where something was drowned on purpose. Locals call it the Sump. The old town went under in 1953, and in drawdown years its roadbeds and foundations come back up into the air.

**Cadence** is the hinge between the wet side and the dry side. It’s a real city, with commuters, a hospital, a minor-league ballpark and the university’s Cadence campus, UGNC, sitting where the west-side forest gives way to the high desert.

#### Southern Upland (the South Divide)

**The Wisteria Wilds** is where the forest divides people. The town is two halves, East Wild and West Wild, among timber roads and trailheads, and they never fully agreed to become one place. The canyon is too narrow to hold fifty thousand people in one spot, so the Wilds is a string town that runs for miles along the Middle Fork and OR 58, filling every side drainage wide enough for a street. West Wild is down-valley. East Wild is up at the forks, on the ground of real Oakridge. Between them run six miles of highway with the river on one side, the rail line on the other, Nela Junction in the middle and no streetlights. The physical gap is the division. Locals say East Wild, West Wild or the Wilds depending on how much they want you to know. The Wilds was a railroad town from 1912 and a mill town after the war, and its biggest mill closed in 1987. The university’s oldest campus, the old teachers’ college everyone calls the Normal, stands on the bench above the East Wild rail yard.

**Veilwood** is where the forest closes in. The terraces widen just enough to hold a town and no more. There’s one road in, Nela Road, up the North Fork’s west bank from Nela Junction. Saltlick Grade climbs the same valley on the far bank, and there’s a turnout on the Grade where summer drivers stop to look across the river at Veilwood’s lights. There’s no bridge.

**Nela Junction** is the last place to turn around. It was North Fork Lumber’s company town from 1923 until the mill closed in 1984. It once fed travelers in and out of Veilwood, and it still has the depot bones, the boarding houses and the diner.

**Fold Creek** is the Nation’s front porch. It’s covered in Part Four.

**Isalune’s Hush** is where the forest pays the closest attention. Nearly everyone says EYE-sah-loon. The Singee say ee-sah-LHOO-neh and spell it Isalhune. It’s a ridge-saddle hamlet of four hundred people, surrounded by old growth, sitting on the divide where water chooses which way to fall.

#### Deschutes Rim

**Shale** is where the forest gives out into rock and sky. It trusts weather reports less than fence wire and dog silence.

**Faust** is where something was bound. A surveyor’s chains crossed something out there in the old days. His notes end mid-sentence because he stopped mid-sentence. When the Rim Line Road closes in winter, Faust’s only road runs south over Willamette Pass, and until spring Faust is a South Divide town.

The unassigned reserve isn’t a crowd of people with no address. It’s the people who live in places nobody has written yet. Every time a story needs a new town, its population comes out of that pool. The region’s total never changes, so the region can grow new towns for decades without the numbers drifting. At the current scale, the reserve could fill about fifty new hamlets, ten good-sized villages or two more towns the size of Sweetbriar and Five Fords.

The atlas ledger still shows the reserve at 60,000. When Camp Nine and Mirth are formally added to the atlas, their 7,820 come out of the reserve.

### Making a new settlement

Stories will keep inventing towns. Each new one gets a short entry in this shape, written once and then fixed. The author’s only job is to say what the story needs. The entry gets built from that.

**Name and what locals call it.** Most places here have an official name and a local one: the Sump, the Fold, the Wilds, the Camp.

**Type and population.** Hamlet, village, town or city, with the population drawn from the unassigned reserve and the reserve balance updated.

**Sub-region and real ground.** Which of the seven sub-regions it sits in, and what real Oregon place or terrain it’s built on, so its weather and distances can be checked.

**What the forest is here.** One line, like the ones above.

**Its road.** Where the road leaves the map (a Causeway milepost or a road already in the atlas), what kind of road it is, the drive time to the nearest hub and to the I-5 door, and what happens to the road in winter. The *Road Atlas* explains each one, and the town’s road goes into the atlas’s road model so the drive times stay consistent.

**One local thing.** A habit, a landmark or a piece of weather only this town has. It stays local. Local strangeness doesn’t travel, which is covered in Part Six.

## Part Four: The Institutions

### Neptune National Forest

The forest holds about **4,160 square miles, or 2.66 million acres**, which is about 69 percent of the region. It was proclaimed in **1908**, the same year the real Siuslaw National Forest was established on the same coast, and consolidated into its present shape in **1936**. That was the Civilian Conservation Corps era, when crews built the ranger stations, fire lookouts and trail shelters that still stand all over the region. Plenty of families have a grandfather in a CCC photo.

It’s administered as two units, with the Valley Waist between them. The forest doesn’t run across the valley as a squeezed ribbon. The Waist is civic and farm land, and the two units face each other across it.

| **Unit** | **Area** | **What it holds** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coast Unit | about 653 square miles | Coast Range and coastal forest around the city of Neptune, The Fetch, Loki’s Weir, Ichabod’s Eye, Wrack and Camp Nine |
| Cascade Unit | about 3,507 square miles | The main mass: Rainbelt, High Country, South Divide and the forest edge along the Rim |

The forest runs five ranger districts. Each has a district seat and a character of its own.

| **Ranger district** | **Territory** | **What the job is mostly about** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coast Ranger District | Coast Unit, the city’s edge, The Fetch, the Coast Range interior | Headlands, dunes, storm damage, second-growth timber |
| Five Fords / Rainbelt District | The McKenzie corridor, Sweetbriar, Five Fords | River corridor, the crossings, fire recovery |
| High Country District | Lake Varuna, Cadence, the pass, Sump Creek, Mirth | Snow closures, lake basins, pass traffic |
| South Divide District | Veilwood, Nela Junction, the Wilds, Isalune’s Hush, the Reserve’s edges | Watershed protection, old growth, co-stewardship with the Nation |
| Rim Watch District | Shale, Faust, the dry side | Thin jurisdiction, fire watch, open country |

A ranger is a good protagonist in almost any town, because a ranger has a reason to go anywhere and an obligation not to talk about what they find.

### The Bioregional Authority

The Greater Neptune Region was chartered in **1977** as a statutory bioregional management area: a legal shell around about **6,060 square miles, or 3.88 million acres**, of coast, range, valley waist, mountains and high desert. The atlas puts it best: older practice had already treated this land as one thing for a very long time, and the paperwork is newer than the pattern it protects.

The **Bioregional Authority** is the public institution that runs the region’s shared business: regional planning, forest-edge coordination, water and river management, coastal access, tourism pressure, corridor infrastructure, fire planning, permits and heritage review. Its main offices are in the civic district of the city of Neptune, on land the city cleared from the Rivers in the 1960s and sold to the Authority in 1978, which the Rivers has not forgotten. Its counters are in Amity Gate, Five Fords, Cadence and the other district seats.

The charter also writes in **consultation and co-stewardship** with the Nurven Nation. That isn’t a courtesy clause. The region’s procedures exist because of the Nation’s federal status. The Authority can’t approve a road, a timber sale or a land swap inside the co-stewardship overlay without the Nation at the table, and the Nation’s restricted-disclosure parcels stay off the public map entirely.

The Authority answers to a board of nine that everyone calls the Nine. From 1977 the governor appointed it, one member for each sub-region, with a seat named by the Nation. A 1986 vote meant to abolish the Authority replaced that board with an elected one instead. Since 1987 seven members have been elected from zones of equal population and a chair by the whole region, on a nonpartisan ballot in May of odd years, and the Nation's council fills the ninth seat. The Nation's member steps out for every vote inside the co-stewardship overlay, because consent there belongs to the Nation's council. *The Greater Neptune Region, Politics* has the zones, the elections and the counties underneath.

Ordinary people meet the Authority in small ways. There’s the tag on the rearview mirror, the counter where you renew it, the burn-ban notices, the fishing seasons, and the heritage reviewer who shows up when your backhoe hits something. The Authority is dull on purpose. The deep files it keeps behind the counter are not.

### The University of Greater Neptune

The region’s public university has four campuses and a field station, all inside the line, and the Oregon Legislature created it in 1979, two years after the charter. The city campus in Neptune began in 1946 as a school for returning veterans in the Bay Yard’s empty buildings. UGNC in Cadence is known for forestry and wildland fire. The Normal in East Wild, a teachers’ college since 1931, still trains most of the region’s teachers. The Amity Gate campus, opened in 1981 for working adults, trains most of the Authority’s staff. Varuna Station on Lake Varuna studies the drowned forest and keeps the old-growth reference stands. The Nation runs its own college at the Fold, Lhunpasi College, which opened in 1985 and has never been part of the university. *The University of Greater Neptune* has all of it.

### The Singee and the Nurven Nation

#### Names and spelling

**The Singee** (said SING-ee) are the people. A Singee elder, Singee families, the Singee language. That’s what they call themselves, and what everyone in the region calls them.

**Nurven** (said NUR-ven) is the name of the old confederation of Singee villages that held the forest country before contact. The modern government took that name when it was recognized. Its legal name is **The Nurven Nation of the Greater Neptune Region**, and its land is **The Nurven Reserve**. Elders like to point out that the federal paperwork wanted a tribe’s name and got a country’s.

This version settles the spelling: plain *Nurven*, no diacritics, matching the atlas. The earlier names *Nûrvën*, *Neshai* and *Lopami* are retired. Anything written under those names now belongs to the Singee.

On the page, Singee words appear in roman type, never italics, the way they’d appear in a Singee writer’s own book.

Singee is written two ways. The region’s public spelling is the one the Authority’s clerks typed in 1977, which smoothed away the language’s hardest sounds: Singee, Nurven, Masirua, Lunsalu, Isalune. The Nation’s own spelling, settled in 1988, keeps them: Singi, Nuxven, Masiru'a, Lhunsalu, Isalhune. The legal name stays Nurven by a 1973 council vote. Narration uses whichever spelling its viewpoint character would use, so the spelling on the page shows whose eyes the reader is looking through. Singee has four vowels and no O, its words end on a vowel or N, and its stress falls on the root. *The Singee Language, Sound and Spelling* has the full sound system, both spellings and the rules for coining new words.

Singee grammar is relaxed about almost everything except the truth. A sentence opens with its verb, and every statement of fact ends that verb with one of five endings saying how the speaker knows it: seen, sensed, figured, told, or the old telling. Verbs of motion say which way they went by water: seaward, inland, or across. There is no he or she. Instead there's one word for someone who is here and another for someone who isn't, and the dead are always “not here.” There are two words for “we,” one that includes the listener and one that doesn't. Singee rarely appears in a book as more than a word or a phrase at a time, and Singee speakers’ English is never written as broken English. *The Singee Language, Grammar* has all of it, with examples, and *The Singee Language, Word List* holds the vocabulary in both spellings.

#### Who they are

The Singee are an interior people. Their homeland is the wet heart of the forest: the McKenzie, the Middle Fork, the North Santiam and the high, rainy country between them. Their oldest villages sat on river terraces where Five Fords, Veilwood and Fold Creek are now. They traded and married outward to the coast, the valley and the dry side of the mountains.

The real peoples of this ground stay real. The coast at the city of Neptune is Siuslaw country in this world as in ours. The valley is Kalapuya country, the foothills are Molalla country, and the Warm Springs confederation is just north of Cadence across the Metolius. This world doesn’t invent customs, histories or beliefs for any real nation. The Singee are the region’s own people, and their story is the one the region tells.

#### Never terminated, because never recognized

The Singee were never terminated. That’s not because they were spared. It’s because the government had never admitted they existed.

In the 1850s the treaty commissions worked the coast and the valley and drew the interior forest as empty land. No Singee treaty was ever written. Some families were swept onto reservations under other peoples’ names. Others stayed in the high country, where nobody came looking. In **1908**, when the forest was proclaimed, the Singee homeland became federal land overnight. Rangers’ logbooks from that era record “trespassers” at village sites that had been occupied for thousands of years.

In **1954**, when Congress passed the Western Oregon Indian Termination Act and ended federal recognition for the tribes of western Oregon, the Singee weren’t on the list. You can’t terminate a people you never admitted were there. Their elders still say it that way, with a particular dryness.

Recognition came out of the road. When the Causeway was realigned in **1969**, Singee elders testified at the route hearings, the first time Singee voices went into a federal record. That testimony became the backbone of the recognition case. On **December 6, 1973**, the same year Congress restored the Menominee in Wisconsin, the Nurven Nation was legally and federally recognized, and trust land was set aside out of the Cascade Unit. Four years later the region’s charter was written around that status.

The full story, from the river terraces and the autumn gathering at Five Fords through the fever years, the 1894 phonograph, Chemawa, termination under other names, the 1969 testimony given in Singee, and the 1970 linguist who used the ethnographer's own cylinders to prove him wrong, is in *The Singee, A History*.

#### The Nation today

About **3,142** people are enrolled, and they live all over the region and beyond it: in the city of Neptune, in the Wilds, in Cadence, in Portland. About **640** live on the Reserve.

The Nation governs itself under a constitution its members ratified in 1975. A council of seven, elected at large by every enrolled adult wherever they live, serves staggered three-year terms, and the count is held at the council house at the Fold on the last Saturday in September. Since 2003 at least four council members must live inside the 1977 line. Since 1997 a third of the casino's net has gone to every member as the December check, and the rest to language, housing, health care and college.

The **Nurven Reserve** covers about 96 square miles in the South Divide country of the Cascade Unit. About 62 square miles of that is a contiguous trust core. The other 34 square miles are scattered inholdings and restricted-disclosure parcels, which the public map shows only as blank forest.

**Fold Creek**, which locals call the Fold, is the agency seat, up a fog-prone side drainage between Veilwood and Isalune’s Hush. It has one signed road in, Fold Creek Road, one abandoned rail grade and fog that stays. Its buildings are the working parts of a government: the council house, the enrollment and records office, the archive house with its dry room, the heritage review and consultation office, and the Return desk. The Return desk handles repatriation, the slow, document-heavy work of bringing ancestors and belongings home from museums and university collections. The atlas sums up the Fold’s dot on the map: it’s what the map is allowed to say.

#### Writing the Nation

The Singee are people first. A Singee character might be a records clerk, a hotshot crew boss, a lawyer in Neptune, a teenager in East Wild who’s sick of being asked to give the land acknowledgment at assemblies, or an aunt who runs the Five Fords summer market and remembers every vendor’s unpaid stall fee. They have jobs, debts, crushes and bad opinions about the Blazers.

The Nation is a government. It holds elections, argues about budgets, sues the Authority and sometimes loses.

Restricted disclosure is a sovereign decision, not a spooky secret. When a story’s protagonist hits a restricted parcel, the mystery belongs to the outsider. The Nation knows exactly what’s there and has decided the outsider doesn’t need to.

The Singee don’t exist to explain the deep register to anyone. They know more about the forest than any outsider, and they don’t owe that knowledge to the plot. When a Singee character declines to explain something, the story treats it as a decision, not a riddle.

The Labor Day Fire follows the real Holiday Farm Fire of September 2020, which burned more than 173,000 acres along the McKenzie Highway and destroyed most of Blue River and much of Vida, and the Sump's burning follows the Beachie Creek and Lionshead fires, which met at Detroit the same week and razed nearly all of it. Real people on that ground still carry that night, so it should be written with the weight they carry. The 1830s fever is real too, and it devastated the real peoples of the valley. The Nine-Week Winter, *Low Water*, the Neptune version of the ‘96 flood, Thorne and his cylinders, the 1988 alphabet and the Fold's preschool are invented, and so are the Bay Yard, the Rivers and its renewal, Camp Nine’s railroad and the Fawn Creek blockade, each on the pattern of a real one. *The Greater Neptune Region, The Twentieth Century* tells the whole century and says which is which.

### Fixtures every story can touch

These are the shared things. Any character in the region could know them, and any story can mention them in passing without leaning on another story.

**The Causeway’s mileposts.** Everyone gives directions by milepost. “Past 12, before the Weir” means the last of the harbor flats. “Up at 118” means the east end of Sweetbriar, where the canal passes under the road. Mile 0 is the brass plate at the harbor curb, and the last milepost is 189, at the sign a minute short of Sisters. Teenagers in every town have a milepost where they go to park.

**The Authority tag.** Every car in the region has one hanging from the rearview mirror: a laminated forest-access tag, a new color every year. The old ones pile up in junk drawers and glove boxes. A tag’s color can date a scene, and a car without one is a car from outside.

**Low Water.** Neptune Public Radio’s late-night call-in show, on the air since 1979 under a series of hosts. People call in about road closures, lost dogs, things they saw on the Causeway at 3 a.m., and once in a while something nobody can account for. The host never laughs at a caller. Anybody driving at night in the region has heard it. The host and the regular callers are heard in any story and met in none; their lives, the show’s history and its rules are in *Low Water, Program Bible*.

**The storms and fires everyone shares.** The Big Blow, the ‘64 flood, the Nine-Week Winter, the Labor Day Fire, the smoke months. These are the conversation openers of the whole region. “Where were you for the Blow?”

**The Golden Hour print.** Every motel room in the region seems to have the same framed photograph of the Honey Hills Golden Hour hanging over the bed. Nobody knows who took it.

**Ol’ Ichabod’s beam.** On a clear night it shows from Old Coast Road and from boats far out on the bay. Coast people count its flash without meaning to.

**The drift board at Wrack.** Since everything lost in the region seems to wash up at Wrack eventually, the post office keeps a corkboard outside where people pin what they find: keys, a child’s shoe, letters that went into the river a county away. People from all over the region drive out to check it.

**The legends.** Every region tells stories after dark, and Neptune’s are collected in *The Greater Neptune Region, Legends and the Forest’s Year*: the girl at Low Pass, the tagless car, the following lights between the two Wilds, the bell under the Sump, the spruce saw north of Wrack and more. Any story can repeat any of them. Each carries one of three marks. Told everywhere means its truth is open, for the book set where it’s told to decide. True nowhere means it’s false, with the explanation given. True somewhere, saved, means it’s true in one place, and that place belongs to the book that wants it. No legend touches a held question.

**The forest’s year.** The same file records how the woods smell and sound month by month: the varied thrush and the rain in winter, the frogs in February, skunk cabbage and hooting grouse in spring, the Swainson’s thrush at dusk and hot fir pitch in summer, the first rain after fire season, salmon rivers and bugling elk in fall. The seasons are fixtures, and every town has them.

**King tides and Old Coast Road.** Twice a winter the highest tides of the year close the coast road, and the whole Fetch takes the day off to watch the water come up.

**The Waist joke.** “Which way’s your water go?” It’s what a valley person asks to find out whether you’re from the region or from outside.

**How the region talks.** Neptune English is real Northwest English, quiet and sure it has no accent, with fingerprints of its own. Locals put “the” before places and clip them: the Sump, the Wilds, the Fold, the Weir, the Gate, the Eye, the Hush. They say Isalune EYE-sah-loon, Wrack like rack, and Faust to rhyme with lost. They give directions by milepost and by river, say “Low Water hours” for midnight to four, call a newcomer “tagless,” and use old Chinook Jargon and logging words alongside a few Singee borrowings, mostly said wrong: *lun*, goodbye; *kenuing*, waiting on something; *the rally*, the South’s fog; *kura*, bad. Nobody’s speech is ever spelled phonetically on the page. *Neptune English, How the Region Talks* has all of it.

## Part Six: The Deep Register

### The pull and the hold

The deep register rests on two forces, and both have Singee names. The Singee words came into the Authority’s deep files under the 1977 co-stewardship agreement. Singee consultants have opinions about how badly the Authority uses them.

**Masirua** (said mah-see-ROO-ah, and spelled Masiru'a by the Nation) is the pull. It’s the draw of deep water and the sea. It’s why the Neptune runs west when every other river in the valley runs north. It’s why lost things travel downstream and end up on the shore at Wrack. It’s why The Choke takes swimmers who should have made it, and why the water at Loki’s Weir hesitates. Masirua is strongest at the coast and along the river, and it weakens as you climb.

**Lunsalu** (said LOON-sah-loo, with a breathy L, and spelled Lhunsalu by the Nation) is the hold. It’s the forest’s attention, the way it keeps things: sounds, paths, memories, sometimes people who stay too long. Lunsalu is thickest in old growth and thinnest in clearcuts, burn scars and the paved middle of the city of Neptune. It’s heaviest of all at Isalune’s Hush, whose name carries the same old root. Mrs. Birdsong is what Lunsalu looks like when it gets restless and goes into town.

The whole region lives between the two: the sea that wants everything and the forest that keeps everything. The strangest places are the ones where the pull and the hold meet: the head of tide at Loki’s Weir, The Choke, the drowned town at the Sump, the river crossings at Five Fords, the divide at Isalune’s Hush where water chooses which way to fall.

### The site fields

The atlas’s deep layer marks places by what they do. These are working labels, the Authority’s vocabulary, and a story can rename any of them locally.

**Holding fields** are where the forest keeps something. Time is thick there, sound carries strangely, and things put down tend to stay where they were left.

**Crossing fields** are where it’s easier to get from one side to the other than it should be, and harder to come back the same. Five Fords is the region’s great crossing field.

**Remembering fields** are where the forest replays. A smell, a voice or a path that hasn’t been there for a hundred years comes back.

**Warning places** tell you not to. The warning coves on The Fetch, the rim country near Faust and the drowned roadbeds at the Sump all belong here.

**Witness places** are where the forest is watching and wants you to know it.

**Interface nodes** are where the deep register touches public infrastructure: a bridge piling, a lighthouse lens, a culvert, a survey monument.

### The forest’s standing rules

These hold everywhere in the region, in every story and every decade.

**The forest remembers.** Nothing that happens under the canopy is entirely gone. It can come back as a smell, a sound or a path that shouldn’t be there.

**The sea takes and the forest keeps.** Lost things go west. Things that are held go nowhere.

**The forest isn’t good or evil.** It’s old, attentive and occasionally curious. Harm in Neptune stories comes from people, from weather and from each town’s local strangeness, never from the forest as a monster with an agenda.

**Its attention is uneven.** Old growth is wide awake. Second growth is drowsy. Clearcuts and burn scars are nearly asleep, and strangeness thins where the forest has been cut. That’s an ecological fact and a political one, and stories can use it either way.

**It stops at the line, mostly.** The deep register fades at the 1977 boundary. Eugene, Bend and Sisters are ordinary places. The boundary was drawn around a pattern that was already there, and nobody at the Authority will say out loud whose pattern it was.

**Nobody who knows explains.** Rangers, Singee elders, the Authority’s deep-file clerks and Mrs. Birdsong all know things. None of them give speeches.

### Local strangeness stays local

Each town’s weirdness belongs to that town and doesn’t travel. The Golden Hour happens over Honey Hills and nowhere else. The Five Fords crossing doesn’t work at Sump Creek. The Eye’s beam does what it does only on The Fetch. Faust’s binding stays at Faust. The divide between East Wild and West Wild is the Wilds’ business. That keeps every town distinct, and it keeps one story’s supernatural rules from bleeding into another’s.

The standing rules above are the only strangeness that’s region-wide.

### What stays unexplained

Some things are locked shut on purpose. No story opens them, and this document doesn’t either.

- The Causeway’s second explanation: why the 1969 realignment really went where it went.

- What’s inside the Nation’s restricted-disclosure parcels.

- What the Singee call Mrs. Birdsong among themselves.

- Whether Masirua has a face the way Lunsalu does, and whether that face has ever come ashore.

- Who Mr. Birdsong is. She’s been asked. She says, “Oh, he’s around.”

- Who took the Golden Hour photograph.

## Part Seven: Mrs. Birdsong

She’s the forest on its day off.

Mostly the forest is patient. Once in a while, after a long wet winter or a quiet decade, it gets restless, the way a house cat gets restless, and it puts on an old woman and goes into town to see what people are up to. It picks the most interesting thing happening, which is usually someone at a low point, because people at low points are honest. It says something kind to them, half by accident, because it’s genuinely fond of them. Then it loses interest, or the kettle boils somewhere, and it goes.

She’s in almost every story, as a minor character the protagonist barely registers. She’s always somewhere in her seventies, in 1931 and in 2026 alike. She never arrives on the page. She’s already there, at the bus depot, in the hospital cafeteria, on the next stool. She leaves while the protagonist is looking at something else, and nobody thinks about her again. Only a reader who’s met her in three other stories puts it together.

Her standing rules, in brief:

- She’s the only character who crosses between stories.

- Two to four lines of dialogue, a question first, the kindness said sideways.

- One or two of her tells per appearance, never all of them.

- She shows up before the turn, never at the climax, and she never solves anything.

- She never appears outside the region, and never in a clearcut or fresh burn.

- If someone looks straight at her and asks who she really is, she’s gone before they finish the sentence.

- Singee elders know her. They nod, pour her a cup and say nothing.

The full character file, with her tells, her voice, sample scenes and the rules for writing her, is a companion document: *Mrs. Birdsong, Character File v2*.

### What’s been settled

**The anthology premise.** The forest is the nexus. Stories are separate. Only the map, the fixtures and Mrs. Birdsong cross.

**The Nation.** The people are the Singee. The polity, government and land use the name Nurven, without diacritics. The names Nûrvën, Neshai and Lopami are retired. The Singee were never terminated because they were never recognized until 1973.

**The river.** The Neptune’s westward course is kept as the map’s one deliberate lie, explained publicly as the Neptune Capture and in the deep register as Masirua.

**Masirua and Lunsalu.** Masirua is the pull and Lunsalu is the hold. Both are Singee words used in the Authority’s deep files.

**The Singee language.** Singee is a full language with its own sound system, set out in *The Singee Language, Sound and Spelling*. It has four vowels and no O, words that end on a vowel or N, and stress on the root, plus five edge sounds English ears miss: a catch in the throat, a popped T and K, a breathy L and a rasp from the back of the mouth. The Authority’s 1977 clerk spelling smoothed those away, and the Nation’s 1988 spelling keeps them. Nurven is Nuxven in Singee, but the legal name stays Nurven by a 1973 council vote. The first new words are xalhi, fog; t'una, cedar; and k'avi, rain.

**Singee grammar.** The verb comes first. Every statement of fact carries one of five evidence endings: -ni seen, -lha sensed, -xun figured, -k'e told, and -k'e'en the old telling. Verbs mark finished (-ka) or not yet (-ya) instead of tense. Motion verbs take a water direction: -ru seaward, -lhun inland, -sa across. Masiru'a and Lhunsalu are built from those direction words. The people words have no he or she, but split someone here (sa) from someone not here (xe), and the dead are always xe. There are two kinds of we: kin, including the listener, and nan, leaving them out. Plurals repeat a syllable, as in t'ut'una, cedars. Ownership is close for body, kin, name, home ground and dog (na lhuni, my home ground) and loose for everything else (t'una u na, my cedar). Isalhune's meaning stays soft, and elders translate it three ways. At the 1969 hearings, the interpreter turned the elders' old-telling endings into plain fact in English.

**Singee words.** *The Singee Language, Word List* holds the vocabulary: land and water, weather and time, trees and plants, animals, kin, the body, verbs, describing words, numbers counted in fives and tens, everyday words, places and words borrowed from English. Every word appears in the Nation's spelling and in clerk spelling, derived by the five clerk habits, with a guide to saying it. The Singee names for the region's places are Tumi, the Neptune River; Yamu, the sea; Xu'a, The Choke; Ikenu'i, Loki's Weir; Ruka'i, Wrack; Vasa'isa, Five Fords, whose English name is a translation; Lhemilhun, Lake Varuna; Lhunpasi, Fold Creek and the Fold; Vevexi, the Cascades; Yamuvexi, the Coast Range; Hevu, the valley; and Tumiyamu, the city of Neptune, a name that describes the place and claims nothing. Hello is vekani, "you've come, I see it," and goodbye is lhun, "keep." Clerk spelling collapses three pairs of words, including k'ura, dog, and k'uxa, bad, which both come out kura. Singee names for places inside a novel's own town are coined only when that book wants them.

**Singee history.** *The Singee, A History* is the public record. The Nurven confederation met every autumn at Vasa'isa, Five Fords. The 1830s fever, malaria, mostly spared the high country because its mosquitoes bred in the lowlands, and the Singee call those years pulhi masi, the deep night. In 1894 a Philadelphia ethnographer, Lucius V. Thorne, recorded forty-one wax cylinders, and his 1896 monograph called Singee a corrupted upland dialect of the valley's Kalapuyan peoples. Petitions in 1912 and 1931 were denied on his say-so. Singee children were enrolled at Chemawa under other nations' names, and in 1954 Singee families carried on other nations' rolls were terminated with them. The 1969 testimony was given in Singee. In 1970 a linguist compared Thorne's cylinders with the hearing tapes and proved Singee its own language. The Federal Cylinder Project returned the cylinders' copies in the 1980s, which led to the 1988 alphabet. The Return desk opened in 1990, and Thorne's notebooks came home in 2009. The Nation runs a forestry contractor, a fuel stop and market at the Fold Creek turn, and since 1996 a casino and hotel beside OR 58 in the Wilds. The immersion preschool opened in 2012. In 2026, Singee has eleven first-language speakers, all over seventy-five, and about two hundred second-language speakers. The Singee's own account of where they come from stays with the Nation and is never retold.

**The region's English.** *Neptune English, How the Region Talks* is the authority on how people speak. Speech is never spelled phonetically. Underneath is real Northwest English: cot and caught merged except among people born before about the end of World War II, *bag* drifting toward *vague*, Oregon said OR-ih-gun and Willamette wil-AM-it. The region's own fingerprints are place nicknames with "the," local pronunciations that give outsiders away (Wrack is rack, not wreck; Faust rhymes with lost; Nela is NEE-luh; Varuna is vuh-ROO-nuh), milepost directions since 1969, "Low Water hours," "tagless" for cars and newcomers since 1977, "the Sisters are out," and "check the board." Chinook Jargon words (skookum, saltchuck, tillicum, tyee, cultus) and logging words (crummy, corks, highball, tin pants, haywire) persist, fading by decade. The Singee borrowings are lun, kenuing, the rally (from the clerk spelling of xalhi) and kura (from the clerk spelling that makes dog and bad the same word). In the Rivers, the Gulf South speech that came west with the Bay Yard in 1942 still lives in the kitchen and at Galilee.

**Legends and the forest's year.** *The Greater Neptune Region, Legends and the Forest's Year* is the legend list and the seasonal record. Every legend carries one mark. Told everywhere: the girl at Low Pass, the tagless car on the Waist, Strand's gold, the spruce saw, telling the bees in Honey Hills, the Camp Nine porridge for the nisse, the following lights between the Wilds, not whistling on the Divide Road, the dogs going quiet on the Rim, the count at Lake Varuna, the flume's note at Sweetbriar and the drift board knowing its own. True nowhere: the Choke's count, the welded man in the Bay Yard, the feu follet on Cannery Slough, the bell under the Sump, the Fawn Creek lights, the splinter cat and the Nine-Week babies. True somewhere and saved for the book set there: the Patrol at Ichabod's Eye, Camp Ten, the ledger in Galilee's cornerstone, the sixth bridge at Five Fords, the Weir's slow minute, more lights than houses at Veilwood and the surveyor's last words at Faust. Ichabod's Eye's own ghost is the Patrol: Seaman Second Class Eldon Pike of Kearney, Nebraska, lost in the surf on February 9, 1944, whose dog Bosun came back alone. On fog nights he walks north toward Wrack with an empty leash and never passes Wrack, and the Eye's count runs one long. The Choke keeps no count because the region's forest and sea keep no quotas. No legend touches a held question, Mrs. Birdsong is no one's legend, and Mirth's and the Singee's stories stay with their owners. In the Road Atlas, the Lake means Lake Varuna, and the Coast Range lake at Mile 41 is the long lake.

**The University of Greater Neptune.** *The University of Greater Neptune* is the authority on the region's colleges. The Oregon Legislature created the university in 1979, at the Authority's request, from Neptune State University, Central Cascades State College and Middle Fork State College, and opened the Amity Gate campus in 1981. Since 2015 it has had one board of trustees, with a trustee nominated by the Nation. The city campus, UGN, began as the Neptune Extension Center for veterans in the Bay Yard's empty buildings in 1946, on the pattern of Portland's Vanport Extension Center, and became Neptune State College in 1955 and Neptune State University in 1968. Its green is the old slipways, called the Ways, and its student union is the yard's mold loft, called the Loft. It has about 17,800 students, the Ospreys in navy and sea-glass green, marine science, naval architecture, nursing, and a law school opened in 1974. Rivers students won its Black Studies department in a 1968 occupation, along with a promise never to build on Rivers land. Its museum returned its Singee collection to the Fold between 1994 and 2006. UGNC opened in 1966 as Central Cascades State College. It has about 8,200 students, the Lookouts in fire orange and charcoal, the School of Forestry and Fire with a late-start policy for fire crews that the chemistry department doesn't honor, and the only fifty-meter pool inside the line. The Normal opened in East Wild in 1931 as Middle Fork Normal School. It has about 3,400 students and the Engineers in maroon and gold, it trains most of the region's teachers, and since 2008 it has run a Singee teacher certification with Lhunpasi College on the Nation's terms. The Gate campus serves about 2,900 working adults and houses the Honey Hills Pollination Lab. Varuna Station opened in 1958, rows its boats because the lake allows no motors, and keeps old-growth reference stands from the 1970s on the pattern of the real H. J. Andrews Experimental Forest. Lhunpasi College (Lunpasee College in clerk spelling) opened at the Fold in 1985 with eleven students, refused the state's offer to join the university, and now has about 260 students. Outside the system, the city has Neptune Bay Community College, and Mirth uses the community college in Albany. The slough's canneries closed in the 1960s, and the fish plant at the port still runs.

**Politics.** *The Greater Neptune Region, Politics* is the authority on how the region votes. Politics has dates, and no town has a permanent party. Oregon has voted by mail since 1998. City, school, district, sheriff, judicial and Authority races are nonpartisan, county commissions depend on the county, and the counties underneath the line are real: Lane holds most of the region, with Linn under Amity Gate, Honey Hills, Mirth and Lake Varuna, Lincoln under Wrack, Marion under the Sump, Jefferson and Deschutes both under Cadence, Deschutes under Shale, and Klamath under most of Faust. Every courthouse is outside the line. The Authority's board is the Nine: the Governor's Eight from 1977, then, after the May 1986 vote that Erase the Line campaigned for as abolition, seven members elected from equal zones (Harbor, Bay, Waist, Santiam, McKenzie, Crest and South), a chair elected region-wide and the Nation's seat, first elected in 1987 with Hollis Marr as chair. The Nation's member stands out of every overlay vote. The Nation's 1975 constitution set a council of seven on staggered three-year terms, counted every September at the Fold, with the four-seat residency rule from 2003, the December check from 1997 and a one-year enrollment window since 2011. The fights that divide the region are the forest's money, land use, whether the Authority should exist, water at the Sump and Sweetbriar, fire and the 2021 hazard map, second homes, the Nation's consent, the Rivers and the Greater Idaho votes. The Sump burned the same night as the Rainbelt in September 2020. Present-day officeholders are left unnamed for each book to fill.

**The twentieth century.** *The Greater Neptune Region, The Twentieth Century* is the public record from the rail camps to the timber wars. Logs went out by splash dam until the railroads came. The Bay line opened to the port in 1916, and Nils Strand's logging railroad climbed Lake Creek through Camps One to Eight to end of track at Camp Nine in 1926. The rails came up in 1948, and the Camp stayed. Spruce soldiers built a railroad north from Wrack in 1918. The Cascade line reached East Wild in 1912 and crossed Willamette Pass in 1926, and North Fork Lumber ran its company town at Nela Junction from 1923 to 1984, with its logging railroad up the North Fork's west bank beside today's Nela Road. From 1942 to 1946 the Neptune Bay Shipbuilding Corporation, the Bay Yard, built 141 Liberty and 38 Victory ships on the city's north-bank flats and recruited workers from Louisiana, Texas and Arkansas. Black families were steered into the eastern blocks of the Harbor Homes war housing on Cannery Slough, named its streets in 1943 for the rivers of home (Sabine, Red River, Ouachita, Calcasieu, Trinity and Brazos) and, shut out of every other neighborhood by the realty board, bought that ground when the project came down. That neighborhood is the Rivers, and its church is Galilee Missionary Baptist, founded in October 1942. The Harbor Center renewal of 1963 to 1966 cleared the Rivers' western half, 412 households and 61 businesses, for the civic district, and the promised replacement housing was never built. The Authority bought the cleared land in 1978, the city voted in 1996 to study a reckoning, and the city hasn't paid. Coast Guard beach patrols with dogs worked out of Ichabod's Eye from early 1943 to July 1944. The region's Japanese American families were taken in 1942. Marlow Pacific's mill ran at East Wild from 1948 to 1987. In the timber wars, the federal record is real (the owl in 1990, Judge Dwyer in 1991, the Forest Conference in 1993, the Northwest Forest Plan in April 1994, the salvage rider in 1995), and the Fawn Creek blockade above East Wild in 1995 and 1996 follows the real Warner Creek. Everyone in the region knows that old growth is awake and a cut is nearly asleep, so the timber wars were partly an argument neither side would make out loud. That's a public fact about belief and adds no deep-register rule. The Nation declined both sides and kept voting down old-growth sales in the overlay for reasons it stated as a government.

**Cadence and Sisters.** The atlas places Cadence in the Suttle Lake basin, west of the region’s eastern edge, so Sisters stays an outside town just past the exit. Cadence fills the basin and the Black Butte flats.

**Amity Gate.** It sits at Harrisburg, north of Eugene and Springfield, so the Waist threads between those cities and Corvallis and Albany. It doesn’t overlap any real city.

**Sub-regions.** There are now seven. The Coast Range Interior and the Valley Waist have climate entries. Veilwood moved to the Southern Upland. Amity Gate and Honey Hills moved to the Valley Waist.

**Camp Nine.** It joins the ledger with 980 people drawn from the unassigned reserve.

**Mirth.** It joins the ledger with 6,840 people drawn from the unassigned reserve, which now stands at 52,180. Mirth is the setting of *The Tithe*, and its own detail is in *Mirth, Encyclopedia Entry*, which lives with the novel in The Tithe’s repository. Mirth is 40 miles down US 20 from Santiam Junction, about an hour from Cadence over the pass and about an hour from Amity Gate by the valley road through Sweet Home.

**The city’s name.** The coastal seat is Neptune. “City” says what kind of place it is and isn’t part of the name, the way you’d say the city of Raleigh. Prose says the city of Neptune, with a lowercase city, wherever the river, bay or region could be meant, and plain Neptune once it’s clear. A capital “City of Neptune” belongs only to the town government by its formal name, as on a permit or a sign at city hall.

**The Causeway.** Its route and history are tied to real roads: the old river highway follows OR 126 through Eugene, and the 1969 realignment avoids it.

**Mrs. Birdsong.** She’s rebuilt as the bored forest, in her seventies, unnoticed. The Myco-Resonant Field, the Guardian Echo idea and her shop in Cadence are retired from her.

**Retired from the older files.** The duplicated “Neptune Bay Neptune Bay” sector label, the four-or-five sector confusion and the old glossary sector names are dropped. Ranger districts and sub-regions replace sectors entirely.

**The roads.** The *Road Atlas* is the authority for every distance and drive time. The Causeway is 189 miles long. It leaves the river at the Lake Creek Turn, Mile 23, crosses the Coast Range at Low Pass, and leaves the old highway at Cheshire Fork so it never passes through Junction City. The Old Grade through Eugene is about half an hour faster from the coast to anywhere east of the valley, and it floods. Forest roads carry two-digit numbers in blocks by ranger district, and their spurs carry four. Fold Creek Road is a Nation road with no forest number. Every new town’s road gets four facts in the atlas.

**Getting in.** No major airport and no passenger station sits inside the region. Eugene and Redmond serve it from just outside the line, Portland handles the long haul, and the city of Neptune keeps its own small fogbound field. The Coast Starlight runs through the South Divide without stopping. The Bay line and the Cascade line carry freight.

**The Wilds.** It’s a string town along the Middle Fork and OR 58, West Wild down-valley and East Wild up at the forks, with six dark miles and Nela Junction between them.

**Veilwood.** Its one road is Nela Road on the North Fork’s west bank. Saltlick Grade runs up the far bank with no bridge across.

**The South in winter.** Every way from the South to the rest of the region leaves the region first, through Eugene or around through Bend. Faust, cut off from Shale when the Rim Line Road closes, is a South Divide town until spring.
