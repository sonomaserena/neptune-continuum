# The Greater Neptune Region: Legends and the Forest's Year

*What the region tells after dark, and how the woods smell and sound from one season to the next*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*, *The Greater Neptune Region, The Twentieth Century*, *Neptune English, How the Region Talks* and *Low Water, Program Bible*

## How to Use This File

Every region tells stories about itself that aren't in the paperwork. This file collects the Greater Neptune Region's: the ones a kid hears at a sleepover, a logger hears on his first week in the crummy, a tourist hears from a bartender in Wrack. It also records what the forest smells and sounds like across the year, so any story can put a reader in a particular month in a particular town with one detail.

Legends sit in the public register. People tell them, and that's a fact anyone can use. Whether a legend is true is a different matter, and the house rule says only a book set where it's told can decide that, for its own town and nowhere else. So every legend here carries one of three marks.

**Told everywhere.** Any book may repeat it, anywhere in the region, and no book has claimed it. Its truth is open. A book set where the legend lives may decide the answer for that place. Until one does, everyone else treats it as a story people tell.

**True nowhere.** It's false, and the public explanation is given. Any book may use it as something people believe, and none may make it true.

**True somewhere, saved.** It's true in one place, and the place, the how and the why are reserved for the book that wants them. Every other book may repeat it only as rumor.

Some things stay out. No legend here touches a held question, so the region's dozen stories about who took the Golden Hour photograph aren't recorded, and neither is any story about why the Causeway was moved in 1969, what's inside the restricted parcels, whether the sea has a face, who Mr. Birdsong is, or what the Singee call Mrs. Birdsong. Mrs. Birdsong isn't a legend at all. Nobody tells stories about her, because nobody notices her. The Singee's own stories belong to the Nation and aren't retold here. Mirth's legends belong to Mirth and live in the Tithe's own canon, so this file carries none of them.

## The Legend List

### Told everywhere

**The girl at Low Pass.** A driver westbound on the Causeway at night picks up a girl standing at the summit pullout at Mile 50, in the rain, with no coat. She asks to go to Camp Nine. She doesn't say much. The radio drops out at the summit, the way *Low Water* always does at the cut where neither transmitter reaches, and when it comes back three miles on, the seat beside the driver is empty and wet, and the Camp Nine turn at Mile 47 is just ahead. It's the region's version of the vanishing hitchhiker, a story told on every highway in America, and every Coast Range family has a cousin it happened to. Some tellers say she's from the rail camps. Some say she's from 1964. Young Singee speakers argue about whether the story has earned the old-telling ending yet, and their elders say it hasn't, it's barely sixty years old.

**The tagless car.** Late at night on the long straight across the Waist, between Cheshire Fork and Amity Gate, a car comes up behind you with its high beams on. It has no Authority tag on the mirror, which you can somehow see, and it never passes and never turns off. Then it's gone. People who tell it say the car is always a make that ought to be thirty years too old for the road. Truckers on the Causeway have a phrase for it: "I had a tagless on me past 62."

**Strand's gold.** In October 1923, the story goes, two men stopped the Strand Timber Company's pay car on the Lake Creek line between Camp Seven and Camp Eight and took a strongbox of gold coin meant for a month's wages. One was shot and caught. The other went up the creek and never came down. Camp Nine people say the box is still up there, buried under a stump that's since rotted to nothing, and every few years someone with a metal detector gets lost in the second growth looking for it. The Strand payroll records for that year are missing, which helps.

**The spruce saw.** On still evenings on the old spruce railroad grade north of Wrack, people walking their dogs hear a two-man crosscut saw working somewhere in the brush, the long pull and the answering push, and then a man laugh. The soldiers who built that grade in 1918 were gone in less than a year. Wrack shrugs about it. The forest remembers, and a few soldiers' afternoons seem a small thing to keep.

**Telling the bees.** In Honey Hills, when someone in a beekeeping family dies, somebody goes out to the hives that same day and tells the bees, out loud, by name. Some families tie a strip of black cloth on each hive. If you don't, the bees leave, or sicken, or sting the mourners at the graveside. The custom came from England and New England, where it was old even when John Greenleaf Whittier wrote his poem about it in 1858, and it came to the benches above Shedd with the first beekeepers. Families who came later, from other places and other traditions, picked it up because the bees were there first. Nobody on the benches skips it, and nobody will tell you whether they believe it.

**The Camp Nine porridge.** Nils Strand's first cook was a Norwegian, and on Christmas Eve he set a bowl of rice porridge with a pat of butter on top on the cookhouse step for the nisse, the barn spirit of every Norwegian farm. Camp Nine still does it, eighty years after the last Norwegian left. The butter goes on top, never underneath. Every Norwegian grandmother's story tells what a nisse does when he thinks he's been cheated of his butter, and Camp Nine tells its own version: the winter somebody hid the butter at the bottom of the bowl as a joke was the winter the yarder threw a cable. Kids from the Camp who move away to Portland still set a bowl out on their apartment steps and feel foolish about it.

**The following lights.** On the six dark miles of OR 58 between West Wild and East Wild, with the river on one side and the railroad on the other, a pair of headlights comes up behind you and holds a steady distance whatever speed you drive. At Nela Junction they turn off. Nobody has ever seen what turns off. East Wild says they're West Wild's, and West Wild says they're East Wild's, which is the Wilds in one sentence.

**Don't whistle on the Divide Road.** Everyone in the South knows not to whistle on the Divide Road above Fold Creek, and nearer the Hush, not to whistle at all. Nobody can say who told them. Kids from East Wild dare each other to whistle at the Hush turnoff and then don't. People who live at the Hush say there's no rule, it's just rude, and they say it the way you'd tell someone not to talk in church.

**The dogs going quiet.** On the Rim, every dog in Shale sometimes stops barking at once, in the middle of the night, and stays quiet for as long as a minute. Rim people trust that silence more than any weather report. Some say it means a fire, some say snow, some say a stranger on the Rim Line Road. The legend is that it never means nothing.

**The count at Lake Varuna.** Divers who go down to the drowned forest a hundred feet under Lake Varuna say you can't count the standing trees. Two divers on the same dive come up with different numbers, and the same diver gets a new count every time. The dive shops in Lake Varuna sell a T-shirt about it. Lake Varuna's money likes the legend fine.

**The flume's note.** The power canal through Sweetbriar hums all night, and people who grew up beside it say its note changes, a half step down, the night before somebody leaves town for good. Sweetbriar kids home from college swear they heard it the night before they left.

**The board knows its own.** Things lost anywhere in the region turn up on the drift board at Wrack, sooner or later, and that much is the forest's standing rule. The legend goes further: if you drop something in the Neptune and say out loud who gave it to you, it'll be on the board by spring. Teenagers in the Rainbelt do this with class rings after breakups. Wrack's postmaster says it's mostly fishing lures.

### True nowhere

**The Choke's count.** Bar pilots and old fishermen on the coast used to say the Choke takes one swimmer a year, and that if a year goes by without one, the next year it takes two. It's false. The Coast Guard's station records and the city's own drowning reports show no count of any kind, only the steady, uneven toll of fast water on people who misjudge it. The Choke is dangerous every day, and that's the whole truth of it. It's recorded here because it's exactly the kind of rule the region doesn't have: the forest and the sea don't keep quotas.

**The welded man.** The Bay Yard welded its ships in sections, fast, and the story started there in 1943: a welder fell into a Liberty ship's double bottom during a night shift, and the crew sealed the plates over him without knowing, and the ship went to sea with him in it. Sailors on the ship heard him knocking in heavy weather. The Bay Yard's records account for every worker who died there, seventeen over four years, and every one of them was buried. The knocking that every Liberty ship's crew heard was the hull working in a seaway. The story is still told in the Rivers, mostly by men whose fathers and grandmothers worked the ways and wanted the children to understand how fast the work was.

**The feu follet on Cannery Slough.** Families from south Louisiana brought the feu follet with them in 1942: the wandering lights of the marshes back home, which Louisiana grandmothers said were lost souls and warned children never to follow. In the Rivers the lights were said to rise over Cannery Slough on winter nights, and the warning was the same. Don't follow them or you'll end up in the water. In Louisiana people put the lights down to marsh gas, and on Cannery Slough there was a plainer answer: the cannery skiffs worked the slough at night with lanterns until the canneries closed, and after that the lights were fishermen. The grandmothers knew it, and told the story anyway, because the slough's mud is deep and the tide comes in fast. It worked. Nobody's child drowned in the slough.

**The bell under the Sump.** People who rent cabins at Sump Creek say that on calm nights you can hear the church bell of the drowned town ringing under the reservoir. In 2001, when drought drained the reservoir nearly dry, half the High Country walked the old town's streets, and the church's bell tower was empty. The bell had been moved in 1952 to the new church on the hill above the reservoir, where it hangs now. What people hear on calm nights is the bell buoy at the boat channel. They still hear it, and still tell it, and the new church rings its bell at Sunday service as though nothing were the matter.

**The Fawn Creek lights.** In the winter of 1995, people in East Wild saw lights moving along the ridge above the Fawn Creek burn at night, and a story went around town that the burned stand was waking up. The lights were the headlamps of the protesters at the blockade, walking the ridge to a place where their radios could reach Eugene. The story stuck to the burn anyway, and some of the people who tell it now were at the barricade.

**The splinter cat.** Old loggers at Camp Nine told every green choker setter about the splinter cat, a beast that rams standing snags with its head on stormy nights to get at the honey and raccoons inside, and that's why there's so much shattered wood after a blow. It came from the Great Lakes logging camps, where a forester named William T. Cox wrote it down with a dozen of its cousins in 1910. At Camp Nine it was a joke on the new man, along with being sent to the shop for a sky hook. Nobody ever believed it, except the new man, for about a day.

**The Nine-Week babies.** Cadence says there was a baby boom in the fall of 1993, nine months after the Nine-Week Winter cut the city off from the west side. The birth records at the hospital in Cadence show no bump at all. Cadence tells it anyway, and every child born that October has heard it at least once from a stranger.

### True somewhere, saved

These are true in one place. Each is saved for the book set there, which decides what's true about it and how. Every other book repeats it only as rumor.

**The Patrol at Ichabod's Eye.** The Eye's own ghost. It has a section of its own below.

**Camp Ten.** Camp Nine was end of track, and the Camp kept its number because nobody ever filed another one. The old-timers' story is that somebody nearly did. In the summer of 1927, the story goes, Strand sent a crew above the end of track to lay out a Camp Ten in the timber at the very head of the drainage. They went up in the morning and came back the same afternoon, all of them, and never said why, and Strand never sent another crew. Saved for a book set at Camp Nine.

**The ledger in Galilee's cornerstone.** The Rivers believes that when Galilee Missionary Baptist built on Sabine Street in 1948, Reverend Emmett Batiste sealed a ledger in the cornerstone listing every family who came on the yard trains, where they came from, what they were paid and what they were refused. Later tellers add that during the renewal he went back and added the names of every family the city moved. The cornerstone has never been opened. Saved for a book set in the Rivers.

**The sixth bridge.** Five bridges have stood at Five Fords under four names. Fishermen on fog mornings say they've seen a sixth, downstream of the others, low and old, with no road at either end. Saved for a book set at Five Fords.

**The Weir's slow minute.** In Loki's Weir, people say, every clock in town runs a minute slow during the hour at the head of tide when the water seems to hesitate, and catches up afterward. Kitchen clocks, church clocks, the clock in the tavern. Phones don't, which only makes it worse. The Weir doesn't talk about it with outsiders. Saved for a book set at Loki's Weir.

**More lights than houses.** From the turnout on Saltlick Grade, summer drivers look across the North Fork at Veilwood's lights. Some nights, the people who count say, there are more lights across the river than Veilwood has houses. There's no bridge to go and check. Saved for a book set at Veilwood.

**The surveyor's last words.** The surveyor whose chains crossed something at Faust ended his notes mid-sentence because he stopped mid-sentence. The legend at Faust is that the end of the sentence exists, written somewhere else, and that people have gone looking for it. Saved for a book set at Faust.

## The Patrol: The Ghost of Ichabod's Eye

From early 1943 to July 1944, Ichabod's Eye was a Coast Guard beach patrol station, as the real Heceta Head was. Every night, pairs of young coastguardsmen walked the sand of The Fetch with a dog on a short leash, from the light north to Wrack and back. The dogs had been family pets, donated to the war by families across the country and trained for the patrols. Most of the men had never seen the ocean before.

Seaman Second Class Eldon Pike was nineteen and from Kearney, Nebraska. He walked the north patrol with a coxswain from Astoria named Harold Lindqvist and a German shepherd named Bosun, who had been a family's dog in Salem. On the night of February 9, 1944, with a big swell running and fog down to the sand, the three of them were crossing a creek mouth a mile north of the light when a sneaker wave came through. Lindqvist got hold of a log and climbed out. Pike and the dog were gone.

The Coast Guard searched the beach for three days and listed Pike as lost in the surf. His body was never found. On the third morning, Bosun walked back up the beach to the Eye alone, soaked and sanded, and sat down at the foot of the tower facing north, and wouldn't come in. When the patrols ended that summer, Bosun went home to Salem, the way the donated dogs did.

That's the record. The legend is what The Fetch has told since.

On fog nights, people on the beach below the Eye see a young man in a Coast Guard peacoat walking north along the waterline toward Wrack, a short leash in one hand and no dog on it. He never turns his head. He never goes past the first houses of Wrack. On other nights, people see the dog alone, a big shepherd sitting at the foot of the tower facing north, and when they look again it isn't there. People on The Fetch say "Pike's out" the way people elsewhere say the fog's in, and they don't let their kids go down on the sand those nights.

The Fetch adds one more thing, and it's the part that belongs only to the Eye. Coast people count Ol' Ichabod's flash without meaning to, the way you'd count your own pulse. On the nights Pike's out, they say, the count runs one long. Somewhere in the night there's a flash that shouldn't be there.

Sometime in the 1960s, a brass leash snap stamped with the Coast Guard's mark turned up on the drift board at Wrack. Nobody claimed it, and nobody has taken it down. It's still pinned there, green with age, beside the keys and the single shoes.

Whether any of this is true, why Pike walks, what the extra flash is and what he's looking for belong to the book set at Ichabod's Eye. The telling is everyone's. What the region agrees on is only what the tellers agree on: he walks north, he never speaks, he never passes Wrack, and he has never hurt anyone. The real Heceta Head has a well-known ghost story of its own, and the Eye borrows nothing from it.

## The Forest's Year

The forest has a different smell and a different noise in every month, and people who grow up in the region can tell the month with their eyes shut. These are the things a viewpoint character would notice. Every one of them is real, on this ground.

### Winter, December to February

Winter smells of wet cedar and woodsmoke, and of the forest floor breaking down: rot, moss, mushrooms going soft, the iron smell of the Coast Range creeks. On the coast it smells of kelp and salt, and after a big storm of the whole torn-up sea lying on the sand. In the High Country it smells of nothing at all, which is snow.

The winter sound of the region is rain. It ticks on moss, roars in the creeks and hisses on the Causeway under tires all night. The bird of winter is the varied thrush, a long, buzzy whistle on one note and then another on a different note, out of the dark under the firs. It's the loneliest sound in the woods and people love it. Ravens talk in the tops of the trees. The great horned owls start calling to each other in the dark of December. In the High Country, snow stops all of it. A snowed-in forest is so quiet you can hear a branch let go of its load a hundred yards away.

On The Fetch in winter, the Steller sea lions crowd into the great sea cave south of the Eye, the one the coast calls the Roar, and on still nights you can hear them barking from Old Coast Road. Gray whales pass going south, most of them between mid-December and mid-January, and coast people watch for the blows from the headlands. On the rivers, the last of the fall salmon have died, and bald eagles sit in the cottonwoods over the gravel bars.

Late in January the frogs start. By February the Pacific chorus frogs are calling from every ditch and pond in the Waist and the Rainbelt, a racket of ribbits that drowns out traffic, and it's the first real promise of spring. Somebody on *Low Water* always calls in the first night they hear them.

### Spring, March to May

Spring starts with smells. The osoberry blooms first, in February and March, before anything else in the woods, with little hanging clusters of greenish-white flowers, and its new leaves smell like cucumber when you crush them. Then skunk cabbage comes up in every wet bottom in the Coast Range and the Rainbelt, yellow hoods in the black mud, smelling exactly as its name says, and kids hold their noses walking past and adults secretly like it because it means winter's over. In April the cottonwoods along the rivers put out sticky buds that smell like balsam and honey, and the whole river corridor smells sweet for two weeks.

The sound of spring in the high timber is the sooty grouse. From March into May, the males hoot from perches high in the firs, often a hundred feet up, a deep, slow run of five or six notes so low you feel it in your chest before you hear it. It's impossible to find the bird, and the sound seems to come from everywhere at once. Old loggers called it the forest's heartbeat. Snowmelt makes every creek in the Cascades loud, and in the High Country the rivers run milky with it.

On The Fetch, the sea lions leave the Roar in spring and move out onto the rock ledges in front of it to breed, and the bulls bellow at each other all day. The rufous hummingbirds come back to the flowering currant. In May, the Swainson's thrushes arrive from Mexico, and the region's summer evenings begin.

### Summer, June to August

Summer smells of hot Douglas-fir: pitch warming on the bark and the dry needle duff underfoot, a smell like a cedar chest left in the sun. On the dry side, around Cadence and on the Rim, the old ponderosas give off something sweeter. Put your nose to the yellow bark of a big old ponderosa on a hot afternoon, and it smells of vanilla or butterscotch, or cookies baking, depending on who's sniffing. Rim kids grow up smelling trees on dares. On the coast, summer smells of fog, which smells of salt and cold, because The Fetch is foggiest in July. In the valley, summer used to smell of smoke from the grass-seed fields, which farmers burned after harvest from the late 1940s until the state ended most of it on the valley floor in 2010. People who grew up in the Waist before then remember the black plumes on the horizon every August and the way the sun went red.

The great sound of summer is the Swainson's thrush at dusk, a song of flute notes that spirals upward and seems to come from a different direction every time you turn your head. It's the sound of a Rainbelt evening on a porch. In the heat of the day the woods go quiet, and you hear grasshoppers clicking in the clearcuts and the creaks of trees cooling at night. The smoke months start in August. Smoke changes the sound of the woods, too. It flattens everything, and the birds go quiet under an orange sun.

August is huckleberry month in the high country, and the huckleberry fields smell of hot leaves and sugar.

### Fall, September to November

Fall begins with the first big rain after fire season, and everyone in the region knows the smell: hot dust and dry needles hit by water, sharp and clean, a smell people walk outside on purpose to breathe. After that it's wet leaves, bigleaf maples dropping their huge yellow leaves on every road, and woodsmoke coming back. In October the rivers smell of salmon. The chinook spawn on the gravel bars and die there, and the Rainbelt's rivers smell of fish for weeks, which outsiders think is terrible and locals think is how a river is supposed to smell. Chanterelles come up under the second-growth fir with the October rains, and every family has a patch it won't tell anyone about.

The sound of fall is the elk. From mid-September to mid-October the bulls bugle, a high, screaming whistle that drops into grunts, ringing up the valleys at dawn and dusk. Somebody from out of state always calls the sheriff about it, thinking a woman is screaming in the woods. Canada geese go over the Waist in long, loud skeins. Filberts drop in the valley orchards in October, and the varied thrushes come down out of the high country in November and start their winter whistling again.

The first hard frost comes in early November in the Rainbelt canyons and earlier on the benches. In Honey Hills, a beekeeper calls *Low Water* every year on the first night of frost on the benches, and much of the Waist treats her call as the official start of winter.

### Smells that date a scene

Some of these are gone, and a scene can use them to say what decade it is.

The wigwam burners: before the mills closed in the 1980s and 1990s, every mill town had a rusted cone burning sawdust and slab wood day and night, and the smoke smelled sweet and bitter, of burning fir. Mill-town children of the 1960s fell asleep to its glow.

The green chain and the log pond: a mill smelled of fresh-cut wet fir and the sour water of the pond where logs waited for the saw.

The canneries: Cannery Slough smelled of fish guts and steam until the last cannery closed.

Grass-seed smoke: the Waist's summers smelled of burning stubble until 2010.

Smoke months: the region always had fire, but since the 2010s, smoke has come for weeks every August and September, and "smoke day" and the air purifier humming in the bedroom are newer than most people over forty can quite believe.

## Writing Legends and Weather

**Let people tell legends badly.** Nobody tells a legend the way it's written here. They get the year wrong, put it in their own town, add a cousin. The version a character tells is part of who they are.

**Keep the marks.** A legend marked true nowhere stays false in every book. A legend saved for a town stays rumor everywhere else, and no other book may quietly make it real. A legend told everywhere may be decided only by a book set where it's told, and only for that place.

**Use one detail.** A single smell or sound puts a reader in a month. Skunk cabbage, a varied thrush, an elk bugling at dawn, the first rain after fire season. One is enough, and two is plenty.

**The forest's year is the same for everyone.** Weather and seasons are fixtures. Any story in the region can have the frogs in February and the elk in September, and no town owns them.
