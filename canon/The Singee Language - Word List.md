# The Singee Language: Word List

*The words of Singee, both spellings, how to say them, and what they mean*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*, *The Singee Language, Sound and Spelling* and *The Singee Language, Grammar*

## How to Read This List

Every word appears three ways. The **Nation's spelling** is how Singee speakers write it. **Clerk spelling** is how the Authority's typists would have written it in 1977, following their five habits: no catch, no breathy L, the rasp written as R, no pop, and a final I written *ee*. **Say it** gives the sounds in plain English letters, with the root syllable in capitals. Narration uses whichever spelling its viewpoint character would use.

A Singee word doesn't always line up with one English word. Some Singee words cover two English ones, some English words take a Singee phrase, and a few Singee words have no English match at all. Those are the ones a novelist should go looking for, and the next section gathers them.

## The Words English Doesn't Have

**kenu**, to wait the way water waits at the head of tide. The water stops without stopping: still moving underneath, deciding. A Singee speaker can say someone is kenu-ing over a job offer, a breakup or a phone call they haven't returned. Loki's Weir is Ikenu'i, the place of waiting.

**xaxalhi**, fog everywhere. It's not "fogs." It's the morning when every drainage in the South Divide is full at once and the whole country has gone white.

**hetaya**, both morning and tomorrow: the day coming. Like *mañana*, it lets a speaker promise something without promising when.

**hetaka masi**, long ago. Literally, "deep yesterday."

**rukesu**, to forget. Literally, the knowing goes to the sea. **lhunkesu**, to remember, is the knowing held.

**lhun'a**, the one who raised you, whoever that was. A grandmother, an aunt, an older sister, a neighbor who took you in. Singee doesn't make you explain which.

**yalhisa**, cousin. Literally, a sibling from across. Singee has words for older and younger siblings, not for brothers and sisters, so a cousin is simply a sibling who grew up on the other side of something.

**xenula**, outsiders. Literally, "the not-here people." It isn't rude. It's exact.

**sayen** and **xeyen**, to be born and to die: to come to be here, and to go to be not here.

**k'ek'eli**, gossip. Literally, "tell and tell."

**yelika**, a burn scar. Literally, "fire finished." In the Rainbelt since 2020, it's a word people use more than they'd like.

**vekani**, hello. It means "you've come, I see it," with the seen ending built in. The usual answer is **e'e, vekani**: yes, you see it.

**lhun**, goodbye. It's the bare command "keep," as in keep well, keep safe, keep going. Singee teenagers text it, and in clerk spelling it comes out *lun*, which their non-Singee friends think is short for lunch.

## Words That Sound Like Something Else

Clerk spelling collapses some words that are different in Singee. Three pairs on this list come out identical:

- **pulhi**, night, and **puli**, wet, are both *pulee*.
- **k'itu**, Sitka spruce, and **kitu**, to take, are both *kitu*.
- **k'ura**, dog, and **k'uxa**, bad, are both *kura*.

The last pair is a standing joke at the Fold. In the Authority's files, a Singee family's dog and a Singee word for trouble are spelled exactly alike, and more than one 1970s report about "kura on the trail" has been read both ways. When the deep files seem to confuse two Singee words, this is usually why. Singee speakers reading those files spot the mix-ups at once, and the files have never been corrected.

A few Singee words happen to look like English or other languages' words. *Mani* is money, *kara* is car and *telepun* is telephone, because those really were borrowed from English and reshaped to fit Singee sounds: no O, no closing consonant except N, and every word ending on a vowel or N. Other look-alikes are coincidence and mean nothing.

## A Telling: The Christmas Flood at Five Fords

The archive house at the Fold holds a reel-to-reel tape, recorded in 1987, of an elder from Five Fords describing the Christmas Flood of 1964. These are the first nine lines, in the Nation's spelling. The hyphens show where the pieces join. Listen for how the endings change as her knowledge changes: what she saw, what she heard in the dark, what her mother told her, and what has always been told.

**K'avi-ka-ni tilu heta, Vasa'isa, hetaka masi.**
It rained for three days at Five Fords, long ago. I saw it.

**Yen-ru-lha tumi, pulhi.**
All night the river was going to the sea. I heard it.

**Suli-ka-k'e sasapa. Ta, ki.**
The bridges went down, one and then two. I was told.

**K'eli-ka-ni na seni.**
My mother told me. I watched her tell it.

**Ve-ka-ni nexa tatalhi, hetaya.**
In the morning, the water came into the town. I saw it.

**Ve-sa-ka-ni u'ula.**
People came across the river. I saw them.

**Suli-ka-k'e yenpa.**
The road was gone too, they said.

**Yen-ru-k'e'en tumi.**
The river goes home to the sea. It has always been told.

**Yen-ru-ka-ni sa, ta pusan.**
That one winter, I watched it go.

## How New Words Are Made

New Singee words are coined only when a story needs one. Each new word has to fit the sound rules: no O, words that end on a vowel or N, no two vowels touching, and the stress on the root. A word that can be built from pieces already in the language, such as a compound, a repeated syllable, or a root with *-ka*, *-ya*, *-'a*, *-i* or *-salu*, is better than a brand-new root. Every new word joins this list in both spellings.
