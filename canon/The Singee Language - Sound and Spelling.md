# The Singee Language: Sound and Spelling

*How Singee sounds, how it's written, and why it's written two ways*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*

## What Singee Sounds Like

Picture an elder at the Fold reading a list of place names into a recorder for the archive. Her words end open, on a vowel or a low hum of N. She never swallows a vowel the way English does, so a four-syllable word gets all four syllables, each one full. Then, every few words, there's an edge: a small catch in her throat, a breathy hiss where an English speaker expects an L, a rasp from far back in the mouth, a T or a K that comes out with a pop.

The soft parts are what everyone in the region hears. The edges are what they miss. That's how the Authority's 1977 spellings came out sounding like a perfume ad, and how the Nation's own spelling came to look rough on purpose. Singee words written by people who speak Singee look like they belong to people.

## The Vowels

Singee has four vowels, and each one always sounds the same.

| **Letter** | **Sounds like** |
| --- | --- |
| a | the a in *father* |
| e | the e in *bed* |
| i | the i in *machine* |
| u | the u in *rule* |

**There is no O.** No Singee word has one, in either spelling, and none ever will. It's the language's simplest tell. A "Singee" word with an O in it was made up by someone who doesn't speak Singee: the gift shop in Cadence selling dreamcatchers under a fake Singee name, the 1950s motel that called itself the Noquila Lodge, the summer camp with the invented chant. The old Neshai vocabulary the region retired (Lanku, Sulu, Nasha, Sulu'noe) was never Singee, and the O in Sulu'noe gives it away.

**Two vowels never touch.** Wherever English would run two vowels together, Singee puts a catch in the throat between them. Masirua isn't *ma-si-rua*. It's *ma-si-ru*, a catch, then *a*.

**Vowels are never swallowed.** English turns unstressed vowels into a mumbled *uh*. Singee says every vowel at full value, stressed or not. More than anything else, this is what makes a Singee speaker's English sound careful, and it's what a Singee teenager's English has mostly lost.

## The Consonants

Singee has seventeen consonants. Most of them sound close to English. Five of them don't, and those five are the edges.

| **Letter** | **Sounds like** | **In a word** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| p | the p in *spin* | |
| t | the t in *stop* | |
| k | the k in *skin* | |
| t' | a popped t (see below) | t'una, cedar |
| k' | a popped k (see below) | k'avi, rain |
| ’ | the catch in the throat, as in *uh-oh* | Masiru'a |
| m | m | Masiru'a |
| n | n | Lhunsalu |
| ng | the ng in *singer*, and never an n followed by a g | Singi |
| s | s | Isalhune |

| x | a rasp at the back of the mouth (see below) | Nuxven, xalhi (fog) |
| h | h | |
| v | a soft v, made with both lips instead of lip and teeth | Nuxven |
| r | a quick tap of the tongue, like the tt in American *butter* | Masiru'a |
| l | l | Lhunsalu |
| y | the y in *yes* | |

There is no letter g on its own, so ng is always one sound.

### The five edges

**The catch** is the stop in the middle of *uh-oh*: the throat closes for an instant and lets go. It falls between any two vowels and sometimes between a vowel and a consonant. The Nation writes it with an apostrophe after a vowel: Masiru'a.

**The popped consonants**, t' and k', are made with the throat closed, so the sound comes out with a small, sharp pop. Say a T while holding your breath and you're close. The Nation writes them with an apostrophe right after the consonant. An apostrophe after a consonant means a pop, and an apostrophe after a vowel means a catch, so the two never get confused.

**The breathy L**, lh, is the sound of the double L in Welsh. Put your tongue where it goes for an L, then blow instead of using your voice. English ears hear it as a plain L, or sometimes as *thl*. The root of the word for the forest's hold carries it, so Lunsalu is really Lhunsalu, and Isalune is Isalhune.

**The rasp**, x, comes from farther back in the mouth than the ch in Scottish *loch*. It's close to a French R said in a whisper. That's why English-speaking clerks wrote it as an R, which is how the Nation's name came to be spelled Nurven.

## How Words Are Built from Sounds

**A syllable is a consonant and a vowel.** Ma, si, ru, lhu, ne. Only the first syllable of a word may begin with a bare vowel, as Isalhune does.

**Inside a word, a syllable may close on N or on the rasp.** Lhun-sa-lu closes its first syllable on N, and Nux-ven closes its first on X.

**A word ends on a vowel or on N, and nothing else.** That's why Singee words trail off or hum at the end and never stop hard.

**The stress falls on the root.** Every Singee word is built around one root syllable. Prefixes in front of it and endings after it are never stressed, so wherever the root sits in the word, the voice lands on it. A Singee speaker can hear which syllable carries the meaning.

The canon words show it. Lhunsalu is LHOON-sah-loo, stressed on *lhun*, the root of the forest's hold. Isalhune carries the same root in the middle, so it's ee-sah-LHOO-neh, with the stress in the middle where the root is. Masiru'a is mah-see-ROO-'ah, stressed on its root, *ru*. Singi is SING-ee, and Nuxven is NOOKH-ven.

## The Two Spellings, and the One Before Them

### The Authority's spelling

When the 1977 charter brought Singee words into the Bioregional Authority's deep files, clerks typed them the way they heard them. Singee teenagers call it clerk spelling. It follows five habits:

- The catch disappears. Masiru'a became Masirua.
- The breathy L becomes a plain L. Lhunsalu became Lunsalu.
- The rasp becomes an R, or sometimes an H, or vanishes. Nuxven became Nurven.
- The popped T and K become plain T and K.
- A final I becomes *ee*. Singi became Singee.

Clerk spelling has one quirk that lore divers love. Singee's tapped R and its rasp are two different sounds, but the clerks wrote both as R. In the Authority's files, Masirua and Nurven share a letter that the two words don't share in Singee.

Clerk spelling is the public one. It's on the highway signs, the Authority's maps and forms, the region's textbooks, and in the name of Isalune's Hush.

### The Nation's spelling

In 1988 the Nation's language committee settled on a spelling of its own. The archive house had begun copying old reel-to-reel tapes of elders, and the transcribers needed one way to write what they heard. The committee worked on a typewriter, so the spelling uses only letters on an ordinary keyboard plus the apostrophe. That turned out to be lucky: thirty years later, it types on any phone.

The Nation's spelling is used on the Nation's own signs and buildings at the Fold, at the foot of Fold Creek Road, in the dictionary and the archive, in the immersion preschool, and in everything Singee people write to each other in Singee.

### The ethnographer's notation

There's a third, older spelling: the private notation of the 1890s ethnographer who recorded Singee on wax cylinders. It's full of symbols no keyboard has ever had. It survives only in his notebooks, which came home to the Fold through the Return desk and now sit in the dry room of the archive house. He is the reason the Singee spent most of a century officially not existing.

## Nurven and the 1973 Vote

When the Nation was recognized in 1973, its council had to give the federal government a legal name. Half the council wanted Nuxven, the real word. The other half wanted Nurven, the spelling already in the 1969 hearing transcripts, written in letters no federal clerk could get wrong. Nurven won by two votes. One of the elders who voted for Nuxven never wrote the English spelling in her life, not even on a check.

So the legal name is Nurven, everywhere and for good, and the Nation itself writes it that way on its letterhead. In Singee, and among Singee speakers, it's Nuxven. Which one a character says aloud, and how close they get to the rasp, tells you a great deal about them.

## Which Spelling Goes on the Page

Narration uses the spelling its viewpoint character would use. A ranger, a motel clerk or most locals see the clerk spelling: Masirua, Lunsalu, Isalune. A Singee character, or anyone who has learned the language, sees the Nation's spelling: Masiru'a, Lhunsalu, Isalhune. The spelling on the page shows whose eyes the reader is looking through, without anyone having to say so.

A Singee character's narration can still use the clerk spelling for anything official, such as a highway sign, a permit or the name on the Hush's post office, because that's what the sign actually says. Dialogue follows the speaker. Both spellings appear in roman type, never italics.

## How the Rest of the Region Says Singee Words

English mouths do four things to Singee words. They pull the stress to the first syllable. They turn unstressed vowels to *uh*. They make a word-initial I into *eye*. And they drop the edges.

So nearly everyone in the region says EYE-sah-loon for Isalune, LUN-suh-loo for Lunsalu and NUR-vin for Nurven. Masirua survives best, as mah-suh-ROO-uh, because its stress already falls where English likes it. A Singee speaker hears all of these the way a Spanish speaker hears Los Angeles said in English, and has long since stopped correcting it out loud.

## The First New Words

Three words beyond the canon five were coined to show the sounds at work. They are now canon.

**xalhi** (XA-lhee), fog. In particular, it's the fog that lives in the South Divide and doesn't burn off. The rasp at the front gives it grit.

**t'una** (T'OO-nah), cedar. It opens with a popped T.

**k'avi** (K'AH-vee), rain. It opens with a popped K.

## Where Singee's Sound Comes From

Singee is invented, and it belongs to the region's own people. Its edge sounds (the catch, the popped consonants, the breathy L and the rasp from the back of the mouth) are common across many languages of the Pacific Northwest. Singee shares that broad family resemblance so it sounds like it grew on this ground. It takes no words, no sound system and no alphabet from any real language. Its four vowels, its soft word endings and its stress on the root are its own. Writing a revived language in plain keyboard letters follows the practical choice many real revival programs have made, and the real peoples of this ground, and their languages, stay their own.
