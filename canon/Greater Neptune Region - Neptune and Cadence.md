# The Greater Neptune Region: Neptune and Cadence

*Two cities at opposite ends of the Causeway: neighborhoods, working waterfronts, hospitals, ballparks and the ways in and out*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to *The Greater Neptune Region — Master Reference v3*, *Road Atlas*, *The Twentieth Century*, *The University of Greater Neptune*, *Politics*, *Neptune English* and *Low Water*. The Master Reference governs every shared fact. All new civic names and local details below are established public-register canon. A novel owns its own characters and local strangeness.

## How to Read the Two Cities

Neptune is the coast's working capital: 143,850 people on the real ground of Florence and the Siuslaw mouth, changed by the fictional Neptune River into a deep-water harbor. Cadence is the dry-side city: 61,240 people spread through the Suttle Lake basin and the Black Butte flats west of real Sisters. The same Causeway reaches both, from its brass plate at Mile 0 to Cadence's lake-edge campus at Mile 178 and its town center at Mile 181. The regional sign is at Mile 189; Sisters lies outside it.

Neither city speaks for the whole region. Neptune controls docks, forms, money and the Authority's public face. Cadence controls the crossing of weather: snow coming off Santiam Pass, tourists coming through the pines, and the homes being built faster than the wells can be settled. Neptune looks west over moving water. Cadence looks west at a road that may close.

Both are ordinary cities with schools, rent, bus routes, late-shift workers, arguments over municipal budgets and places where a person can sit through a whole afternoon without thinking about the forest. Any uncanny occurrence belongs to a particular novel or a previously established fixture. These city files create no new deep-register rules.

## Neptune: The Port City

### Its shape on the ground

The city occupies the coastal plain and riverbanks around the Neptune's mouth. The old riverfront and working harbor are close to Mile 0. The Choke, between the jetties, is the dangerous seaward throat. The Causeway runs inland from the harbor, crosses US 101 at Mile 2, and continues toward the Bay line's swing bridge at Mile 4. Old Coast Road leaves north toward the Fetch. The municipal airport lies on dune flats north of the Mile 2 junction.

There is enough river here to carry freighters because the invented Neptune drains a far larger watershed than the real Siuslaw. There is still surf at the bar, a tidal current at the jetties, shoaling along its edges and a coast that shuts down small boats in bad weather. The city's populous footprint extends along the river flats, onto dune-backed terraces and inland beyond the historic Florence-sized center. No new cape, island or mountain is required.

The north bank is where the Bay Yard stood. The south-bank old town faces it across the water. Cannery Slough and the surviving Rivers lie east of the older waterfront; the Harbor Center civic district occupies the western half that was cleared. The city grows more residential inland and on the dunes. None of these neighborhood names alters a road-mile figure in the atlas.

### Neighborhoods and the six wards

Neptune has a citywide-elected mayor and six councilors, each elected from one ward on a nonpartisan ballot. These are voting wards, while the neighborhoods are the names people put on an envelope or use to describe where they grew up. Boundaries are periodically redrawn, but the six seats remain.

A street named after a river of the Gulf South belongs to the Rivers even when the contemporary ward map cuts it differently. The Sabine Street renewal boundary remains legible in pavement, property records and the route of the city tour that never stops there. The city cannot settle the Rivers' claim by changing a neighborhood name.

**Old Quay** has the fish-auction breakfast, wet rope on the docks, older brick and wood-fronted stores, and the bar board where crews see whether the Choke is passable. **Pilot Row** consists of boardinghouses turned duplexes and tidy cottages owned by families whose boat names are painted on the mailboxes.

**The Ways** is student country now. UGN's campus green uses the Bay Yard's actual slipways; the mold loft is the student union called the Loft. Its School of Marine and Coastal Science and naval architecture program belong here. **Yard Reach**, farther along the working bank, retains fabrication and small-vessel repair. A tourist who calls the whole bank "the old shipyard" has missed the part still earning wages.

**The Rivers** began in the segregated eastern blocks of Harbor Homes during the war. After the temporary project was dismantled between 1949 and 1952, families bought the lots they had occupied because discriminatory lending and sales practices barred them elsewhere. Red River Street was the shopping strip; most of it disappeared in the renewal. Galilee stands on Sabine Street, east of the demolition line. The Red River Street Committee's demand for land, a fund and an apology remains unresolved. The city's 1998 study is still on a shelf. Neighborhood survival includes kitchens, local clubs, rent increases, church funerals, barbers and new residents whose families arrived last month: it cannot be reduced to the city's debt.

**Harbor Center** has an unusually wide plaza where a block of front doors used to be. The headquarters of the Bioregional Authority opened here in 1980 on land the Authority purchased in 1978. The Authority can truthfully say the land was vacant when it bought it; the city cleared it. Every campaign for the six ward seats eventually comes back to that distinction.

**Dune Bend** is higher, near the hospital, and where coastal storm preparedness goes from a waterfront question to an apartment-building question. **Railbank** has the practice fields, machine shops, the ballpark and a row of houses where outfield lights come through the curtains in summer.

### The Port of Neptune

The **Port of Neptune** is a separate public port district, run by five elected commissioners. The port district manages terminal leases, waterfront land and marine facilities; the city manages city streets and its own waterfront zoning. Federal navigation authorities maintain the authorized channel, and the Coast Guard oversees marine safety. Neither the port commissioners nor the Authority's Nine are members of the city council.

The waterfront has three working faces:

- **The Choke and the pilot station.** Incoming deep-draft vessels take a bar pilot on the conditions that day. Tugs work the current, and a scheduled ship can wait outside while a dangerous swell or visibility holds the entrance. Pilots do not treat the river's great volume as a guarantee against the sea.
- **Lower Harbor.** Small commercial fishing boats, crabbers, ice, bait, fuel, gear shops and the port's still-operating fish-processing plant. The old canneries along Cannery Slough closed in the 1960s; this plant is on the working port, and there is no contradiction between those facts.
- **North Terminal.** Break-bulk and bulk freight, timber and engineered-wood products, project cargo, repair materials and the night freight brought in on the Bay line. A working port of this scale has cranes, sheds and marine rail connections. It is no great container megaport. Ships, not sightseeing boats, have priority at its commercial berths.

The channel scours because of the Neptune's volume, while sand at the entrance and the turning basin still demand surveying and periodic dredging. The Port has a dredge-disposal argument whenever a permit comes up; fishing families, waterfront tenants and the people studying estuary habitat at UGN come to the hearings. A strike by terminal workers delays trucks inland as far as the Waist. An unsafe bar means a missed fishing market, not merely a cancelled pleasure cruise.

**The Bay line** opened to the port in 1916 and crosses the river at the 1914 swing bridge at Mile 4. The bridge still turns for vessels. The trains mostly run at night. A person sleeping beside the river recognizes three noises: a horn, the metallic fuss of the bridge, and then a long string of cars moving on.

### The Bay Yard and what became of it

The **Neptune Bay Shipbuilding Corporation** laid its first keel in March 1942 on the north-bank flats. From 1942 to 1946 it produced **141 Liberty ships and 38 Victory ships**, employing about **22,000 people** at its peak. Recruiters worked Shreveport, Houston, Beaumont and Pine Bluff; the yard trains brought the people who built the modern city, including the Black families who made the Rivers. In 1943, eighty-four Black workers were fired over the Boilermakers' segregated auxiliary and most regained their jobs after complaints to the federal Fair Employment Practice Committee. Those events are public history, recorded in *The Twentieth Century*.

The yard launched its last ship in 1945 and closed in 1946. It did **not** reopen as a mass-production shipyard. The north bank now has three distinct uses: the university campus on the old principal ways, a preserved industrial frontage used for smaller repair and fabrication beyond it, and port cargo operations beyond that. The campus's **Ways** lawn is a series of graded former launch slopes; the **Loft** retains the name and shell of the mold loft. Old shipbuilding trades survive in apprenticeships at Neptune Bay Community College and in the Yard Reach shops. A repair contract can mean forty jobs in a wet February; it cannot mean wartime employment again.

The Bay Yard is an argument about who gets commemorated. The campus has plaques about naval innovation and women welders. The Rivers keeps other lists: the auxiliary firings, work crews, addresses lost to renewal and people who never made a plaque. The city can hold both records without claiming the histories reconcile themselves.

### Neptune Bay Medical Center

**Neptune Bay Medical Center** is the city's main general hospital, on the higher ground of Dune Bend. It has a twenty-four-hour emergency department, inpatient medicine and surgery, maternity care, dialysis, respiratory services and a coastal injury service that knows fishing hooks, deck machinery, cold-water immersion and chainsaws. It is the main clinical partner of UGN's nursing programs. Community clinics operate in several neighborhoods, including an outpatient clinic on the Rivers side of town.

The building grew in stages from a postwar community hospital into a modern regional center. Its emergency operations plan keeps coastal inundation, the river floodplain and a prolonged US 101 closure separate: a storm can spare the hospital itself while closing the roads that reach it. The helipad moves patients when the weather permits. The highest-specialty cases still go to the larger Eugene–Springfield medical network outside the region; helicopter or highway transfers depend on weather and access.

A shift-change scene here is ordinary city life: a respiratory therapist waiting for a ride, a cook with a to-go carton, a marine casualty arriving while the taxi drivers argue about which bridge approach is backed up. The hospital's name and capacity are inventions; they should never be mistaken for Peace Harbor Medical Center in real Florence.

### Railbank Field and the Neptune Pilots

**Railbank Field** is the city's ballpark, built on a raised former freight-storage parcel in Ward 6, clear of the working terminal. It has a covered old grandstand, modest modern seating, a manually operated inning board saved from an earlier renovation, and sight lines to the rail approach and port cranes. The sea wind changes the flight of a ball; the first-base seats lose the afternoon sun before the third-base seats. The park can hold several thousand people, but its regular games feel like neighborhood evenings.

The **Neptune Pilots** are a summer wood-bat club, whose roster changes as college players arrive and leave. High schools use the field for selected championship games; UGN's Ospreys have their own college athletics identity and sometimes rent the field. The Pilots' concession stand sells chowder in cold months at community events and hot dogs in July. A person from the Rivers can have a longtime bleacher tradition here without being required to forgive Harbor Center.

The team, park and their history are invented. Their purpose is to give the city a secular summer gathering place, something younger children can remember differently from their grandparents, and a reason for two neighbors who dislike one another to sit under the same roof.

### Neptune Municipal Airport

**Neptune Municipal Airport** is the small city field on dune flats a mile north of the Causeway's Mile 2 junction, on the ground of real Florence Municipal Airport. It is a regional field with **commuter turboprop flights to Portland and Seattle a few times a day**, general aviation, charter flights and urgent cargo. It is **not** a major airport or a jet hub. The airport road connects to the existing highway junction; no new Causeway mileposts are introduced.

A stubborn marine layer makes early departures unreliable, particularly in **July and August**, when the fog is famously worse for flying than in winter. The airline will bus stranded passengers or rebook them, depending on the carrier and the day. The port may be working while the runway is invisible. The weather station is important to fishing boats and schools as well as pilots.

Eugene Airport remains the main airport for the region's west side, outside its border. Portland International is the long-haul gateway. The *Road Atlas* governs all times to those fields.

### How Neptune is governed and what it fights about

Six ward councilors and the citywide mayor work with a separate port commission, the county and the Bioregional Authority. The Authority controls the regional overlay and shared institutions; it does not run the city's garbage pickup. The city's recurring fights are the working port versus housing on the river, tenant displacement on the dune terraces, hospital access during storms, freight noise, and the still-unsettled Rivers renewal.

In the 1930s the city was a Republican lumber port. From the wartime shipyard through the postwar decades it developed a strong union and Democratic electorate; in presidential elections it has voted Democratic since 1988. The contemporary council ballot is nonpartisan, with each ward's interests more useful to a scene than a permanent party label. No present-day officeholder is fixed here.

**At street level:** Neptune smells different depending on the wind: salt, wet cedar, diesel at the terminal, hot fish grease from a harbor window, or rain on sand. A child hears gulls and freight horns. A university student crosses the old ways unaware that a grandmother in the Rivers knows exactly which one her father welded on.

## Cadence: The Pass City

### Its shape on the ground

Cadence occupies the **Suttle Lake basin and the Black Butte flats**, west of Sisters on the dry side of the crest. Its west end and the UGN Cadence campus are at **Causeway Mile 178**, and the center of town is at **Mile 181**. The Rim Line Road, Forest Road 51, leaves south there. The **regional boundary is at Mile 189**, with real Sisters immediately outside. The Black Butte ground is real: pine flats, volcanic soils, cold nights and abrupt changes between sheltered forest and open country. There is no urban shoreline built over the lake and no invented pass through the mountain.

Cadence began as a ranch-and-timber crossroads. The ski bowl at Santiam Pass opened in 1938, the highway over the pass in 1939, and postwar visitors made it a four-season place. Central Cascades State College opened in **1966**, later becoming **UGN Cadence (UGNC)**. The city grew among outfitters, mill families, students, hospital staff, ski workers, retirees, fire crews and commuters to Bend. Its westbound half of the view is a road toward the wet country. Its eastbound half is subdivision signs and the last minutes before Sisters.

### Neighborhoods: lake to rim

Cadence elects its **mayor and six councilors at large**, without party labels. It does not have Neptune's ward system. Neighborhood names help a writer place a resident without inventing county governments.

| Area | Place in the city | On the street |

| **Old Pass** | The early commercial center near Mile 181 | The original main street, taverns, a former mill-office building now used for city meetings, the post office and the pass-condition board. |
| **Mill Creek Shops** | Older service and contractor yards behind Old Pass | Tire shops, snowplow depots, former timber-work buildings, welding, chain sales and the road crews that leave before sunrise. |

The same city address may fall in **Jefferson or Deschutes County**. The county boundary crosses Cadence; its city offices, police, schools and municipal ordinances remain one city's. The county courthouses are outside the region, in **Madras and Bend**. New houses do not get extra votes on city council for being in one county or the other.

**Old Pass** is a place of practical notices: roads open or closed, a lost dog, a fundraiser for a crew member, the school district's smoke-day decision. **Lakeward** can feel like a college city in October until the snow arrives and everyone begins checking Santiam Pass. **Juniper Street** is the sort of rental address a forestry student can give without explaining it to anyone; it preserves the established *Tithe* connection to UGNC without bringing a character into another novel.

**North Butte** and **Eastline** reveal the city's class line. Old residents need reliable wells, snow-cleared roads and insurance they can afford; newer buyers want pine views, trails and a quiet place to work remotely. Both groups use the same grocery checkout.

### UGNC and the town's other anchors

UGNC's approximately **8,200 students** anchor the west edge of Cadence. Its School of Forestry and Fire works around summer fire assignments and allows crew members to begin autumn classes late, a courtesy its chemistry department does not extend. The campus's **fifty-meter pool** is the only one within the Greater Neptune Region, and club swimmers and water-polo players arrive before dawn from across the high country. Its Lookouts compete in fire orange and charcoal. The town's public library is busy with students and retirees; the school district runs smoke-day protocols alongside snow-day protocols.

The city has a public works yard, a winter road crew, a fire-and-rescue service trained for highway crashes and wildland interface calls, and a seasonal transit system joining campus, Old Pass, the medical center and the new housing. The bus is worth more to a student in February than a ski pass. The town's hospitality work is real work: breakfast cooks, lift mechanics, laundry crews, trail crews and hotel reception staff whose seasons rarely line up.

### Cadence Basin Medical Center

**Cadence Basin Medical Center** is the city's general hospital, established before the college arrived in 1966 and expanded as the high-country population grew. It serves the Suttle Lake basin, the pass and surrounding smaller settlements. It has a twenty-four-hour emergency department, inpatient and maternity services, respiratory care, rehabilitation and a winter-sports/trauma service that handles fractures, crashes, frostbite, heat illness and wildfire smoke exposure. UGNC nursing students train there. The ambulance bay is covered because in February the gurney wheels and the snowplow have to share the same morning.

The hospital can stabilize the gravely injured and arrange specialist transfers to Bend or the Eugene–Springfield system. Which direction is possible depends on the pass, the patient and the weather. A medical helicopter may use the hospital pad in suitable conditions. The institution is large enough to be a city employer and small enough that night-shift workers recognize one another at the grocery store. *Low Water* already places a medical center and its night shift here; the name supplies the missing civic detail without creating a new person.

### Switchback Field and the Cadence Switchbacks

**Switchback Field** sits in the **Juniper Flats** recreation grounds, sheltered by ponderosa pines with a view toward the pass when the air is clear. Its bleachers are smaller and more open than Neptune's Railbank Field, with a concession hut that shuts early on school nights. A paved walking loop circles the park, used by older residents at dawn and by teenagers after practice.

The **Cadence Switchbacks** are a local summer baseball club. Little League, school baseball and city softball use the adjoining diamonds; the club plays some evening games under lights. The first snowfall ends the season well before the calendar would elsewhere, and a bad smoke week can cancel games in August. The city holds an annual youth tournament that fills motels with families who spend their money at Old Pass before leaving. The park and team are original civic institutions, not claims about real Sisters sports.

UGNC's **Lookouts** belong to the university. A Switchbacks cap is a town allegiance; a Lookouts sweatshirt is a campus one. Residents can wear both.

### The airport Cadence uses

**Cadence has no major airport and no invented city airport.** Its scheduled-air-service gateway is **Redmond Airport (Roberts Field)**, **35 minutes** away **through real Sisters**, outside the regional boundary. The road north and east remains US 20 and the outside network described in the *Road Atlas*. The hospital's helipad is for medical access, not a municipal aviation terminal.

Eugene Airport serves visitors approaching from the west when the pass and their plans allow it; Portland International serves longer itineraries. A traveler who books a flight to "Cadence" has to explain which actual airport they mean. The city's visitor bureau shows Redmond first. In the Nine-Week Winter of **1993**, Santiam Pass closed to the wet side for nine weeks, but the city retained the long eastern way through Sisters and Bend. People still call that winter the time they learned which relatives owned a truck.

### Water, fire and the city's growth

The political hinge in Cadence is **water**. Wells and springs in Central Oregon exist in a connected groundwater-and-stream system; a new subdivision's pumping is not magically independent of the streams and people downstream. Cadence's water department keeps well records and conservation rules, state water-right decisions matter, and development applications bring hydrologists to rooms full of angry homeowners. The city does not have an invented reservoir sufficient to resolve the dispute.

The other hinge is **fire**. Ponderosa and lodgepole forest on the urban edge needs defensible space and maintained evacuation access. New residents want to keep their screening pines. Experienced fire workers want apparatus access and cleared eaves. Neither position is solved by a slogan. In the smoke months, school sports, the hospital and UGNC's practical training all change schedules.

Cadence's political history is dated. A conservative ranch-and-pass town through much of the twentieth century, it grew more mixed with the college and newcomers, and moved toward Democratic presidential voting in **2020 and 2024**, while the surrounding Rim did not move with it. Its mayor and at-large council still appear without party labels on the ballot. The Crest-zone Authority race is often close. No contemporary mayor, legislator or water-board chair is fixed here.

**At street level:** January is dry cold under high clouds, parking-lot gravel frozen in the ruts, and pine sap on someone's work gloves. July is sun on dark needles until the wind shifts and makes smoke the subject of every conversation. A student can hear the pass report while scraping frost off a windshield; a nurse can finish a shift and see the mountains standing clear to the west.

The *Road Atlas* remains the sole source for intercity distances and driving times. On a normal summer day, **Neptune to Cadence is 3 hours 15 minutes and 155 miles by the Old Grade**, versus **3 hours 45 minutes and 181 miles by the Causeway**. The road is longer by design. A book may choose one route for weather, work or family reasons, but the numbers stay fixed.

Neither city has a passenger rail station. Neptune's Bay line is freight; Cadence is reached by road. The nearest intercity passenger stations are outside the region. Both towns receive the midnight-to-four radio fixture *Low Water*; their callers know the world by different kinds of road and weather.

## Scenes These Places Can Carry

A young dockworker in Neptune leaves a night shift, watches the swing bridge open for a boat and misses the first pitch at Railbank Field. The delay is mechanical and ordinary. An organizer at Galilee asks for the 1998 study while a council candidate tries to describe a plaza as redevelopment. A nursing student eating breakfast at the Loft learns her grandmother worked on one of the ships the university now names its scholarships after.

A Cadence student leaves a forestry fire assignment late and walks into a chemistry lab whose missed work has not been excused. A respiratory therapist phones home from the covered ambulance bay during an ice storm. A North Butte homeowner supports more housing until the next well permit is on the agenda. A youth baseball coach calls off the tournament because the smoke is too thick to see the far fence. Each conflict comes from the city's ordinary public world.

## Real Ground and Invented Ground

**Real terrain and systems checked:** Florence's small municipal airport and marine weather; the real Port of Siuslaw's commercial fishing, marina and cargo heritage; federal coastal navigation channels, bar work and dredging; Suttle Lake and Black Butte's forest/pine setting; Deschutes basin groundwater's connection to streamflow; real Oregon forest-interface fire risk; and World War II Portland-area shipbuilding and Vanport's discriminatory housing history. The real Florence hospital is a small critical-access center, a useful reality check on why a fictional city with 143,850 people would require different hospital infrastructure.

**Fictional changes and institutions:** The Neptune River and deep-water harbor; the city's population, footprint, port district and all six wards and neighborhood names; the Bay Yard and its exact production record, retained from established canon; Neptune Bay Medical Center; Railbank Field and the Pilots; Cadence's population and city footprint; its neighborhoods, hospital, field and Switchbacks. These invented civic details are established for this world only. The actual Suttle Lake basin, Florence municipal field, Sisters and other places outside the boundary retain their real identity.
