# The Singee Language: Grammar

*How a Singee sentence is built, and what it makes a speaker say*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3* and *The Singee Language, Sound and Spelling*

## What Every Singee Sentence Says

A fifteen-year-old in East Wild is telling her great-aunt, over FaceTime, that she saw a cougar cross the Divide Road. She gets one word out in Singee, and her great-aunt stops her. "Ni? Or k'e?" Did you see it, or did somebody tell you? The girl admits that her cousin saw it. Then she has to say the whole sentence again, because in Singee you can't say a thing happened without saying how you know.

That's the heart of the grammar. Every Singee statement carries three things English leaves out or leaves optional: what happened, said first; how the speaker knows it; and, when it moved, which way it went by water. Everything else is simple. There's no "the," no he or she, no tense, and very few irregular forms. Singee is a language that is relaxed about almost everything except the truth.

## The Verb Comes First

A Singee sentence opens with what happened. Who did it comes next, then what it was done to.

**K'avi-ni.** *Rained, seen.* It rained, and I saw it.

**Yen-ru-ka-ni sa.** *Went-downriver-finished-seen, her.* She went downriver, and I watched her go.

The hyphens here only show where the pieces join. Written Singee runs them together: k'avini, yenrukani. The verb is always stressed on its root, so however many endings pile up, the voice lands on the front of the word.

When everyone knows who did it, the person drops out entirely. A sentence can be one long verb.

## How You Know: The Five Evidence Endings

Every Singee statement of fact ends its verb with one of five endings. Each one says how the speaker knows.

| **Ending** | **Name** | **What it means** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -ni | seen | I saw it myself. |
| -lha | sensed | I heard it, smelled it or felt it, with any sense but sight. |
| -xun | figured | I worked it out from signs. |
| -k'e | told | Someone told me. |
| -k'e'en | the old telling | It has always been told. It came down to me. |

Rain shows all five at once:

**K'avi-ni.** It rained. I watched it.

**K'avi-lha.** It rained. I heard it on the roof.

**K'avi-xun.** It rained. The road's wet.

**K'avi-k'e.** It rained, my brother says.

**K'avi-k'e'en.** It rained, as the story has always gone. This is how the old flood stories open.

Questions and commands take no ending, because they don't claim anything. An answer does. Ask a Singee speaker "Did it rain?" and a polite answer can't just be yes. It's "K'avi-lha": yes, I heard it.

### What the endings do in a story

**They make lying specific.** In English you can say "the truck was in the lot that night" and stay vague about how you know. In Singee you have to pick an ending, and choosing -ni when you didn't see it is a particular kind of lie that every listener recognizes. A Singee character can be technically honest and deeply evasive at once, simply by choosing -k'e.

**They sort gossip from history.** A Causeway legend told in Singee takes -k'e: somebody says. Whether a story has been around long enough to deserve -k'e'en is a real family argument. The same legend can be -k'e in a teenager's mouth and -k'e'en in her grandfather's, and the difference is the whole argument between them.

**They change what "I" means.** With *na*, "I," the seen ending means you did something on purpose, because you watched yourself choose it. The sensed ending means it happened to you.

**Suli-ka-ni na.** I let myself fall. I jumped.

**Suli-ka-lha na.** I fell. I didn't mean to.

Singee speakers fall asleep, fall ill and fall in love with -lha.

**They make the future modest.** You can't have seen what hasn't happened yet. So anything not yet done takes only *figured* or *told*. Saying a future thing with -ni is either a prophecy or a joke, and which one depends on who says it.

**They got lost in translation.** At the 1969 Causeway hearings, the elders gave their history of the route with -k'e'en on every verb: this is how it has always been told. The interpreter put it into English as plain fact. The federal record is more certain than the testimony was, and the Nation's lawyers have been grateful and uneasy about that ever since.

## Done, Still Going, Not Yet

Singee verbs have no past, present or future. They say only whether an action is finished, still going, or not yet begun, and words like *yesterday* or *next winter* do the rest.

| **Ending** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- |
| no ending | still going, or always so |
| -ka | finished |
| -ya | not yet; meant, coming, about to |

**Yen-ka-ni sa.** She left. I saw her go.

**Yen-ya-xun sa.** She's going to leave. I can tell.

The pieces always come in the same order: the root, then the water direction if the verb moves, then done or not-yet, then how you know.

## Which Way: The Water Directions

The Singee never give directions by compass. They give them by water. Any verb of motion can take one of three direction endings:

| **Ending** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- |
| -ru | downstream, toward the sea |
| -lhun | upstream, inland, into the forest |
| -sa | across a river or a divide |

**Yen-lhun-ka-ni nan.** We went upriver into the woods, not you. I was there.

Because directions follow the water, the same compass direction gets different words in different drainages. On the Neptune, downstream is west. In the South Divide, where the Middle Fork runs north and west to the valley, downstream bends another way. At Isalhune, where the water parts, the directions flip from one side of the saddle to the other. Singee speakers from the Hush say which side they grew up on before they give directions, the way you'd name your time zone before giving a time.

The deep register's two names are built from these same pieces. Masiru'a is *ru*, seaward, made into the thing that pulls. Lhunsalu is *lhun*, inward, made into the whole of the holding. A Singee child learns the names of the pull and the hold the way an English-speaking child learns that *orient* once meant the east: as ordinary words that turned out to be old.

## People: I, You, and Two Kinds of We

Singee has no he and no she. It has one word for any third person, and it splits that word in two: one for someone who is here, and one for someone who isn't.

| **Singee** | **English** |
| --- | --- |
| na | I |
| ti | you, one person |
| sa | he, she or they, someone here |
| xe | he, she or they, someone not here |
| kin | we, including you |
| nan | we, not including you |
| tin | you, more than one |
| san | they, here |
| xen | they, not here |

**Here and not here.** "Not here" covers someone across town, someone overseas and someone dead. Singee speakers talk about the dead with *xe* forever. Using *sa* for someone who has died, as if they're in the room, is a ghost story told in one syllable.

**Kin and nan.** The two kinds of we differ by one sound, and they don't sound alike to an outsider who's listening for them. When a Singee elder at an Authority meeting says *nan*, every Singee speaker in the room knows the ranger across the table has just been left out of the sentence. The ranger doesn't. Only one of the regional languages in the standard world survey of grammar draws this line at all, so it's one of the things that makes Singee sound like itself and nothing nearby.

## Nouns: Many, Mine and Not Mine

**No "the."** A Singee noun is just the noun. *T'una* is cedar, a cedar, the cedar.

**Many is said by repeating.** To make a noun plural, repeat the first syllable of its root: *t'una*, a cedar, becomes *t'ut'una*, cedars, a stand of them. The stress stays on the root, so it's t'u-T'U-na. Singee only bothers when the number matters. *Xaxalhi* isn't "fogs." It's fog everywhere, fog in every drainage at once.

**Two ways to own something.** Singee has a close way of saying *mine* and a loose way.

The close way puts the owner first and adds nothing: **na seni**, my mother. It's for your body, your kin, your name, your home ground and, by an old rule nobody has ever argued with, your dog: **na k'ura**, my dog.

The loose way puts the thing first and adds *u*: **t'una u na**, my cedar, a cedar I own or cut or am about to sell. It's for everything else.

The line between them is the line between what can change hands and what can't. In 1977, the Authority's forms asked Singee translators to put "my land" into Singee, and they had to choose. They used the close form: **na lhuni**, my home ground, a thing you can't sell any more than your mother. The Authority's lawyers spent a year deciding whether that was a translation or an argument. It was both.

## Saying No, Asking, Telling

**No** goes in front of the verb: **Ma yen-lhun-ya-xun kin.** We're not going up into the woods, I'd say.

**A question** adds *hu* at the end and drops the evidence ending: **Yen-ru-ka ti hu?** Did you go downriver?

**A command** is the bare root, with a direction if it moves: **Yen-ru!** Go downriver! Singee commands sound abrupt to English ears. Among speakers they're ordinary, and softening one is a choice.

## How Singee Builds New Words

A handful of pieces build most of the words a story will need. The canon names are made of them.

| **Piece** | **What it does** | **In a word** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -'a | makes a force, or the thing that does an action | ru'a, the pull |
| masi- | deep, from below | Masiru'a, the deep pull |
| -salu | the whole of something, still going | Lhunsalu, the whole holding |
| -i | makes a place or thing from a root | lhuni, home ground |
| i- | the place where | Isalhune |

**Isalhune** is the one canon name whose meaning stays soft. It's *i-*, the place where, plus *sa*, across, plus *lhun*, the hold, plus an old ending *-e* that nobody can define anymore. Elders translate it three ways: "where the crossing holds," "where it's held from both sides," and "the place that keeps both waters." They've never agreed, and the Nation has never needed them to.

**Singi** and **Nuxven** are older than the pieces. Speakers don't take them apart, and the language doesn't need them to.

## Singee on the Page

Singee should rarely appear in a book as more than a word or a short phrase at a time, in roman type, with its meaning made clear by the scene rather than by a footnote or a glossary. A whole Singee sentence is an event, and the scene around it should know that.

Singee speakers' English is never written as broken English. The grammar leaves a different mark. Older speakers often say how they know things, in English, out of habit: "It rained. I heard it." "She's moving to Portland, her sister says." And when they talk about someone who has died, they tend to say "she's not here," rather than "she's gone" or "she passed."

## Where Singee's Grammar Comes From

Singee is invented, and it belongs to the region's own people. It's designed to sit believably among the real languages of this part of the continent without copying any one of them. The *World Atlas of Language Structures* shows what that neighborhood looks like.

Among the languages it samples in Oregon, Washington and southern British Columbia, starting a sentence with the verb is the most common order wherever there's a fixed order at all: nine of the twelve such languages. Worldwide, it's under a tenth of the sample (chapter 81, Dryer). Most of the region's sampled languages mark how the speaker knows something, seventeen of twenty-four, though usually more simply than Singee's five endings (chapters 77 and 78, de Haan). Building plurals and other words by repeating a syllable is productive in nearly all of them (chapter 27, Rubino). The two kinds of *we* are the deliberate exception: only one of the seven regional languages sampled has them (chapter 39, Cysouw).

So Singee's sentence order, its evidence endings and its repeated syllables make it sound like it grew on this ground. Its particular endings, its water directions, its here and not-here, and its two kinds of *we* make it sound like no one else's. It takes no words, no endings and no grammar from any real language.

*Source: Dryer, Matthew S. and Martin Haspelmath (eds.), The World Atlas of Language Structures Online (2013), wals.info, consulted November 2026 through its published data.*
