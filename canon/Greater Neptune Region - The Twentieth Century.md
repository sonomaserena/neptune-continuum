# The Greater Neptune Region: The Twentieth Century

*Rail camps, the Bay Yard and the timber wars, 1900 to 2000*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*, *The Greater Neptune Region, Road Atlas*, *The Singee, A History* and *Neptune English, How the Region Talks*

## How to Use This History

This is the region's public record for the hundred years between the first rail camps and the end of the timber wars. It's what the newspapers printed, what the county histories say, what a grandparent remembers and what a teacher could look up. It fills the stretch the Master Reference's timeline used to jump, from the CCC crews of 1936 to the drowned town of 1953, and the stretch after the Christmas Flood when the mills were still running, and then weren't.

It stands on real Oregon. Where a date belongs to the real world, it's the real date: the spruce soldiers of 1918, the Santiam Highway in 1939, the Coast Guard's beach patrols, the owl, the 1994 forest plan. Inside the region, real events wear the region's names, the way the Holiday Farm Fire wears the Labor Day Fire's. The Bay Yard is built on the pattern of Henry Kaiser's shipyards in Portland, and the Rivers on the pattern of what Black shipyard families met in Oregon, but the yard and the neighborhood are Neptune's own.

Three things stay out. The Singee's century has its own history, *The Singee, A History*, and this file only points to it. Mirth's century belongs to Mirth and lives in *Mirth, Encyclopedia Entry* in the Tithe's repository, so this file repeats only the public dates the rest of the region would know. And the deep register gets no new rule. The timber wars section is about what people believed and argued, which is a public fact. What the forest actually does is still set by Part Six of the Master Reference and nowhere else.

## The Rail Years, 1900 to 1929

### Logs by water

In 1900 the land that would be Neptune National Forest was timber claims, homesteads, Singee village sites nobody had written down and a great deal of country nobody had walked. Logs left it by water. On the coast side, fallers dropped trees into the creeks and waited for winter, or built splash dams: log cribs that held back a pond full of cut timber and then let it all go at once, a wall of water and logs roaring down to tidewater. Western Oregon had more than two hundred splash dams between the 1880s and the 1950s, and the region's Coast Range creeks had their share. They scoured the creek beds down to bedrock, and the old-timers on Lake Creek would tell you which pools the salmon stopped using and the year they stopped.

Then came the steam donkey, an upright boiler and a winch bolted to a log sled, which dragged felled trees to a landing by wire rope. Around 1910 loggers added the high lead, rigging the line from the top of a limbed spar tree so a log came in with one end in the air, clear of stumps. It was faster than anything before it and far more dangerous. The donkey and the high lead are where most of the region's logging words come from. The crew that hauled the logs was the yarding crew, the boy who signaled the donkey with a whistle wire was the whistle punk, and the man who fixed the cable around each log was the choker setter, the worst job in the woods.

In 1908 the forest was proclaimed, and rangers came with survey chains and logbooks. The Singee history tells what those logbooks said about the people already living there.

### The Bay line, 1916

The railroad reached the coast in 1916. The line ran from the outside valley at Eugene, down the river through Loki's Weir and across the Neptune to the port, then south down the coast. It's the Bay line, and its swing bridge at Mile 4 went in two years before the first through train, in 1914. A 1916 guide to the new railroad called the town on the real ground of Loki's Weir a center for "dairying, fishing and lumbering," and that held for fifty years.

The railroad made the city of Neptune a lumber port. Before 1916 the harbor shipped what it could on schooners. By the 1920s the north-bank waterfront was a mile of sawmills, a box factory, shingle mills and the canneries along Cannery Slough, and lumber went out through The Choke to California and across the Pacific.

### Camp One through Camp Nine

Nils Strand was a Norwegian-born logger who had worked the Grays Harbor woods in Washington before he came south with a partner's money. In 1909 he bought timber at the head of Lake Creek. For seven years the Strand Timber Company splash-dammed its logs down the creek. When the Bay line came through, Strand built a logging railroad up Lake Creek from a junction at the creek's mouth, where the Lake Creek Turn is now, and started up it in 1917.

A rail camp wasn't a town. It was a string of bunkhouse cars, a cookhouse car, a filing shack and an office car set out on a siding, with the woods being cut all around it. When the timber in reach was gone, every two or three years, the camp was coupled up and pulled farther up the line, and it took the next number. Camps One through Eight went up Lake Creek that way. In 1926 the track reached the head of the creek, where it could climb no farther, and Camp Nine was end of track. Strand built a store and a one-room school there, and frame houses for the married men, and families came.

After the Second World War, trucks did what the railroad had done, cheaper and on steeper ground. Strand pulled up its rails in 1948, and the camp stayed. It kept its number because nobody ever filed another one. The upper miles of Forest Road 14 still follow the old grade. Strand sold its land and its camp to a Portland plywood company in 1956, and the hills around Camp Nine were logged by truck, by the company and then by gyppo outfits on contract, until the timber wars. A choker setter from Camp Nine was still setting chokers in the 1970s and playing lap steel at Grange dances on Saturday nights.

### The spruce soldiers, 1918

In the First World War the airplanes of the day were built of Sitka spruce, and the best of it grew on the central Oregon coast. In 1917 the Industrial Workers of the World struck the Northwest's camps and mills for an eight-hour day. The Army answered with the Spruce Production Division, which put close to thirty thousand soldiers into the woods and mills, and with a government-backed union, the Loyal Legion of Loggers and Lumbermen, the 4L. Together they broke the strike.

In the spring of 1918, spruce soldiers came to the coast just north of Wrack. In a matter of months they cut their own ties, built trestles and laid a railroad from just north of the village up the coast toward the big spruce mill the Army was building at Toledo, outside the region. The war ended that November before much spruce came down the line, and by January 1919 the soldiers were gone. The grade is still there in the brush north of Wrack. People walk dogs on it and mostly don't know what it is.

### The Cascade line and the North Fork

The other railroad came up the Middle Fork. Track reached East Wild in 1912, and then a federal antitrust suit against the railroad stopped work for twelve years, and East Wild sat at the end of a dead-end branch. Construction resumed in the mid-1920s, and the line over Willamette Pass was completed on September 1, 1926. It's the Cascade line, now the main line between Portland and California. Eastbound trains needed helper engines to climb from East Wild to the summit, so the Wilds became a railroad town, with roundhouse crews, a division point and boarding houses for train crews between runs.

The North Fork Lumber Company built the region's first company town at Nela Junction in 1923, mill and store and houses together, on the pattern of the real Western Lumber Company at Westfir. Its logging railroad ran up the North Fork's west bank about twenty miles into Veilwood's valley. That rail grade is the one beside Nela Road today, which is why the only road into Veilwood runs where it does.

### Dated politics: the 1920s

On November 7, 1922, Oregon voters passed an initiative requiring every child to attend public school. Its sponsors were Masons, its loudest supporters were the Ku Klux Klan, and its targets were Catholic schools. The Klan was a power in Oregon that year, and it had klaverns in the valley towns of the Waist. The region's Catholics were mostly in the canyon towns and among the Italian and Irish rail crews of the Wilds, and they remember who voted how. The Supreme Court struck the law down on June 1, 1925, in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, before it ever took effect.

On November 2, 1926, Oregon voters finally repealed the clause in the state constitution that had barred Black people from living in Oregon since statehood. It had been dead law since the Fourteenth Amendment, and it had stayed on the books anyway. Fifteen years later, the Bay Yard would bring Black families to a city that had never wanted them.

## The Lean Years, 1929 to 1941

The Depression closed most of the region's mills at least part of the time. Strand ran Camp Nine half the year and let the cookhouse go dark the other half. In August 1933 the Tillamook Burn, far to the north and outside the region, killed the timber on some 240,000 acres, most of it in a single day, and every logging family in Oregon knew someone who'd fought it.

The Civilian Conservation Corps came in 1933 and stayed until 1942. Its crews built the ranger stations, fire lookouts and trail shelters that still stand all over the forest, and the forest was consolidated into its two units in 1936, in the middle of their work. Between 1934 and 1936 the state finished the great bridges of the Coast Highway, and US 101 crossed the Neptune at the city on a new drawbridge. The Coast Guard took over the nation's lighthouses in 1939, and the keeper families at Ichabod's Eye became Coast Guard families without moving out.

In 1930 Eugene's municipal utility finished a dam and power canal on the river at Sweetbriar, its second on the river after a canal it dug by hand in 1911 just downstream. So the hum in Sweetbriar belongs to a city the region doesn't govern, and Sweetbriar has been saying so for ninety years.

The Santiam Highway over the pass, now US 20, was completed in September 1939. A ski bowl had opened at the pass the year before, and Cadence, which had been a ranch-and-timber crossroads on the dry side, started to look west.

By 1938 Oregon had passed Washington as the country's leading lumber state, and it has held that place since.

## The War Years, 1941 to 1945

### The Bay Yard

In 1941 a West Coast construction combine, the kind of company that had built dams in the Depression, took a Maritime Commission contract for cargo ships and chose the city of Neptune for the yard. It was a deep-water port with a railroad, a lumber supply at its back and miles of tide flats on the north bank below the old riverfront. The Neptune Bay Shipbuilding Corporation laid its first keel in March 1942. Everyone called it the Bay Yard.

The Bay Yard built Liberty ships, the plain, slow freighters that carried the war, welded in sections and assembled on the ways in weeks. Later it built the faster Victory ships. By the time it closed in 1946 it had launched 141 Liberty ships and 38 Victory ships. At its peak, in the winter of 1943, it ran three shifts around the clock and employed about twenty-two thousand people, in a city that had held about forty thousand when the war began.

The yard could never find enough workers in Oregon, so it went looking. Its recruiters opened hiring halls in Shreveport, Houston, Beaumont and Pine Bluff, offering wages no sawmill or cotton field in the South would pay and a train ticket west. On the real ground, Kaiser's Portland yards did exactly this, and Portland's Black population went from about 1,800 before the war to an estimated 15,000 in 1946. Neptune's went from fewer than two hundred in 1940 to about seven thousand by the summer of 1944. They came from Louisiana, Texas and Arkansas, mostly, on what the city called the yard trains. Women came too. The yard hired its first women welders in 1942, months after the Portland yards did, and by 1944 one welder in four on the ways was a woman.

### Harbor Homes and the Rivers

The city had nowhere to put any of them. In 1942 its new housing authority built Harbor Homes, a federal war-housing project of thin-walled two-story blocks on the low ground along Cannery Slough, east of the old riverfront. It was meant to be temporary. The authority said the project's sections were assigned by choice of available units. In fact, Black families were steered to the eastern blocks, nearest the slough, the rail yard and the smell of the canneries.

In the fall of 1943 the tenants' council in those eastern blocks named their own streets. The project had numbered them. The council renamed them for the rivers of home: Sabine, Red River, Ouachita, Calcasieu, Trinity and Brazos. People started calling the whole section the Rivers, and the name outlived the project.

Outside Harbor Homes, the city was closed. The Neptune Realty Board had adopted the national realtors' code of 1924, which told an agent never to introduce members of a race into a neighborhood if their presence would hurt property values, and the board's members read it the way Portland's did: no sales to Black buyers anywhere white people lived. Banks lent accordingly. A Black welder with a paycheck larger than his foreman's couldn't buy a house in the city of Neptune, and couldn't rent one either, except in the Rivers.

### Galilee

Galilee Missionary Baptist Church began in October 1942, in the Harbor Homes recreation hall, with a shipfitter from Lake Charles, Louisiana, named Emmett Batiste preaching on Sunday mornings after a Saturday night shift. Its name was the congregation's choice: a city of shipbuilders and fishermen, a sea in the gospels, Jesus calling men off their boats. By 1944 the hall couldn't hold the congregation. In 1948 the members built their own church on Sabine Street, mostly with their own hands and with lumber bought board by board.

Galilee became what Black churches in the region's new city had to be: a church, a hiring hall, a credit union, a bail fund, a place to register voters and the address on every letter to Washington. It still stands on Sabine Street, on the east side, and the reason it stands there is told below.

### The auxiliary

The Bay Yard was a closed shop. Every worker had to belong to the craft union for his trade, and the biggest was the Boilermakers. The Boilermakers didn't admit Black members. They put them in a separate auxiliary, as the union did at the Portland yards, which took Black workers' dues, gave them no vote and no hall, and offered fewer protections. In the summer of 1943, more than three hundred Black workers at the Kaiser yards were fired for refusing to join the auxiliary. That same summer the Bay Yard fired eighty-four of its own for the same refusal.

Galilee kept the list. Reverend Batiste and a committee of welders took the men's complaints to the President's Fair Employment Practice Committee, which was taking complaints from the Portland yards that year. By the winter most of the eighty-four were back on the ways. The auxiliary stayed until the war ended.

### The families who were taken

On February 19, 1942, President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, and the Army declared the western half of Oregon a military area from which every person of Japanese ancestry would be removed. The region's Japanese American families, truck farmers on the Waist and a few families who worked the cannery line on the slough, reported that May to the assembly center at the Portland stockyards, and most were sent on to the camp at Minidoka, in Idaho. Some came back after the war and found their land sold and their houses lived in. Some never came back. Some families who had never lived in the region came after the war, bought land on the Waist from people who were leaving and stayed.

### Watching the coast

On the night of June 21, 1942, a Japanese submarine shelled Fort Stevens at the mouth of the Columbia. That September a floatplane from the same submarine dropped firebombs on the forest near Brookings. Both were far outside the region, and both put the coast on alert. In May 1945, a Japanese balloon bomb killed a pregnant woman and five children on a church picnic near Bly, in southern Oregon, the only deaths from enemy action on the American mainland during the war.

From early 1943 to July 1944, Ichabod's Eye was a Coast Guard beach patrol station, as the real Heceta Head was. As many as seventy-five coastguardsmen lived at the light, and with them came dogs, trained back East for the Coast Guard's patrols. Every night, two-man patrols walked the sand of The Fetch with a dog on a short leash, looking for submarines, saboteurs and signal lights, from the Eye up to Wrack and back. Grandparents on The Fetch still talk about the dogs.

Inland, the fire lookouts doubled as aircraft warning posts. Civilian volunteers had been watching the sky since the summer of 1941, and in 1942 the lookouts went on round-the-clock watch, reporting every plane by phone. The Army put the volunteer service on reserve in October 1943.

Just outside the line north of Wrack, four miles south of Waldport, the federal Civilian Public Service ran Camp Angel, a work camp for conscientious objectors, from October 1942 to the spring of 1946. The men fought fires and did forest work on the coast. The camp's Fine Arts Group printed poetry on a hand press and put on plays, and the coast towns weren't sure what to make of any of it.

### The woods at war

The war wanted lumber, and the national forests sold it. On the real ground, the Willamette National Forest's cut went from 44 million board feet in 1940 to 144 million in 1944, and the Cascade Unit followed the same curve. The war is where the long boom began.

## The Long Boom, 1945 to 1979

### The cut

The postwar building boom ran on Northwest lumber and plywood, and the region's timber towns had the best thirty years they would ever have. Big mills came to the timber. The Canyon Fir Lumber Company built a sawmill at Mirth in 1946, and the Marlow Pacific Lumber Company bought timber above East Wild that year and had a large mill running there by 1948, with more than five hundred people on the payroll. Plywood plants went up in the city of Neptune and in the Wilds.

The work changed. Heavy trucks had been taking over from logging railroads since the 1930s, and by 1960 the logging railroads were nearly gone. Steam donkeys gave way to diesel yarders after the war, and the one-man chainsaw replaced the two-man crosscut. Oregon's harvest crested in the 1950s and 1960s and stayed high until the end of the 1980s. More and more of it came off federal land, and in 1963, for the first time, western Oregon's loggers cut more timber on federal land than on private. Timber towns came to depend on the Forest Service's sale schedule the way farm towns depend on rain.

### The Rivers after the war

The Bay Yard launched its last ship in the fall of 1945 and closed in 1946. About half the families in the Rivers left, for California, for Portland, or home. The other half stayed, and they had one place to stay. When the housing authority tore down Harbor Homes between 1949 and 1952, the eastern blocks were the only lots in the city that a realtor would sell to a Black buyer. The families bought the ground under their old apartments, and kept the street names. The Rivers became a neighborhood of small houses built in the early 1950s, with a business strip on Red River Street: barbershops, a café, a funeral home, the Elks lodge, a record store and a pharmacy.

Oregon's law caught up slowly. The Public Accommodations Act of 1953 made it illegal for restaurants and hotels to turn Black customers away, and the signs in the windows downtown came down that summer. The state passed a fair housing law in 1957. In 1959, on the eve of its centennial, Oregon ratified the Fifteenth Amendment, eighty-nine years after it became law.

### The renewal

In 1961 the city of Neptune won federal urban renewal money for what it called the Harbor Center project. The plan cleared twenty-seven blocks of what the city's own appraisers called blight, which was the western half of the Rivers, from the slough to Sabine Street. Demolition ran from 1963 to 1966. The renewal took 412 households and 61 businesses, nearly all of Red River Street. Owners were paid appraised values, in a market where the realty board's own rules had held those values down for twenty years. The plan promised replacement housing for every displaced family, and none was ever built.

The renewal line ran down the middle of Sabine Street. Galilee was on the east side. The congregation has always said the line was drawn there on purpose, because the city didn't want to condemn a church in an election year, and the city has always said it wasn't.

On the cleared land the city built a new city hall in 1967, a civic auditorium and a parking structure. Most of the rest stood empty for a decade. In 1978 the city sold it to the newly chartered Bioregional Authority, which opened its headquarters there in 1980. The civic district where the Authority lives is the western half of the Rivers. The Authority's position, which is accurate as far as it goes, is that it bought vacant land.

In 1992 Rivers families who had lost homes and businesses to the renewal formed the Red River Street Committee and asked the city council for a reckoning: a fund, land and an apology. In 1996 the council voted four to three to study the question. The study was finished in 1998 and has been on a shelf ever since. The city hasn't paid. Every council candidate in the city of Neptune gets asked about the study at the Galilee forum, and most of them say they've read it.

### Storms, dams and roads

The Corps of Engineers began the dam below Sump Creek in 1949, and in 1953 the reservoir rose over the old town. On the real ground, about two hundred people in Detroit were moved for the same dam.

The Big Blow of October 12, 1962, knocked down eleven billion board feet of timber or more across northern California, Oregon and Washington in less than a day. The Coast Unit was flattened in places, Camp Nine logged nothing but blowdown for three years, and salvage logs went out through the harbor to Japan. Two years later the Christmas Flood took out the old river highway's low grades.

The Coast Guard automated the light at Ichabod's Eye in July 1963, and the last keeper family moved away that summer. Interstate 5 was finished through Oregon in 1966, and Amity Gate became the crossroads it is.

Then came the years when Oregon wrote its land into law. The Beach Bill of 1967 made the state's beaches public from the water to the dune line, which is why the sand of The Fetch belongs to everyone. The Causeway was realigned in 1969, and the Master Reference says what can be said about that. The Forest Practices Act of 1971 set rules for logging on public and private land alike. Senate Bill 100 of 1973 made every city and county in Oregon plan its land. The Nurven Nation was recognized that December, and in 1977 the region was chartered around the Nation's status, with co-stewardship written in.

## The Bust, 1979 to 1989

In October 1979 the bottom fell out of the wood-products market. Over the next three years lumber prices dropped by more than 48 percent. Ninety-three mills closed in Oregon in 1980 alone. By the time the national economy recovered in the mid-1980s, forty-eight thousand lumber jobs in the Northwest were gone, most of them for good, and mills that reopened did it with machines that needed fewer people.

North Fork Lumber closed its Nela Junction mill in 1984, and Nela Junction stopped being a company town and became the last place to turn around. Marlow Pacific shut its East Wild mill in 1987. Camp Nine's gyppo outfits kept working, smaller every year.

The 1977 charter added a fight of its own. Inside the co-stewardship overlay, the Authority couldn't approve a timber sale without the Nation at the table. Most of the overlay was in the South Divide, and the Nation's council voted down every old-growth sale put in front of it. Timber families in the Wilds blamed the Nation and the Authority for the slow sales schedule. Some still do, and the casino, when it opened in 1996, didn't help.

At the end of the decade Congress briefly pushed federal timber sales back up. On the real ground, a 1989 rider called Section 318 sent the Willamette National Forest's sales to nearly a billion board feet in 1990, the last big year anyone in the region would see.

## The Timber Wars, 1989 to 2000

### What happened

The northern spotted owl nests in old growth. In June 1990 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed it as threatened. In May 1991 Federal Judge William Dwyer in Seattle stopped federal timber sales in the owl's habitat, which was nearly every national forest west of the Cascade crest, including nearly all of Neptune's. Timber towns put yellow ribbons on their antennas and mailboxes. Log trucks drove in convoys hundreds long through the valley towns with their horns going. Tavern menus offered spotted owl, and the bumper sticker read "Spotted owl tastes like chicken."

On April 2, 1993, President Clinton and Vice President Gore came to Portland for a Forest Conference, and out of it came the Northwest Forest Plan. Its record of decision was signed on April 13, 1994. It set aside most of the remaining old growth on federal land in reserves and cut the federal harvest to less than a quarter of what it had been in the 1980s. Judge Dwyer upheld it on December 21, 1994. Washington sent money: county payments to replace lost timber receipts, and an economic adjustment program for timber towns that, by the Forest Service's own later review, replaced few of the jobs. Across the region the plan covered, about thirty thousand direct timber jobs were lost between 1990 and 2000.

In 1994 the Canyon Fir Lumber Company closed its mill at Mirth, and about a third of the town's jobs went with it. That's in Mirth's own history.

### Fawn Creek

In October 1991 an arsonist nobody ever caught set fire to Fawn Creek, a roadless drainage of old growth above East Wild that had been set aside for the owl. The fire burned about nine thousand acres before the rain came. Within weeks the Forest Service proposed to salvage-log the burn, on the reasoning that dead trees were no use to owls and a great deal of use to mills. Environmental groups appealed, and the sale sat in court.

On July 27, 1995, President Clinton signed a budget bill carrying a salvage rider, which exempted salvage logging from the environmental laws and the lawsuits that enforced them. Fawn Creek was back on the block. That fall, protesters, most of them young and many of them tagless, out of Eugene and Portland, dug a trench across Forest Road 4460, the spur off the Divide Road into the burn, built a log barricade behind it and moved in. They stayed eleven months, through a winter of rain and the '96 flood, in tarps and a cook tent, with East Wild a few miles down the hill. In the summer of 1996 the Secretary of Agriculture ordered an end to salvage logging in roadless areas, and the Fawn Creek sale was cancelled. Soon after, the Forest Service came up the road and cleared the camp. On the real ground, all of this happened at Warner Creek, above Oakridge, almost to the day.

East Wild's opinion of the protesters ran from fury to grudging respect to taking them soup, sometimes in the same household.

### The argument nobody made out loud

Everyone who grows up in the region knows that old growth is wide awake and a clearcut is nearly asleep, the way they know which way the water goes. Nobody teaches it. It comes with the place. That meant the timber wars in Neptune were an argument about something neither side would say in public.

Timber families knew what their grandparents had done. When the first growth came off the hills around Camp Nine, the hills got easier to live beside, and the old-timers said so in kitchens, never in print. A cut was a kind of light left on. Mothers in East Wild let their kids roam the second growth and not the old. So when a logger stood up at a hearing and said he was managing the forest for everyone, some of the people in the room heard a second meaning, and he knew they did.

On the other side, the hearing record was all owls, salmon and watersheds, which were real and which the protesters meant. But plenty of people who stood at the Fawn Creek barricade, and plenty of locals who quietly sent them money, wanted the last old growth left awake for reasons that weren't in any brief. The forest awake was the region's own, the thing no other place had. Every place in the region that mattered to anyone was old growth: the Hush, the terraces at Veilwood, the woods behind the Fold. And some of them, it has to be said, liked the strangeness, and timber families thought liking it was a luxury for people who didn't have to live next to it.

At a 1993 hearing in Amity Gate, a woman from Camp Nine testified for two minutes about jobs and then finished, "We've lived next to the old trees. We know what they're like." The room went quiet. The transcript records the sentence and nothing else, because there was nothing else to record.

There was one more irony, and East Wild enjoyed it. A burn sleeps. Everybody knows that. The protesters were guarding a forest that was already about as quiet as a forest gets, and the loggers were trying to cut it. The protesters' answer, when anyone pushed them on it, was the one the old-timers at Camp Nine had been giving for years: leave it alone, and it wakes back up.

### The Nation's position

Both sides wanted the Nurven Nation on their side, and the Nation declined both. It was a government with a forestry company that thinned second growth on contract and bid on Authority work, and it had members whose fathers had set chokers. In the co-stewardship overlay it went on voting down old-growth sales, and it gave its reasons in the language of a government: watersheds, salmon, cultural sites and its own sovereignty. It gave no others, and nobody who knew the Singee expected it to. Fawn Creek lay outside the overlay, and the Nation said nothing about it at all.

### What the century left

By 2000 the region's biggest employers were hospitals, school districts, the Forest Service, the Authority and the Nation. Sweetbriar's mill survived by retooling in 1991 to cut the small logs of second growth, and it still runs a swing shift. The wigwam burners rusted where they stood. Logging words started to fade from the young, as *Neptune English* says. Camp Nine's last gyppo outfit sold its yarder in 1999, and the Camp stayed anyway, as it had in 1948.

## The Towns Through the Century

**The city of Neptune.** A lumber port from 1916, the Bay Yard's city from 1942 to 1946, and the home of the Rivers, the region's Black neighborhood, since 1943. The civic district, city hall and the Authority's headquarters stand on the western half of the Rivers, cleared from 1963 to 1966.

**Loki's Weir.** A Bay line town since 1916, living on dairying, fishing and lumbering, and flooding.

**Ichabod's Eye.** Lit in 1894, a Coast Guard light from 1939, a beach patrol station with its dogs from early 1943 to July 1944, and automated in July 1963, when the last keeper family left.

**Wrack.** The spruce soldiers' railroad ran north from just outside the village in 1918. The conscientious objectors of Camp Angel worked the coast from just north of it from 1942 to 1946. The Beach Bill of 1967 keeps its shore public.

**Camp Nine.** The Strand Timber Company's end of track from 1926. Rails up in 1948, trucks until the timber wars, and still there.

**Amity Gate.** A farm town on the old Pacific Highway until Interstate 5 was finished in 1966, then the region's front door after 1969 and 1977. In 1993 it held the hearing where the Camp Nine woman testified.

**Honey Hills.** Bee yards and grass-seed fields on the benches. Some of its Japanese American families were taken in 1942, and some new ones came after the war.

**Sweetbriar.** Eugene's power canals of 1911 and 1930 run through it, and its mill outlived the timber wars by learning to cut small logs.

**Mirth.** The Santiam Highway came through in September 1939, and the Canyon Fir mill ran from 1946 to 1994. Its own history is in *Mirth, Encyclopedia Entry*.

**Five Fords.** A ranger seat and a timber-sale town through the boom, the site of the 1969 Causeway hearings, and a town whose small mills were gone by the early 1990s.

**Lake Varuna.** Lodges on the shore by the 1940s, and money after that.

**Sump Creek Settlement.** The dam was begun in 1949, and the old town went under in 1953.

**Cadence.** A ranch-and-timber crossroads on the dry side until the ski bowl opened at the pass in 1938 and the Santiam Highway came over in 1939. It grew into a city after the war on the pass, the lakes and the people who wanted both. The details belong to its own entry, when it's written.

**The Wisteria Wilds.** A railroad town from 1912 and a division point with helper engines after 1926. Then it was a mill town, with Marlow Pacific's mill running from 1948 to 1987. The Fawn Creek blockade sat above East Wild in 1995 and 1996, and the Nation's casino opened in 1996.

**Nela Junction.** North Fork Lumber's company town from 1923 until the mill closed in 1984.

**Veilwood.** North Fork Lumber's logging railroad reached its valley in the 1920s, and Nela Road runs beside the old grade.

**Fold Creek.** The Nation's century is in *The Singee, A History*.

**Isalune's Hush.** Too far and too steep for the boom, and inside the co-stewardship overlay after 1977. It was never cut.

**Shale and Faust.** Pine camps on logging spurs that ran out of the big Bend mills. On the real ground, Bend's Shevlin-Hixon mill closed in 1950 and Brooks-Scanlon in 1992, and the Rim's camps emptied with them.

## A Timeline

## Writing This Century

**Date people by their work.** A man born in 1905 rode the cars to Camp Four. His son set chokers off a truck road. His granddaughter worked the Marlow Pacific green chain until 1987, then retrained at the college in Cadence. Each of them talks about the woods differently, and *Neptune English* has the words for each decade.

**The Rivers families are people first.** A Rivers character might be a welder's granddaughter who's a nurse in Cadence, a Galilee deacon who sells insurance, or a kid who's sick of hearing about the renewal at every family reunion and goes to the Galilee forum anyway. The renewal and the unpaid study are the background of their lives, not the plot of anyone's book. The Gulf South came west with them, and *Neptune English* says how it sounds.

**The timber wars weren't a morality play.** The logger at the hearing and the kid at the barricade both loved the woods, and both were partly right. A book set in 1991 can be on either side of the gate, and it should let the other side be human.

**What people believed isn't what's true.** The argument about awake and asleep is a public fact about the region. Whether a given grove is awake, and what that means for a given town, is still decided by the Master Reference's standing rules and by each book's own local strangeness, never by this history.
