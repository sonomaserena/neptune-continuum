# The Greater Neptune Region: Road Atlas

*How every town connects to every other, to the rest of Oregon, and to the world*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*

## How to Use This Atlas

The map is one of only three things that cross between stories, and this is the map's arithmetic. Every mileage, milepost and drive time a story needs comes from here, and every story agrees with it. If a character in 1962 and a character in 2026 both drive from Loki's Weir to Five Fords, the road is the same length for both of them, even if the car, the radio and the reason for the trip are not.

The times are ordinary driving: a dry road, daylight, no logging truck to follow up the grade. Night, fog, snow, smoke, a tractor at Amity Gate or a funeral procession on the Causeway all slow things down, and a story can add that time freely. What a story shouldn't do is shorten it.

All of it sits on real Oregon. The real highways keep their real lengths, and the region's own roads were laid on real ground at believable lengths, so a reader with a road map open beside the book will find the distances honest.

There are almost always two ways to make a long drive here. One stays inside the region on the Causeway and the forest roads. The other cuts through a town the region doesn't govern, usually Eugene, and is usually faster. Locals know both and have opinions about each. The tables say which one they're showing.

## The Neptune Causeway, Mile by Mile

The Causeway runs **189 miles** from the brass plate at the harbor to the sign at the eastern edge, a minute short of Sisters. Mileposts count up from the sea. They're the region's real address system: people say "past 12, before the Weir," and they mean the last mile of the harbor flats before the river narrows. They say "up at 118," and they mean the east end of Sweetbriar, where the canal crosses under the road.

In winter almost nothing changes north of the South Divide. Santiam Pass stays open under chains, and the Causeway is plowed end to end. Two places do change. With the Rim Line Road closed, Shale and Faust lose their road to each other and to Cadence's back door. Shale's winter is all Bend: Cadence is 50 minutes through Sisters. Faust's winter is all South Divide: East Wild is 50 minutes over Willamette Pass, and Cadence is more than two hours around through Bend. With Saltlick Grade closed, the South keeps its summer times, because the Grade was never the fast way out anyway.

## The Two Ways East: The Causeway and the Old Grade

The Causeway was built in 1969 so that the region's one signed road would never pass through a city the region doesn't govern. That's why it makes its long dogleg north from Loki's Weir, up Lake Creek, over Low Pass and across the fields to Amity Gate, instead of running straight up the river to Eugene. It's also why it's slow.

The Old Grade is the pre-1969 river highway, the real OR 126. It's still there, unsigned, running from Loki's Weir up the river to Eugene. From the coast to anywhere east of the valley, it's faster by about half an hour.

| **Drive** | **By the Causeway** | **By the Old Grade, through Eugene** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The city of Neptune to Cadence | 3 hours 45 minutes, 181 miles | 3 hours 15 minutes, 155 miles |
| Loki's Weir to Five Fords | 2 hours 20 minutes, 114 miles | 1 hour 45 minutes, 88 miles |
| The city of Neptune to Sweetbriar | 2 hours 20 minutes, 112 miles | 1 hour 45 minutes, 86 miles |

So coast people take the Old Grade, and know it floods. When it goes under, which it did in 1964 and does a little most winters, the coast's only road east is the Causeway, and nobody complains about the dogleg that week. Freight, Authority vehicles, school buses on away trips and a certain kind of stubborn grandfather take the Causeway on principle. A coast kid who drives to Cadence by the Causeway on purpose is making a point, and everyone in the car knows what it is.

The South has its own version of the argument. Saltlick Grade, the summer road from the Wilds over to Five Fords, takes 2 hours 5 minutes. Going around through Eugene and Springfield takes 1 hour 50 minutes. The Grade is never the fast way. It's the way that stays inside. Southerners who take it in July are choosing the region over fifteen minutes. When it snows shut in November, they drive through Eugene and grumble, and the grumbling is about Eugene, not the miles.

## Getting In from the Outside World

### By road

The region has five doors to the rest of the country.

**I-5 at Mile 76.** The front door. North, Portland is under two hours and Seattle about four and a half. South, the California line is a little over three hours. It's the only interstate that touches the region, and most Oregonians know the region only as this exit sign.

**US 101 along the coast.** North from the city of Neptune, past Ichabod's Eye and Wrack, Newport is 50 miles and an hour. South, Reedsport is 21 miles and Coos Bay 48.

**US 20 east past the sign at Mile 189.** Sisters is a minute on, and Bend is 35 minutes from Cadence. From Bend, US 97 runs north to the Columbia and south to California, and US 20 keeps going east across the high desert toward Idaho.

**OR 22 north from Santiam Junction.** It passes Sump Creek and comes down the North Santiam to Salem, 50 miles past the reservoir.

**OR 58 through the South Divide.** It runs from I-5 south of Eugene up the Middle Fork through the Wilds, over Willamette Pass and down to US 97 on the dry side.

### By air

No major airport sits inside the region. The two that serve it sit just past the line on either side, which suits the region's habit of being near things without being them.

**Eugene Airport**, on Mahlon Sweet Field, is the region's main airport in everything but name. It sits just outside the line north of Eugene. From Amity Gate it's 20 minutes, by Airport Road off the Causeway at Mile 66. It's an hour from the Wilds, and an hour and a half from the city of Neptune by the Old Grade.

**Redmond Airport**, Roberts Field, serves the dry side: 35 minutes from Cadence through Sisters, and a little over an hour from Shale by way of Bend.

**Portland International** is the long-haul airport for overseas flights and anything Eugene doesn't have. It's 1 hour 50 minutes from Amity Gate, 2 hours from Sump Creek by OR 22 through Salem, about 3 hours from Cadence and 3 hours 20 minutes from the city of Neptune.

**Neptune Municipal Airport** is the city's own field, on the dune flats a mile north of Mile 2, on the ground of Florence's small municipal airport. A city of 143,000 with a deep-water port keeps a regional field. Commuter turboprops fly to Portland and Seattle a few times a day. Everyone in Neptune knows the summer joke: July and August are the worst months to fly out, because the fog that rolls in off the water closes the field more summer mornings than winter ones.

The Wilds also keeps a state airstrip at East Wild, on the ground of Oakridge's real one, for fire crews, bush pilots and the occasional surgeon flying in a heart.

### By rail

**No passenger train stops inside the region.** The nearest stations sit just outside it. Albany is 25 minutes up I-5 from Amity Gate and Eugene is 20 minutes down it, and both take the Cascades trains to Portland and Seattle as well as the Coast Starlight.

The Coast Starlight does more than pass nearby. Southbound out of Eugene, it climbs the Middle Fork right through the South Divide: past West Wild, through Nela Junction, past East Wild and over Willamette Pass. It stops at none of them. Its next stop is Chemult, on the dry side, where a connecting bus runs north to Bend. Every evening the whole South hears the train go through, and nobody in the South can get on it. Nela Junction's depot still has its platform. People stand on it to watch the train pass, the way you'd watch a parade you aren't in.

**The Bay line** is the freight railroad on real Coos Bay Rail Line ground. It comes out of the outside valley at west Eugene and follows the river through Loki's Weir. It crosses the Neptune on the swing bridge at Mile 4 and runs on south down the coast. Its best customer is the city of Neptune's deep-water port. It mostly runs at night, so in the river towns the train is a sound more than a sight: a horn at the Lake Creek Turn at two in the morning, and the clank of the swing bridge closing.

**The Cascade line** is the Union Pacific main line through the South, on the same track the Coast Starlight uses. Freights run day and night. The Wilds was a rail town before it was anything else, and Nela Junction still has the depot bones and the boarding houses to prove it.

## Forest Roads and Their Numbers

Rangers say numbers and locals say names. A ranger says "Forty-one's gated at the Box Canyon guard station," and a Southerner says "the Grade's shut," and they mean the same thing.

Neptune National Forest numbers its roads the way the real Forest Service does. Each main forest road gets a two-digit number, and each spur off it gets a four-digit number that begins with its parent's two: 4410 branches off the Divide Road, 44, and 4415 branches off 4410. The two-digit numbers run in blocks by ranger district, so a forest road's number tells a ranger which district plows it, closes it and gets blamed for it.

| **District** | **Numbers** | **The named roads** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coast | 10–19 | Forest Road 14, the Camp Nine road |
| Five Fords / Rainbelt | 20–29 | The canal service roads above Sweetbriar and the burn-recovery roads around Five Fords |
| High Country | 30–39 | Forest Road 31, Sump Creek Road |
| South Divide | 40–49 | Forest Road 41, Saltlick Grade. Forest Road 42, Nela Road. Forest Road 44, the Divide Road. Forest Road 45, the Windigo road |
| Rim Watch | 50–59 | Forest Road 51, the Rim Line Road |

Some roads have no forest number at all. Old Coast Road is a county road. Fold Creek Road is a Nation road: the Nurven Nation built it, maintains it and plows it, and the only sign at its foot is the Nation's. The roads into the restricted-disclosure parcels appear on no public map. On the Authority's forest map, a numbered spur simply ends in blank green, and that's all the map is allowed to say.

## What Closes, and When

**Old Coast Road** closes in king tides and big swell, usually twice a winter, and the whole Fetch takes the day off to watch. US 101 above it stays open.

**The Old Grade and Loki's Weir** go under in big floods. In the Christmas Flood of 1964 both local roads at the Weir were underwater. Most winters, the low stretches of the Old Grade flood at least once.

**Saltlick Grade** is gated from the first heavy snow until late spring.

**The Rim Line Road** closes with the first real snow, which cuts Shale and Faust apart until spring.

**Forest Road 45**, over Windigo Pass between Isalune's Hush and Faust, is summer only.

**The Divide Road** above Fold Creek is plowed by the county when the county can, and the Hush is cut off for a week or two most winters.

**Santiam Pass** stays open under chains nearly every winter. The great exception was 1993, the Nine-Week Winter, when the pass shut for nine weeks and Cadence was cut off from the west side.

**Smoke and fire** close stretches of the Causeway in the smoke months. In September 2020 the Labor Day Fire closed it through the Rainbelt for weeks. A story set in late summer can close any road the fire would plausibly reach.

## Hooking In a New Town

Every new town needs four facts about its road, written once and then fixed.

**Where its road leaves the map.** That's a Causeway milepost, or a road already in this atlas: "off the Causeway at Mile 97," or "up Forest Road 4410 from the Divide Road."

**What kind of road it is.** A state highway, a county road, a numbered forest road, or a Nation road. A forest road gets a number from its district's block.

**How far it is to the nearest hub.** The drive time to the nearest of the four hubs (the city of Neptune, Amity Gate, Cadence or East Wild), and to the I-5 door at Mile 76.

**What happens to the road in winter.** Open all year, closed by snow, or closed by floods or tides.

For example, here's how that would read for an imagined mill hamlet up a Rainbelt creek, which is not on the map: "Off the Causeway at Mile 124, six miles up Forest Road 2204, gravel. Twenty minutes to Five Fords, an hour and a half to Cadence. Open all year, washes out in March." That's the whole entry. The town's population comes out of the unassigned reserve, as the Master Reference explains, and the four facts go into this atlas.

## The Road Model

Every distance and time in this atlas comes from one road model, kept in this repository as `tools/road_model.py`. It holds the Causeway's mileposts and every road segment with its length and ordinary speed. When a new town gets its four facts, its road goes into the model, and the drive-time table is regenerated from it, so new numbers never contradict old ones. Real highway lengths come from the real roads. The region's own roads were laid on real ground at believable lengths.
