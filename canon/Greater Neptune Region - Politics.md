# The Greater Neptune Region: Politics

*Who votes for what, at which courthouse, and what the region has been arguing about since the rail camps*

Version 1.1 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*, *The Greater Neptune Region, The Twentieth Century*, *The Singee, A History*, *The University of Greater Neptune*, and *The Greater Neptune Region, Neptune and Cadence*

## How to Use This File

Every book set in the region has a ballot in it somewhere, even if nobody opens the envelope. There's a levy for the sheriff, a recall in the Wilds, a chair race for the Authority that nobody follows until something is on fire. This file says who governs each town, which races carry a party label and which don't, how the Authority's board and the Nurven Nation's council are elected, how each part of the region has leaned in each decade, and which fights actually split kitchens and church basements.

The house rule is that politics has dates. The region's books run from the 1930s to now, and no town here has a permanent party. A mill town that voted with its union in 1956 may have voted the other way in 1996 and may be arguing with itself in 2026. Every leaning below belongs to its decade. A book takes the politics of its own year.

Everything here is public register. Ballots, budgets and lawsuits are the most public things there are. Nothing in this file touches the deep register, and no fight described here has a secret reason underneath it that a book can reveal.

## The Ballot in Your Hand

Oregon votes by mail. The state's voters made it the only way to vote in 1998, by 69 percent to 31, and there are no polling places. The ballot comes in the mailbox about two weeks before an election, and it goes back by mail or into a county drop box. In the region the drop boxes stand outside libraries, city halls, the Authority's counters and, in Camp Nine, beside the fire hall, and on the last evening before an election there's a line of cars at each one with their headlights on.

A ballot in the region carries two kinds of race. Partisan races print a party beside each name. Nonpartisan races print none.

| **Race** | **Party labels** | **When it's on the ballot** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| President, Congress, governor, legislature | Yes | November of even years, with primaries in May |
| County commissioners | Depends on the county | May and November of even years |
| Sheriff, district attorney, judges | No | May and November of even years |
| Mayor and city council | No | November of even years, in most of the region's cities |
| School boards and special districts | No | The third Tuesday in May of odd years |
| The Authority's board, the Nine | No | The third Tuesday in May of odd years |
| The Nurven Nation's council | The Nation's own election | Every September, enrolled members only |

In a nonpartisan county or city race, a candidate who wins a majority in May takes the seat outright, and if nobody does, the top two meet again in November. Nonpartisan doesn't mean nobody knows. Bend's city council, just outside the line, is nonpartisan on the ballot and came out of the 2024 election entirely Democrat-backed in practice, and in the region's own towns the parties endorse, the yard signs travel in pairs and everyone at the café knows whose people a candidate's people are. What the missing label changes is the conversation. A council candidate in the region gets asked about the culvert on Third Street, not about Washington.

## The Counties Underneath

The region is a 1977 line drawn over the top of eight real Oregon counties, and the counties never went anywhere. They run the courts, the jails, the sheriff's patrols outside city limits, the property rolls, the county roads and the elections themselves. The Authority runs the shared business of the land. The counties run the business of people.

| **Settlement** | **County** | **Courthouse** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neptune, Loki's Weir, Ichabod's Eye, Camp Nine | Lane | Eugene |
| Wrack | Lincoln | Newport |
| Amity Gate, Honey Hills | Linn | Albany |
| Sweetbriar, Five Fords | Lane | Eugene |
| Mirth, Lake Varuna | Linn | Albany |
| Sump Creek Settlement | Marion | Salem |
| Cadence | Jefferson and Deschutes | Madras and Bend |
| Shale | Deschutes | Bend |
| Faust | Klamath, with its north edge in Deschutes | Klamath Falls |
| The Wilds, Nela Junction, Veilwood, Fold Creek | Lane | Eugene |
| Isalune's Hush | Lane, on the Douglas line | Eugene |

The first thing a writer should notice is the right-hand column. Every courthouse is outside the line. Nobody in the region can serve on a jury, contest a will or pay a delinquent tax bill without leaving the region to do it, and the Authority exists partly because of that. In 1977 the legislature drew a line around a place that had eight county seats and no center.

**Lane County** holds most of the region's people. Its home-rule charter, adopted by its voters in 1962, gives it five full-time commissioners elected by district on a nonpartisan ballot. In this world the city of Neptune is Lane's second city, after Eugene, big enough to fill one commissioner district and spill into a second, and Neptune and Eugene have fought over the county's budget since the charter was new. The coast says the county spends its money where the courthouse is. Eugene says the coast votes against every levy and then wants deputies. Both are right often enough to keep it going.

**Linn County** has three commissioners, and they run as party nominees. Of the ten county offices on a Linn ballot, the commission is the only partisan one, which makes it the one race on Mirth's or Honey Hills' local ballot that carries a party label. Amity Gate, Honey Hills, Mirth and Lake Varuna all answer to a courthouse in Albany.

**Lincoln County** elects its commissioners without party labels. The county line between Lane and Lincoln crosses The Fetch between Wrack and the Eye, where nobody can see it, so the lighthouse answers to Eugene and the drift board answers to Newport, and a sheriff's deputy called to the Old Coast Road has to know which side of it the call came from.

**Deschutes County** voted in 2022 to take party labels off its commission, and in 2024 it voted, 64 percent to 36, to grow the commission from three seats to five. Shale votes in Deschutes. Cadence votes in two counties at once, because the city fills the Suttle Lake basin, on the Jefferson side of the line, and the Black Butte flats, on the Deschutes side. A Cadence voter on the west side of town gets a Jefferson County ballot and her neighbor across the street may get a Deschutes one, and the city has two county commissions to lobby for every road it shares.

**Faust** sits on the line between Klamath and Deschutes, the way real Davis Lake does. Most of the hamlet is in Klamath, and its courthouse is in Klamath Falls, a long way south. **Sump Creek** votes in Marion County, with Salem's water drawn from the river below it, which matters more every year. **Isalune's Hush** sits on the divide where Lane County ends and Douglas begins, and the Hush's mailboxes are all on the Lane side.

The unincorporated places have no city government at all. Camp Nine and Nela Junction are run, as far as anyone runs them, by Lane County's commissioners, Lane County's road department and whichever deputy is on the overnight shift. Both have a community council that meets in the fire hall, has no legal power and is the most argued-over body in either place.

## Who Runs the Towns

Oregon's city charters, town after town, require the same thing: city offices are elected without party labels. Every incorporated town in the region elects a mayor and a council on the November ballot of even years, with no party beside anyone's name.

**The city of Neptune** has a mayor elected citywide and six councilors elected by ward, which is why its council votes run four to three. Council politics in Neptune split along older lines than the parties: the port and the builders against the neighborhoods, the university against the parking, and, since 1992, the question of the Rivers. Every council candidate goes to the forum at Galilee Missionary Baptist Church and is asked whether they've read the 1998 study. The six wards are Harbor, North Bank, Rivers, Center, Dune Side and Upstream; their streets, neighborhoods and civic pressures are detailed in *The Greater Neptune Region, Neptune and Cadence*.

**Cadence** elects a mayor and six councilors at large. Its politics are the politics of a city that's growing faster than its water: housing, the aquifer, short-term rentals and whether the next subdivision goes north toward the Jefferson side or east toward Sisters. Cadence's council races draw party lines in everything but name, the way Bend's do.

**The Wisteria Wilds** is one city on paper. Its charter gives each half four council seats, and the council picks the mayor from among its members, by custom alternating between East Wild and West Wild every two years. Petitions to dissolve the city into two have reached the ballot twice, in 1979 and 2011, and failed both times. Why the halves vote differently is the Wilds' business, and the Wilds doesn't explain it to outsiders.

**Mirth** has a mayor and a five-member council elected at large, and contracts its policing from the Linn County Sheriff. Its own detail lives in the novel's repository.

The smaller towns run on the same pattern with fewer people. In Honey Hills, every candidate for anything is asked where they stand on aerial spraying in the hills above the hives, the way every Neptune candidate is asked about the study. In Lake Varuna, half the shore owners are registered to vote somewhere else, which means the year-round town, the lodge cooks and the ranger's family and the woman who runs the store, decides every levy for houses whose owners can't vote on it.

## The Authority's Board: the Nine

### The Governor's Eight, 1977 to 1987

The 1977 charter gave the Bioregional Authority an appointed board. The governor named one member for each of the region's seven sub-regions, the state Senate confirmed them, and the Nurven Nation's council named an eighth. People called it the Governor's Eight. It met in the city of Neptune, and from 1980 it met in the new headquarters on the cleared half of the Rivers.

An appointed board was easy to hate in the 1980s. The bust came in 1979, the mills started closing, and the board that set the burn bans, the fishing seasons and the timber-sale schedule inside the co-stewardship overlay answered to no one the region had voted for. The Rim had a seat of its own, one member for a few thousand ranchers, the same as one member for the city of Neptune and its whole coast, and the coast resented that. But the people who hated the board most were in the South, where the Nation's council had voted down every old-growth sale put in front of it, and on the Rim, where any board that met on the coast was a board from somewhere else.

### Erase the Line

In 1984 a committee from East Wild and Shale called Erase the Line began gathering signatures to abolish the Authority. The legislature, which had chartered the Authority and didn't want to watch it dissolve, answered in 1985 with a bill of its own and sent it to the region's voters. It abolished the appointed board and created an elected one in the same stroke. Its ballot title began with the word *Abolishes*.

The Erase the Line people campaigned for it, believing they were voting the Authority out. The coast and the Waist voted for it because they wanted to elect the board. It passed on the May 1986 ballot, 58 percent to 42. The Authority survived, and the board that ran it would answer to the voters from then on. Erase the Line's founders still say the region was tricked. In the Wilds the joke is that they voted to get rid of the Authority and got to vote for it instead.

That isn't invention for invention's sake. It's how Portland got its regional government. In May 1978 the Portland area's voters passed a measure that abolished the area's council of governments and created Metro, which since January 1979 has been the only directly elected regional government in the United States. Nobody knows how many of the people who voted to abolish the old agency expected the stronger one they got. The region's 1986 vote follows the same pattern, eight years later.

### How the Nine are elected

Since July 1987 the Authority's board has had nine seats, and everyone calls it the Nine.

Seven members are elected from zones of equal population, redrawn after every census, because the Supreme Court held in 1970 that an elected board has to give every voter's ballot the same weight. That rule cost the Rim its own seat, and the Rim hasn't forgiven it. One member, the chair, is elected by the whole region. The ninth seat belongs to the Nurven Nation and is filled by the Nation's council.

| **Zone** | **What it holds** |
| --- | --- |
| Harbor | The city of Neptune's older half: the port, the Rivers, the civic district and the Ways |
| Bay | The rest of the city, Loki's Weir, The Fetch from the Eye to Wrack, Camp Nine and the Coast Range |
| Waist | Amity Gate, Honey Hills and the valley floor |
| Santiam | Mirth, Sump Creek, Lake Varuna and the foothills between them |
| McKenzie | Sweetbriar, Five Fords and the canyon road between |
| Crest | Cadence, Shale and Faust |
| South | The Wilds, Nela Junction, Veilwood, the Hush and the Fold |

The zones have personalities. The Santiam is the zone with no middle. Its towns sit in three different canyons, a candidate has to drive over the top or out through the valley to get from one to another, and the Mirth vote and the Sump vote almost never go the same way. The Crest is a Cadence seat in practice, and Shale and Faust know it. Faust votes in the Crest and spends its winters in the South, cut off from Shale when the Rim Line Road closes, and its people say they're represented by a city they can't drive to.

The elections are nonpartisan. They fall on Oregon's regular date for special districts, the third Tuesday in May of odd-numbered years, and members take office on the first of July. Terms are four years and staggered, so half the zones are on each odd-year ballot, and the chair is elected in the same years as the even-numbered zones. Turnout in odd-year May elections is low everywhere in Oregon, and the region is no exception. A Nine race is invisible until the year something burns, floods or gets sold, and then it's the only race anyone talks about.

The chair's race is the region's only contest on a ballot that every voter inside the line shares, and it's run along the Causeway. A serious chair candidate drives Mile 0 to the eastern sign more than once, shakes hands at the Gate's freight yards and the Wilds' casino buffet and the Shale feed store, and does a debate on Neptune Public Radio in late April that's better attended by callers than by voters. The first elected chair, in 1987, was Hollis Marr, a millwright laid off when Marlow Pacific closed its East Wild mill that year, who had signed Erase the Line's petition and ran on the slogan "Then I'll run it." He served two terms.

### The Nation's seat

The Nation's seat is the oldest seat on the board. It came with the 1977 charter and survived the 1986 vote unchanged. The Nation's council names its member, who serves at the council's pleasure and votes on the board's ordinary business like anyone else: budgets, burn bans, the corridor desk, the tags.

There's one thing the Nation's member doesn't vote on. Inside the co-stewardship overlay, the Nation's consent belongs to its council, not to one member of nine. Since 1987 the Nation's member has stepped out of the room for every overlay vote by standing rule, and the board takes the council's answer by letter. A board majority can't vote around that letter, and the Nine has never tried. When people in the Wilds say "the Nation voted it down," they mean the council at the Fold, not the member in the ninth chair.

The seat was challenged once, in 1989, by a Shale rancher who argued that a member named by another government had no place on an elected board. The state's appeals court upheld the seat on the reasoning that it represents a government that is party to the charter, not a block of voters. Inside the Nation, the seat has its own critics. Some members have argued since the 1980s that a sovereign shouldn't sit at the Authority's table as one vote of nine. The council has kept the seat every time the question has come up, on the reasoning that it's better to hear the arguments than to read about them.

## The Nation's Own Elections

The Nurven Nation governs itself under a constitution its members ratified in 1975, two years after recognition. The interim council that argued over the Nation's legal name in 1973, and settled it by two votes, wrote the draft.

The council has seven members, elected at large for staggered three-year terms, so two or three seats are on the ballot every September. That's a pattern several Oregon tribal governments use, with small councils, short staggered terms and an election every year. Every enrolled member eighteen or older can vote, wherever they live, and most of the Nation's voters live off the Reserve: in the city of Neptune, the Wilds, Cadence and Portland. Ballots go out by mail in August. They're counted at the council house at the Fold on the last Saturday in September, with the doors open and folding chairs set out, and the count draws more people than the vote does. Since 2024 a student from the language nest's first class reads the totals out in Singee before the election clerk reads them in English. The numbers are the hard part, because Singee counts in fives and tens.

After each election the council chooses its own chair from among its seven. The General Council, every enrolled adult, meets at the Fold in spring and fall, and can send a question to the whole membership by petition. Turnout runs about a third of eligible members in an ordinary year and higher when the casino's money is on the ballot.

The Nation's fights are a government's fights.

**Who lives where.** About 640 members live on the Reserve, out of about 3,142. For thirty years the Fold has argued that a council elected mostly by members in Portland and Neptune doesn't know what the roads crew needs in February. In 2003 the membership voted that at least four of the seven seats must be held by members living inside the 1977 line. A proposal to reserve seats for residents of the Reserve itself has failed twice.

**The December check.** The Nation's casino and hotel opened in the Wilds in 1996, on trust land beside OR 58, under the federal law of 1988 that lets a tribe run casino gaming under a compact negotiated with the state. Oregon signed its first such compact in 1992, with the Cow Creek Band of Umpqua, and the Nation's came three years later. In 1997 the General Council voted on what to do with the money. Two thirds of the net goes into a trust for language, housing, health care and college. One third is paid to every enrolled member once a year, in December, and people call it the December check. Every few years a petition to raise the check reaches the ballot, usually from members off the Reserve, and the language nest's parents campaign against it. The check is enough for Christmas and a set of tires, in a good year.

**Enrollment.** The constitution enrolls anyone descended from someone on the Nation's 1973 base roll who isn't enrolled in another nation. The second half is the hard part. The removals of 1856 and the boarding-school years carried Singee families onto other nations' rolls under other names, and a person can belong to only one. Since 2011 a member of another nation who can show Singee descent has had a year to give up the other enrollment and join, and every family that has faced that choice has a story about it. The Nation doesn't discuss individual enrollments with outsiders, and the story treats that as a decision.

**The forestry company and the overlay.** The Nation's forestry company bids on Authority thinning and restoration contracts, and in the early 2000s members asked how the same government could consent to a sale in the overlay and bid on the work. Since 2005 the company hasn't bid on work inside the overlay, and council members with ties to it recuse themselves from consent votes.

## How Each Part of the Region Has Leaned

Oregon as a whole voted Republican for president in almost every election from statehood to 1984, except for 1868, 1912, Roosevelt's four elections and 1964. Since 1988 it has voted Democratic every time. Underneath that, the state has always been a quarrel between its cities and its timber and ranch country, and the quarrel has changed sides more than once. The region follows the same lines, decade by decade. What follows describes the region's sub-regions as their own people would, and every leaning is dated.

### The city of Neptune

In the 1930s Neptune was a small Republican port with a lumber mill and a cannery slough, run by its merchants. The Bay Yard changed it in four years. The yard was a closed union shop and brought thousands of workers from the Gulf South and the Midwest, white and Black, and the city voted for Roosevelt in 1944 and kept voting with the unions afterward. Galilee registered voters from its first year, and in a city where the realty board kept Black families inside Harbor Homes, the Rivers voted as a block because it had to.

In the 1950s and 1960s Neptune's partisan politics were union and Democratic, and its council politics were downtown's. The same voters who sent Democrats to Salem elected the merchants who ran the 1961 renewal. From the 1970s the university and the Authority added a third city of teachers, planners and scientists, and in the 1990s the timber wars ran straight through the city: mill families in the east-side neighborhoods, the Station's scientists and the university's students on the other side, and both at the same Little League games. Like Lane County as a whole, the city has voted Democratic for president in every election since 1988. Its real arguments happen in council races, where the ward lines matter more than the party lines.

### The coast and The Fetch

Loki's Weir, Ichabod's Eye and Wrack were fishing, lighthouse and storm-watch places, Republican and suspicious of Salem through the middle of the century, with a union streak where the canneries were. From the 1970s retirees came, from Portland, then California, and the coast became a swing coast. Lincoln County, where Wrack votes, has voted Democratic for president in every election since 1988, and Loki's Weir has stayed closer to even. The coast's present-day politics are about who gets to live on it. The fight over vacation rentals that came to a head in 2021 was the biggest local election Wrack has had in a generation.

### The Coast Range and Camp Nine

Camp Nine was a company camp until 1948 and a gyppo town after, full of small independent logging outfits that hired by the season and never went union. It has voted for whoever promised the most federal timber in every decade it has voted, which made it Democratic in some years of the 1950s, solidly Republican since the 1990s and furious at both parties since the county payments started lapsing. It's the place in the region most likely to have a write-in candidate for sheriff.

### The Valley Waist

The Waist is Grange country. Its farmers voted Republican through the middle of the century and mostly still do, and Linn County's commission has been Republican for a long time. Amity Gate complicates it. The freight yards and the I-5 warehouses brought union drivers and warehouse workers, and since the 2000s the Gate has filled with commuters to Eugene and Corvallis. The Gate votes closer than the farms around it, and Honey Hills votes on spray.

### The McKenzie Rainbelt and the Santiam

Sweetbriar and Five Fords were mill and river towns, and from the founding of the International Woodworkers of America in 1937, the woodworkers' union was the biggest thing in both. Through the 1950s and 1960s they were union Democratic towns that voted for the man who'd keep the timber sales coming. In 1954 Oregon sent its first Democrat to the U.S. Senate in forty years, and the Rainbelt's mill precincts helped. The timber wars broke that. In the early 1990s Sweetbriar flew yellow ribbons from every antenna, and across Oregon's timber country the Democrats lost the mill towns and mostly haven't won them back. Five Fords is the exception that proves it. As the ranger seat, it's full of federal employees, and it has always voted more like a government town than a mill town.

Since the Labor Day Fire in 2020, politics in the Rainbelt has been about rebuilding: insurance, the state's wildfire map, the burn scar and who pays for what the fire left. Mirth, at the Santiam end, has its own record in its own book.

### The High Country

Cadence was a small Republican pass town until UGNC opened in 1966. Through the 1980s and 1990s it grew the way Bend grew, with skiers, retirees, the hospital and the college, and voted Republican with them. Then it kept growing. On the real ground, Deschutes County gave a Democrat for president a majority in 2020 for the first time since 1964, and did it again in 2024. Cadence moved the same way in the same years, and the Rim around it didn't. The Crest zone's chair races since 2017 have been the closest in the region.

Lake Varuna votes with its year-round people, and they're a small, ornery, mixed lot. Sump Creek is a dam town in Marion County that has voted on whatever the Corps of Engineers did last, which in 2026 is the drawdown.

### The South Divide

The Wilds was a railroad town first, with the railroad brotherhoods, and a mill town after the war, with Marlow Pacific's mill and its union local. Through the 1960s East Wild and West Wild were union Democratic towns, and through the 1980s they blamed the Nation and the Authority for the slow sales in the overlay. Marlow closed in 1987. The Fawn Creek blockade came in 1995, and the Wilds voted Republican through the late 1990s and 2000s with the rest of Oregon's timber country.

The 2010s scrambled it. The casino became one of the biggest employers in the Wilds, the Normal's teachers and the mountain-bike shops brought new voters, and retirees came for cheap houses. The Wilds' two halves now regularly vote opposite ways, and a candidate who carries both is rare enough to be talked about. Veilwood and the Hush are too small to swing anything and vote the way their few hundred people feel that year. The Fold votes on the Nation's business first and Oregon's second.

### The Deschutes Rim

Shale and Faust are ranch, timber-edge and wind country, and they've voted Republican in every decade that anyone can remember, with the independent streak of places where the nearest deputy is an hour away. The Rim's grievance is the seat it lost on the Nine in 1987, and its deepest distrust is of the Authority, which it has never seen as anything but a coast institution. Shale has grown with commuters to Bend since the 2000s, and its newer streets vote like Bend's. Faust hasn't, and doesn't.

## The Fights That Really Divide the Region

### The forest's money

Since 1908 a quarter of the money from each national forest has gone back to its counties for schools and roads, and in the region that money was the county. When the cut fell in the 1990s the money fell with it. The same year the owl was listed, 1990, Oregon's voters passed Measure 5, which capped property taxes, by 52 percent to 48. Counties that had lived on timber found themselves with neither timber nor a tax base to fall back on.

In 2000 Congress passed the Secure Rural Schools Act to replace the lost receipts, and ever since then a deputy on the Old Grade at two in the morning has existed at the pleasure of Congress. The program has to be renewed every few years. It lapsed in 2023, the last payments came in early 2024, and Oregon lost more than any other state while it was gone. Congress renewed it in December 2025, through the end of fiscal 2026, and the back payments reached Oregon's counties in April 2026. In Camp Nine, Nela Junction and the Hush, where the county is the only government there is, every lapse means a patrol shift cut or a road left ungraded. Everyone in the region has an opinion about who's to blame, and the opinions follow the 1990s.

### The line on the map

In 1973 Senate Bill 100 made every Oregon city and county plan its land and draw urban growth boundaries, and the fight over it has never stopped. Repeal measures failed in 1976, 57 percent to 43, in 1978, 61 to 39, and in 1982, 55 to 45. In 2004 Measure 37 passed with 61 percent, requiring governments to pay landowners or waive rules that cost them value. In 2007 Measure 49 scaled it back by nearly the same margin.

In the region, the fight is the Authority's too. Amity Gate wants its growth boundary moved for the warehouses. Cadence argues about every subdivision. Lake Varuna's shore rules say how much of the shore money can own, and the shore owners have sued over them three times. On the Rim, ranch families want to split off a house lot for a grandchild and find that both the county and the Authority get a say. To people in the South and on the Rim, the Authority is land-use planning with a regional badge on it.

### Whether the Authority should exist

The 1986 vote didn't settle it. Every decade has had its petition to abolish the Authority or shrink it, and every decade's petition has failed. The argument has the same shape every time. One side says the region's land is one place and has to be run as one. The other side says the region is eight counties' worth of people who never voted to be one place. Both sides drive the Causeway to make their case.

### Water

The water fights are the old ones. The Corps of Engineers drowned the old town under the Sump in 1953, and in 2026 the Corps is set to begin the first deep drawdown of the lake, the lowest it has ever been, to help young Chinook salmon get past the dam. Salem and the canyon towns downstream, whose drinking water comes from the river below it, sued to stop it. A federal judge let the drawdown go ahead in November 2026, Marion County appealed, and the Corps said it could begin within days. The Sump is arguing about the turbidity, the marina's season and whether it wants to see the old town that far out of the water.

Sweetbriar's canals belong to the city of Eugene's public utility, outside the line, whose voters don't live there. The upper canal stopped making power in 2018, when federal regulators ordered it drained, and in January 2023 the utility voted to decommission it and take out its dam, no sooner than 2032. The dam is also the bridge some of Sweetbriar's families drive across, and the lake behind it will go back to river. Sweetbriar's name is prettier than its politics, and this is why.

On the Rim and in Cadence, the water fight is underground: wells, the aquifer and who gets to drill the next one.

### Fire and smoke

The Labor Day Fire of September 2020 burned the Rainbelt along the Causeway, and the same night the fires in the North Santiam canyon came down on the Sump. On the real ground, a jury found in 2023 that a utility had been grossly negligent in causing several of that night's fires, including the Santiam Canyon fire, and by early 2026 the damages juries had awarded survivors passed a billion dollars. In April 2026 the Court of Appeals reversed the liability verdict over a jury instruction, and the state Supreme Court heard the case in November. In the Sump, whether the power should have been cut that night is a political question, and nobody there is neutral on it.

After the fires the legislature ordered a statewide wildfire hazard map in 2021, with stricter building codes and clearing rules for the high-hazard zones. Rural owners called the map wrong, said it was costing them their insurance and fought it until the legislature repealed it in 2025, by unanimous vote in the Senate and 50 to 1 in the House. In the region it split people who had both lost houses to the same fire. Some wanted the map so the next house would survive. Others wanted it gone so they could afford the next house at all. Smoke days are the other fire politics, and in August every school board in the region argues about when to cancel.

### Who gets to live here

The newest fight is the oldest one in a new coat. Wrack, the Lake, Cadence and the Benches above Mirth all have more houses than people who live in them all year. In 2021 Lincoln County's voters passed a measure to phase out vacation rentals in unincorporated residential neighborhoods, 58 percent to 42. The state's land-use board struck it down in August 2022, and the county's own narrower rules were upheld by the Court of Appeals in 2026. The houses on the Old Coast Road south of Wrack's limits are where it bit hardest, and people there can tell you which neighbors signed the petition.

In Cadence the same fight is about rent. In Lake Varuna it's about the shore. Across the region it's the argument between the people who came because the place was beautiful and the people who were already here when it wasn't paying anyone.

### The Nation's consent

Through the 1980s the Nation's council voted down every old-growth sale in the overlay, and timber families in the Wilds blamed the Nation for the slow sales and the closed mills. Some still do. The casino in 1996 made it worse for a while, because it put Nation money in plain sight in the town where the mill had closed. Thirty years on, the casino's payroll includes plenty of children of Marlow Pacific families, and the argument has softened into something people have at Thanksgiving rather than at council meetings. It hasn't gone away. When the Nation withholds consent in the overlay now, it's usually over a road, a land swap or a utility line, and the people on the losing side still say "the Fold" the way their grandparents said it in 1985.

The story treats the Nation's consent as what it is, a government exercising a right written into the charter. A book can have characters who resent it. The book itself doesn't treat it as a mystery.

### The Rivers

The Rivers' fight is with the city of Neptune and, through the land, with the Authority. In 1992 the Red River Street Committee asked for a fund, land and an apology for the renewal of the 1960s. In 1996 the council voted four to three to study it, the study was finished in 1998, and it has sat on a shelf ever since. Every council race since has been asked about it at Galilee, and every Nine race in the Harbor zone has been asked whether the Authority, which bought the cleared land in 1978, owes anything for standing on it. The Authority's answer has always been that it bought vacant land. The Rivers' answer has always been that the land was vacant because the city emptied it.

### Leaving Oregon

Since 2020 a movement to move the Idaho border west has put advisory measures on county ballots across eastern Oregon. Jefferson County voted yes in 2020. Klamath County voted 57 percent to 43 in 2022 to create a board to study it. Douglas County voted no, twice. The movement's map would cut through Jefferson, Deschutes and Klamath, which puts the line through the middle of the Rim, past Faust, and almost through Cadence.

Nobody in the region seriously expects the border to move, since it would take both legislatures and Congress. The measure matters as a way of saying something. In Faust the yes vote was a vote about the Authority, Salem and every regulation that ever came over the pass. In Cadence, where half the city votes in Jefferson County, the vote split the city, and some people there still won't discuss it at dinner.

## Writing Politics

**A ballot dates a story.** A character who voted on the Authority's board in 1987 was voting in its first election. A character who mentions putting a ballot in a box in 1996 means a polling place. After 1998 there aren't any. A Wilds union man in 1965 and a Wilds union man in 1995 may hold the same opinions about timber and vote for different parties.

**Nonpartisan races still have teams.** Council and Nine races carry no label, and everyone knows anyway. Let characters decode them the way real people do, by who's endorsing, whose yard has the sign and who showed up at Galilee.

**The Nine is dull until it isn't.** Most years nobody can name their zone's member. The year of a fire, a drawdown or a land swap, everyone can, and the May election is the only thing callers to *Low Water* want to talk about besides the road.

**Present-day officeholders are left unnamed.** This file names historical figures and leaves today's mayors, commissioners, Nine members and council members open, so each book can fill its own without contradicting another.

**Keep the Nation's elections the Nation's.** Singee characters have opinions about the December check, the four-seat rule and who's running this September, the same way anyone has opinions about their own government. A Singee character doesn't explain the Nation's politics to an outsider, and the Nation's decisions are never clues.

**No fight here hides a supernatural cause.** Water, fire, land and money are reasons enough. A book can set a ghost story against a levy election, but the levy is a levy.

Junior College District, decided in 1970; the International Woodworkers of America, founded in 1937; Richard Neuberger's election in 1954 as Oregon's first Democratic U.S. senator in forty years; Oregon's presidential record and Deschutes County's results in 2020 and 2024; Senate Bill 100 of 1973 and the failed repeal votes of 1976, 1978 and 1982; Measure 5 of 1990; Measures 37 and 49; the Secure Rural Schools Act of 2000, its lapse in 2023 and its renewal in December 2025; the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 and Oregon's first tribal-state compact in 1992; the 2020 Labor Day fires, the 2023 jury verdict over the Santiam Canyon fires, the damages verdicts that followed and the 2026 appeal; the 2021 wildfire hazard map and its repeal in 2025; the Willamette dam drawdowns and the Detroit Lake drawdown fight of 2026; the Leaburg canal's shutdown in 2018 and the 2023 decision to decommission it; Lincoln County's 2021 vacation-rental measure, the 2022 ruling against it and the 2026 appeals ruling; and the Greater Idaho votes in Jefferson, Klamath and Douglas counties.
