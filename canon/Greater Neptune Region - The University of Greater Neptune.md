# The University of Greater Neptune

*Four campuses, a field station on a cold lake, and the Nation's college at the Fold, which stays its own*

Version 1.0 · November 2026

Companion to: *The Greater Neptune Region, Master Reference v3*, *The Greater Neptune Region, The Twentieth Century*, *The Singee, A History* and *Neptune English, How the Region Talks*

## How to Use This File

The University of Greater Neptune is where the region sends its children when it can't afford to send them farther, and where a good many of them stay. It's a public university with four campuses and a field station, all inside the 1977 line. This file gives each one its history, its look, what it's known for and what its students call it, so any book can send a character there, or have a character drop out of one, without inventing the place from scratch.

It also covers the college the Nurven Nation runs at the Fold, which is not part of the university and never has been, and the colleges outside the system that the region's students use anyway.

Everything here is public register. The university keeps no secrets the rest of the region doesn't, and its scientists study the forest the way scientists do. The deep register stays where the Master Reference puts it.

The Oregon Legislature created the University of Greater Neptune in 1979, two years after the region's charter, at the Bioregional Authority's request. It joined three state colleges that already stood inside the line, Neptune State University, Central Cascades State College and Middle Fork State College, under one name and one president, and it opened the Amity Gate campus two years later. For thirty-six years UGN answered to the State Board of Higher Education with every other public university in Oregon. When Oregon dissolved its university system in 2015 and gave its universities their own boards, UGN got one board of trustees for all its campuses. By statute, one trustee is nominated by the Nurven Nation.

## UGN, the City Campus

### The college on the Ways

The Bay Yard launched its last ship in the fall of 1945 and closed in 1946, and the city of Neptune was left with miles of empty shipways and thousands of young men coming home on the GI Bill with nowhere to use it. On the real ground, Portland answered the same problem with the Vanport Extension Center, a temporary school for veterans in a wartime housing project that grew into Portland State University. The city of Neptune did the same thing on its own flats. In the summer of 1946, the Neptune Extension Center opened in the Bay Yard's empty administration building and its mold loft, the great open floor where the yard's loftsmen had chalked out ships' plates full size. It offered two years of college to about three hundred veterans that first summer, and nearly fifteen hundred by the next fall.

It was meant to be temporary, and it refused to close. It became Neptune State College, a four-year school, in 1955, and Neptune State University in 1968. In 1979 it became the University of Greater Neptune and the seat of the system.

The campus is still on the yard. The slipways where Liberty ships slid into the river are the campus green, called the Ways, a long lawn that runs downhill to a seawall, and students sit on it in their first sunbreak in March as if it were a beach. The mold loft is the student union, still called the Loft, with the yard's chalk lines preserved under glass in one corner of the floor. The yard's whirley crane stands at the foot of the Ways, painted navy, and every graduating class climbs it once, illegally.

### The Rivers and the university

The Extension Center admitted Black veterans from its first summer, the way the Vanport school did, and some of the Bay Yard's welders and shipfitters from the Rivers walked across the flats to take classes in the buildings where they had worked. Elsewhere in the country the GI Bill's promise was largely kept from Black veterans, through segregated colleges, counselors who steered them away and banks that refused their loans. In Neptune the classroom door was open and almost every other door wasn't. The realty board's rule kept Black students out of rooming houses near campus, and for twenty years Black students lived in the Rivers and walked.

In the spring of 1968, Black students from the Rivers occupied the president's office for two days and came out with three things: a Black Studies department, an admissions program for Rivers high schools, and a promise that the college would never again build on land taken from the Rivers. The department opened in 1969 and is still there. The promise has been kept, and Rivers families point out that it was the only promise of that decade anyone kept. The Galilee forum for council candidates is held in the Loft every election year.

### What it's known for

UGN is a port's university. Its School of Marine and Coastal Science runs boats out through the Choke on the bar report like everyone else, and its students learn the river mouth by crossing it. The naval architecture program descends directly from the yard's own loftsmen, a few of whom taught there into the 1960s. The School of Nursing feeds every hospital in the region. The School of Law, opened in 1974, is best known for natural resources law and federal Indian law. A good share of the lawyers who argued both sides of the timber wars went there, and so did many of the Nation's attorneys, who have sued the Authority with the training the state paid for.

UGN's Museum of Natural and Cultural History held Singee belongings collected in the 1920s through the 1960s. After Congress passed the repatriation law in 1990, the Return desk at the Fold asked for them back. They went home between 1994 and 2006, box by box, and the museum's Singee gallery is now a room of photographs of the empty cases with the Nation's statement on the wall.

UGN has no veterinary school and no agriculture college. Students who want those drive to the state university in Corvallis, just outside the line.

### Students and teams

About 17,800 students, most from inside the region, many the first in their families to go to college, and a good number commuting from the dune-back neighborhoods and the Weir. The Ospreys play Division I basketball in a gym on the old yard's fitting-out pier, where the bleachers shake when the Bay line goes by at night. The football team is mediocre and loved. Students say "the U" or "UGN," and nobody says the full name except at graduation.

## UGN Cadence

### The college at the crest

After the war Cadence grew from a pass town into a small city, and by the early 1960s it had a hospital, a ski bowl, mills, a growing high school and no college closer than Eugene or Bend. The state opened Central Cascades State College in Cadence in 1966 on the west edge of the lake basin, in pine and fir with the crest behind it. It joined UGN in 1979 as the University of Greater Neptune at Cadence, and nobody has ever called it anything but UGNC.

It's a campus built for weather. The buildings are low and long, with deep eaves that drop snow away from the doors, and the walkways between them are covered. In the Nine-Week Winter of 1993, with the pass shut, UGNC was where Cadence went for heat and news. The dorms took in stranded travelers, and the dining hall fed half the west end.

### What it's known for

UGNC's School of Forestry and Fire is why most of its students come. It teaches forest ecology, silviculture, recreation management and wildland fire, and a large share of its students work fire crews for the Forest Service, the state or private contractors every summer. Fire season runs late, so the School of Forestry and Fire has a standing policy that lets crew members start the fall term late without penalty. The chemistry department has never adopted it, and every crew kid at UGNC has stories about chemistry in October.

Its outdoor recreation and hospitality programs serve the lakes and the ski bowl, and its nursing program trains for the hospital in Cadence. In 1987, when Marlow Pacific's mill in East Wild closed, UGNC ran retraining classes for laid-off mill workers, who drove over Saltlick Grade to take them and, once the Grade snowed shut, over Willamette Pass and around through Bend. Some of them still teach there.

### The pool

The campus natatorium, at the west edge of campus by Mile 178, is the only fifty-meter pool inside the line, so the region's club swim and water polo teams train there in the dark before classes start. On winter mornings the parking lot fills with cars that came over the pass with their wipers going, and the deck smells of chlorine and wet wool.

### Students and teams

About 8,200 students. A lot of them are from the west side and go home on weekends over the pass, which UGNC calls "going wet." The Lookouts play in a small gym called the Tower. The cross-country team trains on the Rim Line Road before it closes for snow.

## UGN Middle Fork: the Normal

### The teachers' college

Oregon trained its teachers at normal schools, from the French école normale, a school for the norms of teaching. On the real ground, the state reopened its normal school at Ashland in 1926 and opened one at La Grande in 1929, and renamed its normal schools colleges of education in 1939. Inside the region, the timber country of the South Divide had one-room schools up every creek and almost nobody to teach in them. The Cascade line had made East Wild reachable in 1926, and in 1931 the state opened Middle Fork Normal School on a bench above the rail yard, in a brick building with the depot whistle coming through the windows.

Its first students were mostly the daughters of railroad and mill families, who rode the train in from the string of towns along the Middle Fork. The Normal sent them to practice-teach in one-room schools up the drainages, the way the Monmouth normal school did on the real ground, and many of them married where they taught and never came back down. The school became Middle Fork College of Education in 1939, Middle Fork State College in 1956, and UGN Middle Fork in 1979. Everyone in the South still calls it the Normal, and the sign at the gate says so in smaller letters under the official name.

### What it's known for

The Normal still trains most of the region's teachers and most of its school principals. A teacher anywhere in the region, asked where she trained, says "the Normal" the way a lawyer says the name of a law school. It's also the South's college, the only campus most East Wild and West Wild families can afford to send a child to without the child moving away, so it has added nursing, business and a rail and heavy-equipment program for the Cascade line and the Nation's forestry contractor.

Since 2008 the Normal has run a Singee teacher certification jointly with Lhunpasi College at the Fold. The Nation designed the language half and teaches it at the Fold, the Normal teaches the rest, and the license says both names. Most of the teachers at the Fold's immersion preschool hold it.

### Students and teams

About 3,400 students. The Engineers, named for the railroad, play basketball in an old brick gym with a running track hung from the balcony, and the whole of East Wild comes to the Wilds Cup game against the Lookouts every February, which the Normal usually loses and celebrates anyway.

## UGN Amity Gate

When the region was chartered in 1977, the Authority needed planners, permit officers, heritage reviewers and land-use lawyers faster than the universities could produce them, and most of the people who wanted those jobs already had jobs and families. UGN opened its Amity Gate campus in 1981 in the former headquarters of a grain-elevator company beside the I-5 interchange, the region's front door, for working adults who couldn't move. Classes run evenings and weekends, and the parking lot is full at seven at night.

It's known for public administration and land-use planning, which is where most of the Authority's staff took their degrees, and for logistics and transportation, for the freight yards and the interstate. It also houses the Honey Hills Pollination Lab, which studies the bee yards on the benches and the valley's seed crops. Beekeepers on the benches send samples and opinions to the lab in roughly equal quantity.

About 2,900 students, most part time. The Gate campus has no teams and no dorms, and its students like it that way.

## Varuna Station

In 1958, Neptune State College opened a field station on the north shore of Lake Varuna, three cabins, a dock and a lab in a converted boathouse, to study the coldest clear lake in the region and the forest standing a hundred feet down in it. The lake allows no motors, so the Station's boats are rowed. Its scientific divers have been working in the drowned forest since the 1960s, dating the trees and mapping them, and they don't sell the T-shirt the dive shops in town do.

In the 1970s the Station's work moved up the hill into the old growth between Lake Varuna and Five Fords. Working with the Forest Service, its ecologists laid out reference stands in uncut forest, permanent plots measured the same way every year, which now hold some of the longest continuous records of old-growth forest anywhere. On the real ground, the same work was done at the H. J. Andrews Experimental Forest near Blue River, established by the Forest Service in 1948, where scientists did some of the first studies of old-growth ecosystems in the 1970s and which became one of the National Science Foundation's original Long-Term Ecological Research sites in 1980. The Station is the region's version, and its plots and data are UGN's.

That made the Station a party to the timber wars. Its scientists testified at the owl hearings, and in timber towns "the Lake people" meant the scientists, not the money on the shore. The Station measures everything about the old growth that can be measured. Its scientists grew up in the region like everyone else, and they know what everyone in the region knows about old growth being awake. It doesn't appear in any of their papers, and it isn't supposed to.

A few dozen graduate students, researchers and summer interns live at the Station at a time. Undergraduates from all four campuses come for a summer field course that most of them describe afterward as the best six weeks of their lives and the coldest.

## Lhunpasi College: The Nation's Own

The Nurven Nation runs its own college at the Fold. It's Lhunpasi College in the Nation's spelling, and Lunpasee College in the clerks', and outsiders who want to be polite learn to say the breathy L. It isn't part of the University of Greater Neptune, and it was never going to be.

Tribal colleges began in 1968, when the Navajo Nation opened the first one, and Congress began funding them under the Tribally Controlled Community College Assistance Act of 1978. The Nation was recognized in 1973 and spent its first decade building a government. Lhunpasi College opened in 1985 in the old agency office at the Fold, with eleven students, two full-time teachers and a classroom heated by a woodstove. When it opened, the state offered to bring it into UGN as a branch campus. The council answered in one sentence, which the college keeps framed in its front office: the Nation will educate its own.

It's small, about 260 students, most of them part time and many of them older than college age: grandmothers finishing degrees, crew bosses taking forestry, records clerks taking archives. It teaches the Singee language at every level, and it's where the language committee's work after 1988 turned into classes. It trains the Nation's teachers, with the Normal, and its foresters, its nurses' aides, its records keepers and its government. Its archives course is taught partly in the dry room of the archive house, and what's restricted there stays restricted, for the students as for anyone. It admits anyone, and it exists first for the Nation.

UGN accepts its credits, and its graduates transfer to the city campus and to the Normal every year. The partnerships run on the Nation's terms, in writing.

## Outside the System

Not every student in the region goes to UGN. The city of Neptune has its own two-year school, Neptune Bay Community College, on the dune-back side of town, which trains welders, fish processors, nurses' aides and sound engineers, and runs the night classes half the harbor has taken at some point. The University of Oregon in Eugene and Oregon State in Corvallis sit just outside the line, and the region sends them students every year, Corvallis especially for agriculture, engineering and veterinary medicine. Families in Mirth and the Santiam end of the Rainbelt use the community college in Albany, which is closer than anything inside the line. And a region with a port and a pass sends its share of children as far away as they can get, some of whom come back.

## Real and Invented

Real, with real dates: the Vanport Extension Center of 1946 and its growth into Portland State, a college in 1955 and a university in 1969; the GI Bill and the ways its benefits were kept from Black veterans; Oregon's normal schools, the Ashland reopening in 1926, La Grande in 1929, and the 1939 renaming to colleges of education; the State Board of Higher Education, which governed Oregon's public universities together from 1929, and Senate Bill 270 of 2013, which began the move to separate boards and led to the system's dissolution in 2015; the first tribal college in 1968 and the Tribally Controlled Community College Assistance Act of 1978; the repatriation law of 1990; the H. J. Andrews Experimental Forest, its 1948 founding, its old-growth research of the 1970s and its 1980 designation as a long-term ecological research site; and the University of Oregon, Oregon State and the community college in Albany, all outside the line.

Invented for the region: the University of Greater Neptune and all its campuses, names, dates, numbers, teams and colors; the Neptune Extension Center and Neptune State; Central Cascades State College; Middle Fork Normal School and its successors; the Amity Gate campus and the Pollination Lab; Varuna Station; the 1968 occupation; the law school; the museum's Singee collection and its return; Lhunpasi College; and Neptune Bay Community College.

## Writing the University

**Use the nicknames.** Nobody says "the University of Greater Neptune at Cadence" out loud. People say UGN, UGNC, the Normal, the Gate campus and the Station, and they say "the Ways" and "the Loft" without explaining them.

**A degree dates a character.** A teacher who trained at Middle Fork State College is over seventy. A forester from the Station's first reference-stand crews is retired and still angry about 1994. A Lhunpasi graduate in her sixties may have taken her first Singee class at a woodstove.

**The campuses aren't equal, and everybody knows it.** The city campus thinks it's the university. UGNC thinks it's the one that matters. The Normal knows it trains everyone's children's teachers and doesn't care what the other two think. The Gate campus is too busy to have an opinion.

**The Nation's college is the Nation's.** Lhunpasi College is a school, with budgets, tenure fights and a parking problem, not a door into anything. Its students are people taking classes. What it chooses not to teach outsiders is a sovereign decision, and the story treats it that way.
